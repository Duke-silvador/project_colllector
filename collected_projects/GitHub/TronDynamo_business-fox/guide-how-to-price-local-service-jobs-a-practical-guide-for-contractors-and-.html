How to Price Local Service Jobs: A Practical Guide for Contractors and Trades

Pricing local service work accurately is the difference between a healthy business and constant cash flow stress. If you undercharge, you work long hours for little return. If you overcharge, you lose bids to competitors.

Here is a practical, step-by-step method to price your jobs so you cover your costs, pay yourself fairly, and make a sustainable profit.

### Step 1: Calculate Your True Operating Costs Most service businesses fail because they only count the materials they buy for a specific job. You must calculate your total overhead costs per month.

Add up everything it takes to keep your doors open: - Vehicle payments, insurance, and fuel - Tools, equipment maintenance, and software subscriptions - Licensing, bonding, and general liability insurance - Marketing, website hosting, and phone bills - Storage unit or shop rent

Divide your total monthly overhead by the number of billable hours you work in a month. This gives you your baseline overhead cost per hour.

### Step 2: Determine Your Desired Wage You are an employee of your own business first. Decide what hourly wage or salary you need to live on. Add that wage to your overhead calculation to find your true break-even point per hour.

### Step 3: Factor in Billable vs. Non-Billable Time You cannot bill a client for every hour you are on the clock. You spend time driving to sites, giving estimates, picking up parts, and doing paperwork.

If you work 40 hours a week, you might only have 25 to 30 billable hours. Divide your total weekly expenses and desired pay by your actual billable hours, not your total working hours. This is your minimum required hourly rate.

### Step 4: Choose Your Pricing Model Depending on your trade, choose one of these standard pricing structures:

1. **Time and Materials:** Best for repair work or jobs where the scope is unknown until you start. Charge your hourly rate plus the cost of materials marked up by a standard percentage (usually 10% to 20%) to cover handling and warranty. 2. **Flat-Rate Pricing:** Best for standard installations or routine maintenance. Estimate how long the job will take, add materials, overhead, and profit, then quote one clear price. Clients like flat rates because there are no surprises.

### Step 5: Add Your Profit Margin Break-even is not a business model; it is survival. Once you have your total cost for a job (labor plus materials plus overhead), add a profit margin of 10% to 20%. This money stays in the business to fund future equipment purchases, training, and slow seasons.

### Putting It All Together Review your past five invoices. Did you make money on all of them, or did some barely cover costs? Adjust your rates upward if your margins are too thin. Pricing is not permanent; review your numbers at least once a year.
