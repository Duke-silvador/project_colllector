README.TXT~/lensing

Einstein's general relativity says that mass curves spacetime, and light follows that curvature. A galaxy or a cluster of galaxies between us and a more distant source works like a lens. Not a very good lens, but a huge one. It can magnify, stretch and multiply what lies behind it, turning a distant galaxy into arcs, rings and crosses.

There are three toys on this page:

Deep fieldDrag a galaxy or a whole cluster across the real Hubble Ultra Deep Field and watch the background galaxies warp. This is strong lensing at cluster scale.
Lens labOne lens, one source. Move the source and watch images appear, merge and vanish as it crosses a caustic. You also get magnifications and time delays.
Time delaysA quasar behind the lens flickers, and each image flickers later than the last. Line up the light curves and you have measured the Hubble constant.

Then there's the theory behind it (with an interactive ray diagram and an Einstein-radius calculator), a gallery of famous lenses you can load into the simulators, and a list of things to try.

Time-delay cosmography: measure H₀ yourself~/lensing/timedelays

A quasar flickers on its own, by a few tenths of a magnitude over weeks to months. Every lensed image shows the same flicker, but each one arrives late by a different amount. The light took paths of different lengths, through different depths of the lens potential. All those delays scale with the time-delay distance DΔt, and DΔt ∝ 1/H0. Theory: how a lens made of gravity works~/lensing/theory.tex

1Light falls, too

In general relativity, light travels along the straightest possible paths (null geodesics) through curved spacetime. Near a mass those paths bend. A light ray passing a compact mass M at impact parameter ξ, far outside the mass, is deflected by

α^=4GMc2ξ=2RSξ(1)

where RS = 2GM/c2 is the Schwarzschild radius. This is a weak-field result, valid when ξ ≫ RS, which is true for every lens on this page. For light grazing the Sun it gives 1.75″. If you treat light as Newtonian particles moving at c, you get exactly half of that, 0.87″. Johann von Soldner did that calculation around 1801, and Einstein got the same half value in 1911, before he finished general relativity (0.84″ and 0.83″ with the solar data of their day). The extra factor of 2 comes from the curvature of space itself. In 1919, Dyson, Eddington and Davidson measured stars near the eclipsed Sun and found the full value. That result made Einstein famous overnight.

For every lens on this page the deflection is tiny: α is at most a few tens of arcseconds. That allows two very good approximations. First, the thin-lens approximation: all the bending happens in a single plane, because the lens is far thinner than the distances involved. Second, all angles are small, so sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ.

2The lens equation

Put the observer, the lens and the source on a line, at angular-diameter distances Dd (to the lens, the "deflector"), Ds (to the source) and Dds (from lens to source). Note that in an expanding universe Dds ≠ Ds − Dd. The source sits at true angular position β on the sky. We see it at angle θ instead. Simple geometry gives the lens equation:

𝛃=𝛉−DdsDs𝛂^(Dd𝛉)≡𝛉−𝛂(𝛉)(2)

It maps each image position θ to exactly one source position β. Going the other way, from β back to θ, is what makes lensing interesting: one β can have several solutions θ. Each solution is an image. The simulators on this page do exactly this: for every pixel θ they compute β = θ − α(θ) and look up the colour of the source there. This is called inverse ray-tracing.

3The Einstein radius

If the source sits exactly behind a round lens (β = 0), symmetry turns its image into a ring, the Einstein ring. Its angular radius θE sets the scale of everything in strong lensing. For a point mass:

θE=4GMc2⁢DdsDdDs,θ±=12(β±β2+4θE2)(3)

A point mass always makes two images, one on each side. Galaxies are better described by the singular isothermal sphere (SIS). Its density falls as ρ ∝ r−2, which is what gives galaxies their flat rotation curves. Its strength is set by the stellar velocity dispersion σv. It deflects every ray by the same angle, θE:

θE=4πσv2c2DdsDs,θ±=β±θE(second image only if β<θE)(4)

For any round lens, the mean surface density inside the Einstein radius equals the critical surface density. So the Einstein radius directly measures the projected mass inside it, independent of how that mass is distributed:

M(<θE)=πΣcr(DdθE)2,Σcr=c24πGDsDdDds(5)

This is why lensing is so good at weighing dark matter: it doesn't care whether the mass shines.

4Magnification, critical curves and caustics

It is convenient to write the deflection as the gradient of a lensing potential ψ, α = ∇ψ, which itself obeys a 2D Poisson equation, ∇²ψ = 2κ. Here κ = Σ/Σcr is the convergence: the projected surface density in units of the critical one. How a small patch of the source gets distorted is described by the Jacobian of the lens equation:

𝐀=∂𝛃∂𝛉=(1−κ−γ1−γ2−γ21−κ+γ1),μ=1det𝐀=1(1−κ)2−γ2(6)

Convergence κ magnifies images the same way in every direction. Shear γ stretches them along one direction. Lensing never creates or destroys photons, and it conserves surface brightness. So a magnified image is not brighter per square arcsecond. It is bigger, and the total flux grows by |μ|. Images with μ < 0 are mirror images: they have negative parity.

Where det A = 0, the magnification formally becomes infinite. In the image plane these points form the critical curves (red in the simulators). Mapped back to the source plane, they become the caustics (yellow). Caustics divide the source plane into regions with different numbers of images. Each time a source crosses a caustic, the image count changes by two: a pair of images is born or merges on the critical curve. A tangential critical curve (1 − κ − γ = 0) stretches images tangentially into arcs. A radial one (1 − κ + γ = 0) stretches them radially. Smooth parts of a caustic are folds (two images merge), and the sharp points are cusps (three images merge).

The odd-number theoremA smooth, non-singular lens always makes an odd number of images (1, 3, 5, …, as long as the source is not exactly on a caustic). Cluster lenses can make 7 or more. So why do we usually see 2 or 4? The extra image forms near the dense centre of the lens. It is strongly demagnified, and the lens galaxy's own light usually drowns it out. A perfectly singular isothermal lens destroys that image completely. Its boundary in the source plane is then not a caustic but a cut (dashed orange in the lens lab). Give the lens a core in the lens lab and watch the faint central image appear.

5Time delays and the Hubble constant

Each image is a different path from the source to us, and different paths take different times. The arrival time, relative to an undeflected ray, is the Fermat potential:

t(𝛉)=DΔtc[12(𝛉−𝛃)2−ψ(𝛉)],DΔt=(1+zd)DdDsDds∝1H0(7)

The first term is the geometric delay (bent paths are longer). The second is the Shapiro delay (light passing through a potential well is slowed down). Images form where t is stationary: at minima, saddle points and maxima of the arrival-time surface. That is Fermat's principle, applied to gravity. If the source varies, as a quasar or a supernova does, the variation shows up in each image at a different time. A measured delay gives DΔt, and with it the Hubble constant H0, in a single step and without any distance ladder. Sjur Refsdal proposed exactly this in 1964. You can try it in the time-delay box. The catch is that you need the lens potential ψ. The mass-sheet degeneracy is the main reason that is hard: adding a uniform sheet of mass while rescaling the lens (κ → λκ + 1 − λ, with the unobservable source position rescaled to λβ) changes neither the image positions nor the flux ratios, but it scales every time delay by λ.

6Strong, weak and micro

Strong lensing means multiple images, arcs and rings. It happens only where κ ≳ 1, in the cores of massive galaxies and clusters. It is what the first two simulators show.

Weak lensing happens everywhere else. Each background galaxy is sheared by a percent or so, far below the natural scatter in galaxy shapes. Averaging over thousands of galaxies reveals a coherent pattern, the reduced shear g = γ/(1 − κ). From it we can map the mass of clusters (including the dark matter in the Bullet Cluster) and of the cosmic web itself ("cosmic shear", a key science goal of Euclid and the Rubin Observatory).

Microlensing is strong lensing by stars, with images far too close together to resolve. Because the lens and source move relative to each other, the total magnification changes with time. For a point lens it is:

A(u)=u2+2uu2+4,u(t)=u02+(t−t0tE)2(8)

Here u = β/θE, and tE = θE/μrel is the Einstein-crossing time, typically a few weeks. This symmetric curve is the "Paczyński curve" (Paczyński 1986). A planet orbiting the lens star adds small caustics of its own. A source that crosses or passes near one of them produces a short anomaly. Microlensing is most sensitive to planets near the Einstein ring (a few AU), including cold, low-mass planets that other methods struggle to find.

7What these simulators leave out

  • The deep field puts every background galaxy at the same redshift, the "source z" slider. Real galaxies sit at many redshifts and each one would see a different θE. The few stars with diffraction spikes are in our own Milky Way. In reality they sit in front of any lens and would not be lensed at all.
  • The deep-field image is a finite, pixelated patch of sky. Rays that land outside it come back black.
  • All lenses are thin and use simple analytic profiles: isothermal ellipsoids (optionally cored), point masses and external shear. Real lens models (with GLEE, lenstronomy, Lenstool and friends) use more flexible mass profiles. They also model the PSF, noise and the light of the source.
  • The time-delay box assumes the intrinsic light curve of the quasar is known. Real analyses have to infer it together with the delays. Microlensing by stars in the lens galaxy is mimicked by a small, slow, independent wobble in each image.
  • Light is treated with geometric optics. Wave effects (relevant for gravitational waves, not for starlight) are ignored.
