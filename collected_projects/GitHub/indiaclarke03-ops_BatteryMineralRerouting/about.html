About Paper Trail

Paper Trail tests whether trade and corporate-registry data can detect Chinese battery materials being routed through third-country plants before they reach US battery supply chains. It uses the Sayari knowledge graph through Sayari's MCP server.

Purpose and audience

Since July 2025, US battery makers can lose clean-energy tax credits if too much of a component's cost comes from a prohibited foreign entity. In March 2026 the US International Trade Commission declined to impose duties on Chinese anode material. Together these shift the compliance question from where did the goods ship from? to who owns the plant they came from? Answering that takes ownership data and trade data together.

The site is for sourcing, compliance and policy analysts, and for the classmates and instructor of the course it was built for. In about three minutes a reader should understand one rerouting pattern, how Sayari found it, and how sure we are of each link.

It contributes three things:

  1. A reusable screening playbook: a Sayari MCP call sequence and red-flag rubric that can be rerun on any battery supplier (playbook).
  2. An honest scorecard of the tool, showing where Sayari worked, partly worked or failed (scorecard).
  3. Declared vs. observed: a Passport vs. Paper Trail card that compares battery-passport fields with what trade data shows (Case Files).

How to read it

Every link between two companies has an edge type, shown by both colour and line style, and a confidence badge. Click any receipt such as s017 to see the call that produced it.

The verdict on the home page sorts findings into what Sayari detected (a record shows it), suggested (signals point to it without a record) and couldn't see.

How to read Sayari risk flags

  • *_adjacent: the entity is one hop from a flagged entity, through any kind of relationship. It isn't flagged itself.
  • psa_*: the path runs through a "Possibly the Same As" link, a probable but unconfirmed record match.
  • *_origin_subtier: a listed entity appears in tier 2 or 3 of the supplier network in the past 730 days, based on trade data.
  • We show flags as context and mark risky ones with ⚑. We never present a flag as a finding about the company itself. Definitions come from s003.

Limitations and ethics

  • This is not an accusation tool. We write "pattern consistent with" or "warrants due diligence", never that a company is evading anything.
  • Most third-country processing of Chinese inputs is legal and commercially normal. The US buyers are named only because customs records name them. That says nothing about their compliance.
  • Trade data has gaps: some countries aren't covered, product descriptions are vague, dates lag, and HS 3801.10 also covers non-battery graphite.
  • Trade records can't show which input went into which export, so the Chinese-origin share of any shipment is unknown.
  • Entity resolution can merge or split companies wrongly. Every "possibly the same as" link is labelled Inferred, with the reasoning shown.
  • Whether any company counts as a prohibited foreign entity is a legal test that Treasury hasn't fully defined. We don't make that call.
  • Policy is moving fast. Dates are as of 14 Oct 2026. China's suspension of its Oct 2025 anode controls runs to 10 Nov 2026; a reported extension to Jan 2027 is unverified.
  • AI use. The investigation and the site were built with Claude Code, which ran the Sayari and Tavily calls. India Clarke set the question and the method and reviews the findings.

Course context

Built for GLOB1-GC 2620 Beyond the Brand: Harnessing Emerging Tech in Ethical Sourcing (NYU Center for Global Affairs, Fall 2026). The assignment: demonstrate Sayari MCP on a problem that traces contractual, transactional and inferred relationships, show it in class, then update it based on feedback.

It is a companion to my earlier project, Battery Passport Audit, which mocked up EU battery passports and marked their gaps. A passport is a self-declaration; trade data is an independent check. The gaps are where due diligence should look.
