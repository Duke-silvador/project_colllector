SMALL TESTS, BIG AHA MOMENTS

Science is better hands-on.

Try these low-cost observations at home. Ask a question, make a prediction, and notice what changes. Adult supervision is recommended for children.

10 MINI INVESTIGATIONS

Look closer. Find out why.

Each activity includes a simple setup, clear steps, a prediction to make, and an explanation you can reveal when you're ready.

10hands-on ideas5–20minutes each
✳

Safety first. Use only the listed household items. Ask an adult to help if you are a child. Never taste experiment materials, look directly at the Sun, mix cleaning products, use flames, or experiment with mains electricity.

01 / 10LIGHTMake a pencil look bent

Make a pencil look bent

You need: a clear glass, water and a pencil.

  1. Look at the pencil before adding water.
  2. Place it in the glass and slowly add water.
  3. Look from the side and compare the pencil above and below the water.
02 / 10WATERBuild a water-drop dome

Build a water-drop dome

You need: a coin, water and a dropper or teaspoon.

  1. Put a clean coin on a flat plate.
  2. Add tiny drops one at a time.
  3. Count the drops and watch the water rise into a dome before it spills.
03 / 10SUNLIGHTTrack a changing shadow

Track a changing shadow

You need: chalk or paper, sunlight and a safe fixed object.

  1. In the morning, mark the tip of the object's shadow.
  2. Repeat after one or two hours from the same spot.
  3. Compare the length and direction. Never look directly at the Sun.
04 / 10STATIC ELECTRICITYMake a balloon attract paper

Make a balloon attract paper

You need: a balloon and tiny pieces of dry paper.

  1. Tear paper into tiny pieces and place them on a table.
  2. Rub the balloon on dry hair or a woollen cloth for 10–15 seconds.
  3. Hold it near the paper without touching and observe.
05 / 10COLOURWatch colours travel through paper

Watch colours travel through paper

You need: two clear cups, water, food colouring and paper towels.

  1. Fill one cup halfway with coloured water and leave the other empty.
  2. Fold a paper towel into a strip and put one end in each cup.
  3. Wait 15–30 minutes and watch the water climb and move across.
06 / 10SURFACE TENSIONMake pepper run away from soap

Make pepper run away from soap

You need: a shallow plate, water, ground pepper and a drop of dish soap.

  1. Pour a thin layer of water into the plate and sprinkle pepper on top.
  2. Touch the water's centre with a clean finger and observe.
  3. Put a tiny drop of soap on another finger and touch the centre.
07 / 10ENGINEERINGBuild a stronger paper bridge

Build a stronger paper bridge

You need: two books, one sheet of paper and a few coins.

  1. Place the books a short distance apart and lay flat paper across them.
  2. Add coins one by one until the paper bends; count them.
  3. Fold a fresh sheet like an accordion and repeat with the same spacing.
08 / 10SOUNDSee sound make tiny grains dance

See sound make tiny grains dance

You need: a bowl, plastic wrap, a few grains of rice and a speaker.

  1. Stretch plastic wrap tightly over the bowl; ask an adult to help if needed.
  2. Place a few rice grains on top.
  3. Play a steady sound nearby at a moderate volume and watch for movement. Do not place the speaker in water.
09 / 10DENSITYTest what floats and what sinks

Test what floats and what sinks

You need: a bowl of water and a few safe objects such as a cork, plastic cap and spoon.

  1. Predict which objects will float before testing.
  2. Place each object gently in the water, one at a time.
  3. Dry the objects and group them into floaters and sinkers.
10 / 10COLOUR MIXINGMix colours like a tiny scientist

Mix colours like a tiny scientist

You need: three clear cups, water and food colouring in two colours.

  1. Fill two cups with water and add a different colour to each.
  2. Pour a little from both coloured cups into the third cup.
  3. Predict the result first, then compare the new colour with your prediction.
THE SCIENTIFIC METHOD

Wonder. Predict. Test. Learn.

Keep a tiny notebook: write what you think will happen, what you actually observed, and one new question. A surprising result is not a failure—it is a clue.
