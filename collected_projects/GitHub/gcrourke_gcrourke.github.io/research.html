Hospital Mergers and Labor Market Outcomes: Evidence from U.S. Metropolitan Areas, 2012–2019

When hospitals merge, fewer employers compete for medical workers in a local labor market, which may give the merged system the power to hold down wages. This paper studies how hospital mergers affect employment and average hourly wages for registered nurses, nurse practitioners, and physician assistants across U.S. metropolitan areas. I assemble a new MSA-level panel that links hospital ownership changes from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, hospital concentration measures built from Medicare cost reports, and occupational employment and wage data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Using a staggered difference-in-differences design with event studies, I find little evidence that mergers changed employment in any of the three occupations or wages for registered nurses. Wage estimates for nurse practitioners and physician assistants turn negative in the years after a merger but are imprecise. By extending the analysis beyond nurses to advanced practice providers, the paper adds new evidence on how employer consolidation shapes one of the largest labor markets in the United States.

Resort Town Shortages: Effect of J-1 Visa Restrictions on the Labor Market Outcomes of Natives

In the summer of 2020, numerous changes were made to immigration policy in response to the Covid-19 pandemic. One such change was Proclamation 10052, which extended Proclamation 10014 and suspended entry for numerous visa holders. One particularly affected group was the J-1 Summer Work Travel (SWT) program, where participation went from 108,301 participants in 2019 to 4,952 in 2020. This provides a unique opportunity to estimate the labor market impact of immigration. Using a synthetic control method, I estimate the effects of this sudden immigration shock on employment levels, average weekly wages, and unemployment.
