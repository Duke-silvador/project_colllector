A data center is a building whose whole job is to keep computers running around the clock: fed with power, kept cool, kept secure and connected to the internet.

Every time you stream a show, send a message, search the web or ask an AI chatbot a question, your request travels to a data center. "The cloud" is not in the sky. It is millions of computers called servers, stacked in metal cabinets called racks, inside buildings like these all over the country.

Why put computers in a special building?

  • Power that never stops. Servers need steady electricity. A data center has backup batteries and generators so a storm does not knock your bank offline.
  • Cooling. Computers turn almost all the electricity they use into heat. Packed together, they would overheat in minutes without industrial cooling.
  • Connectivity. Many fiber lines from different carriers meet in one place, so traffic is fast and has backup routes.
  • Security and scale. Guards, cameras and badge access protect the equipment, and running thousands of servers together is far cheaper than each company running its own closet.
Why it's in the news: U.S. data centers used about 4.4% of the nation's electricity in 2023, and a Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study projects 6.7% to 12% by 2028, mostly because of AI. That is why new campuses, power deals and local zoning votes are now front-page stories.
Key takeaways
  • The cloud is physical: servers in racks in buildings.
  • Data centers exist to deliver four things: power, cooling, connectivity and security.
  • Demand is growing fast because of AI.

Data centers are measured by how much power they can deliver to computers, not by floor space. Getting a feel for the units makes every announcement easier to read.

  • Kilowatt (kW): 1,000 watts. A home's average draw is about 1.2 kW. A traditional server rack draws 5 to 15 kW.
  • Megawatt (MW): 1,000 kW. Running all year, 1 MW uses about as much electricity as 800 U.S. homes.
  • Gigawatt (GW): 1,000 MW, roughly the output of one large nuclear reactor. Several U.S. AI campuses now plan for 1 to 5 GW.
  • Megawatt-hour (MWh): energy, not power. 1 MW running for one hour is 1 MWh. Your electric bill counts kWh.

Key takeaways
  • MW measures power (a rate). MWh measures energy (an amount).
  • 1 MW running all year is roughly 800 homes' worth of electricity.
  • A gigawatt campus uses about as much as a mid-sized city.

Read a data center spec sheet and you'll see the building split into "white space" and "gray space". Knowing the difference explains why campuses are so big.

  • White space is the data hall floor where racks stand. It's what tenants lease and what capacity in MW ultimately serves.
  • Gray space is everything that supports it: electrical rooms, UPS and battery rooms, mechanical plant, generator yards, meet-me rooms, loading docks and offices. In modern facilities gray space is often as large as, or larger than, the white space.
  • Critical IT load is the power available to the racks, the number in most announcements. The utility connection is bigger, because cooling and losses come on top.
  • Powered shell vs. turnkey: a developer can deliver a "powered shell" (building, power and fiber) for the tenant to fit out, or a turnkey hall with cooling and electrical gear already installed.

Anatomy of a campus

A campus is several buildings on one site sharing a substation, fiber routes, roads, water connections and security. Each building is usually built in phases, so a "1 GW campus" may open its first 100 to 200 MW years before the last building is finished.

Key takeaways
  • White space holds racks; gray space powers, cools and connects them.
  • Announced MW usually means critical IT load.

Electricity takes a long path from the grid to a chip, and every step is built so that a single failure never turns the servers off.

Follow the electricity

What happens when the grid goes down

  1. 0 seconds: the utility feed drops. The UPS batteries take over instantly, so servers never notice.
  2. About 10 seconds: the diesel or gas generators start and reach full speed.
  3. Seconds later: the automatic transfer switch moves the load onto the generators, and the batteries begin recharging.
  4. When the grid is back: the switch moves the load back and the generators cool down. Sites keep fuel on hand for 24 to 72 hours or more.
Voltage steps down along the way. Transmission lines can run at 138 kV to 500 kV. The campus substation steps that down to medium voltage (around 13 to 35 kV), then transformers bring it to 480 V for the building and finally to the 208 V to 415 V that racks use.
Key takeaways
  • Grid → substation → switchgear → UPS → PDU → rack PDU → server.
  • Batteries cover the seconds; generators cover the hours.
  • Big campuses need their own substation, which is why utility approvals are a major milestone.

The biggest constraint on new data centers in America is not money or land. It is getting enough electricity, fast enough.

How a campus gets power

  • Grid interconnection: the developer applies to the utility and regional grid operator (such as PJM in the Mid-Atlantic, ERCOT in Texas or MISO in the Midwest). Studies can take years, and big loads may need new transmission lines and substations.
  • Behind-the-meter generation: building gas turbines, fuel cells or solar plus batteries on site to start sooner or avoid grid limits. Several Texas and Wyoming projects take this route.
  • Long-term power deals: hyperscalers sign power purchase agreements for new wind, solar, geothermal and nuclear, including restarts of retired reactors and future small modular reactors (SMRs).
  • Flexibility: some campuses agree to cut load during grid emergencies in exchange for a faster connection.

Every watt that goes into a server comes out as heat. Cooling is about moving that heat out of the building as efficiently as possible.

From air to liquid

Air works well up to about 20 to 30 kW per rack. Beyond that, fans cannot move enough air, so dense AI racks bring liquid right to the chips. Direct-to-chip cooling pumps coolant through cold plates mounted on GPUs. Immersion dunks whole servers in a non-conductive fluid. The heat then goes to chillers, cooling towers or dry coolers outside.

Water trade-off: evaporative cooling towers use less electricity but consume water. Closed-loop and dry coolers use little water but more power on hot days. That is why water use shows up in local hearings, especially in the Southwest.
Key takeaways
  • Cold aisle in, hot aisle out, and keep them separate.
  • Above roughly 30 kW per rack, liquid cooling takes over.
  • Cooling design is a trade-off between energy use and water use.
Quick check

Two numbers come up in nearly every sustainability claim and local debate. Here is how to read them.

  • PUE (power usage effectiveness) = total facility power ÷ IT power. A PUE of 1.0 would mean every watt goes to computing. The industry average is around 1.5 to 1.6; the best hyperscale sites report about 1.1.
  • WUE (water usage effectiveness) = liters of water consumed on site ÷ kWh of IT energy. It ranges from near 0 for dry-cooled sites to around 2 L/kWh for evaporative cooling in hot climates.
  • CUE (carbon usage effectiveness) = kg of CO₂ ÷ kWh of IT energy, driven mostly by how clean the local grid is.

Watch out: a low PUE can hide high water use, and the reverse. A site that switches from evaporative cooling to dry coolers may raise its PUE while cutting water consumption to almost nothing.
Key takeaways
  • PUE measures overhead, not total consumption.
  • WUE and PUE often trade off against each other.
  • Ask for both, plus where the power comes from.

Data has to move between servers inside the building and out to the internet. Modern data centers use a spine-leaf design so any server can reach any other in a few hops.

  • Carriers and the meet-me room: fiber from several providers enters through separate routes so a backhoe cannot cut the building off.
  • North-south traffic flows between users and the data center. East-west traffic flows between servers inside it, and it is now the larger share.
  • AI clusters add a separate, very fast "back-end" network (400 to 800 Gb/s per GPU) so thousands of GPUs can work as one machine.
Key takeaways
  • Spine-leaf gives predictable, short paths.
  • Diverse fiber routes are as important as backup power.
  • AI training needs its own ultra-fast GPU network.
Quick check

Getting data in and out fast

  • Interconnection: networks, clouds and enterprises connect to each other inside colocation buildings through cross-connects and internet exchange points. Places like Ashburn, Virginia grew because so many networks already meet there.
  • Latency follows distance: light in fiber travels roughly 200 km per millisecond, so every 100 km adds about 1 ms of round-trip time. That is why inference and trading workloads want to be near users and exchanges, while training can go wherever power is cheap.
  • Diverse routes: at least two physically separate fiber paths enter a well-built site, so a single cut never isolates it.
  • Optics: modern switches connect with pluggable optical transceivers running 400 to 800 Gb/s per port; an AI cluster can need tens of thousands of them.
Key takeaways
  • Interconnection density is why some markets attract more data centers.
  • About 1 ms of round trip per 100 km of fiber.
  • AI clusters multiply the amount of networking per server.

A rack is a standard steel cabinet about 2 feet wide and 7 feet tall. Its height is measured in rack units (U), each 1.75 inches. Most racks are 42U to 48U.

  • Top-of-rack (ToR) switch: connects every server in the rack to the data center network.
  • Servers: CPUs, memory and local drives. A common 1U or 2U server draws 300 to 1,000 W.
  • GPU servers: 4U to 8U boxes with 8 accelerators each, drawing 6 to 12 kW apiece.
  • Rack PDUs: the power strips, usually two (A and B feeds) so either can fail.
  • Blanking panels: cover empty slots so hot air cannot loop back to the front.
Key takeaways
  • Racks are measured in U (1.75 in) and in kW.
  • Dual A/B power feeds are standard.
  • GPU servers pack 10 times the power of a regular server into the same rack.

Data centers are designed around a simple question: what happens when a part fails, or needs maintenance? The answer is spare capacity, described with N notation.

  • N: exactly enough equipment for the load. Any failure means an outage.
  • N+1: one spare unit. One failure is fine.
  • 2N: two complete, independent systems. An entire path can fail.

A data center protects what's inside with layers: every layer someone passes through has its own check, so one mistake never opens the whole site.

Protecting against fire and leaks

  • Very early smoke detection (VESDA) samples the air continuously and can spot overheating gear long before visible smoke.
  • Pre-action sprinklers keep pipes dry until two separate signals confirm a fire, so a damaged pipe can't flood the hall. Some rooms use clean-agent gas instead of water.
  • Leak detection cables run under floors and along liquid-cooling pipes.
Key takeaways
  • Security works in nested layers: site, building, hall, rack.
  • Fire systems are designed to avoid water damage.
  • Every entry is logged.

The monitoring stack

Thousands of sensors report into software that operators watch around the clock. Each system has a specific job:

SystemWatches
BMS (building management)Cooling units, chillers, pumps, airflow, temperature and humidity
EPMS (electrical power monitoring)Switchgear, UPS, generators, breakers and power quality
DCIM (infrastructure management)Assets, rack power, capacity planning and cable records
NOC / SOCPeople on shift 24/7 responding to network, facility and security alarms
  • Environmental limits: many operators follow ASHRAE guidance, which recommends server inlet air of about 18 to 27 °C.
  • Operational technology security: the networks that run cooling and power are kept separate from customer and office networks, because a cyberattack on them could cause a physical outage.
  • Change control: work follows written procedures: methods of procedure (MOPs) for planned work, standard procedures (SOPs) for routine tasks and emergency procedures (EOPs) for failures. Mistakes during maintenance and changes are among the most common causes of outages.
  • Testing: generators are run under load, infrared scans look for hot electrical connections, and failover is rehearsed.
  • Audits: customers ask for third-party reports such as SOC 2 and ISO 27001 before moving in.
Key takeaways
  • BMS, EPMS and DCIM cover cooling, power and capacity.
  • Facility control networks are isolated from everything else.
  • Written procedures prevent most human-caused outages.

A GPU is a chip with thousands of small cores that do math in parallel, which is exactly what training and running AI models needs. Thousands of them are wired together to act as one machine.

Training vs. inference

  • Training builds a model by running huge datasets through tens of thousands of GPUs for weeks. It wants one giant, tightly networked cluster and lots of cheap, steady power, so it can sit far from cities.
  • Inference runs the finished model to answer questions. Each request is small, but there are billions of them, and users notice delay, so inference sits closer to people.
Key takeaways
  • AI hardware is organized in pods of liquid-cooled GPU racks.
  • Two networks: a fast back-end GPU fabric and a front-end for users.
  • Training chases power; inference chases users.

Training and running large AI models changed data center design more in three years than the previous fifteen. The reason is the GPU.

  • Density: a classic rack draws 8 to 15 kW. A rack-scale AI system holding 72 GPUs draws about 120 to 140 kW, roughly ten times more in the same footprint.
  • Liquid cooling becomes mandatory, and buildings need far more piping, pumps and heat rejection.
  • One giant computer: training clusters link tens or hundreds of thousands of GPUs with a dedicated network, so they want to sit close together on one campus with a huge, steady power supply.
  • Training vs. inference: training builds a model and can sit in remote places with cheap power. Inference answers users' questions and benefits from being closer to population centers.

Key takeaways
  • AI racks are about 10 times denser than traditional racks.
  • 100,000 GPUs need on the order of 200 to 250 MW at the meter.
  • Power availability, not land, decides where AI campuses go.

An announcement is the start, not the finish. Here is what happens between a press release and servers running, and how long each part typically takes.

What developers look for in a site

  • Power: hundreds of megawatts available soon, ideally near high-voltage lines.
  • Land: flat, outside flood zones, typically 100 to 1,000+ acres for a campus.
  • Fiber: at least two diverse long-haul routes.
  • Water and climate: water supply for evaporative cooling or a cool climate for free cooling.
  • Incentives and permitting: sales tax exemptions on equipment, property tax abatements, and a predictable local approval process.
Typical project timeline (months from site search)

Ranges vary widely. Power can be the longest pole; fast-track AI builds have compressed construction to under a year.

The money

Building costs commonly run around $10 million or more per megawatt for the shell, power and cooling, before servers. AI hardware can cost several times that again. That is why gigawatt campuses come with headlines quoting tens of billions of dollars.

Reading project news: "announced" usually means land and a plan; "approved" means zoning or incentives passed; "broke ground" means construction began; "energized" or "online" means the first buildings have power. Rack Radar tracks these stages.
Key takeaways
  • Power, land, fiber, water and permits decide where campuses go.
  • Projects take 2 to 5 years end to end, and power often sets the pace.
  • Big dollar figures usually include phases over many years.
