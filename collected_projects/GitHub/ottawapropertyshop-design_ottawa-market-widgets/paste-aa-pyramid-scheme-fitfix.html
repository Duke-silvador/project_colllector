What a Pyramid Scheme Actually Is

A pyramid scheme has three marks — payment to join buys your position, your income comes from recruits' fees rather than customers, and it collapses when recruitment stops. Test any company against all three.

Strip away the insults people throw around online and a pyramid scheme has a precise structure. Participants pay to join. The money they are promised comes from the payments of people recruited after them, not from selling anything to customers outside the scheme. And because each level needs more recruits than the last, the scheme mathematically collapses — which is why pyramid schemes are illegal in Canada under the Competition Act, and why the people at the bottom reliably lose their money.

That definition gives us a fair test. It does not ask whether a company has levels, or whether people can earn money connected to recruiting — many legitimate businesses, from law firm partnerships to franchise systems, have both. It asks where the money comes from, and whether a real customer and a real product sit at the base of it.

Where eXp's Money Actually Comes From

eXp's revenue is commission on home sales to the public. Revenue share is the company sharing its own portion of that transaction revenue with agents who helped build the company — if no agent closes a sale, there is nothing to share.

eXp Realty is a licensed brokerage — in Ottawa, operating as eXp Realty, Brokerage — with over 82,000 agents across 29 countries; in Q2 2026 the reported count was 87,338 agents including approximately 4,900 NextHome agents. Its income is the brokerage share of real estate commissions: in Canada, 20 percent of an agent's commission on an 80/20 split, up to a $16,000 CAD cap per agent per year.

Revenue share comes out of that company portion. When an agent you sponsored closes a transaction, eXp pays you a share of the brokerage revenue that transaction generated. The full tier mechanics are set out in the 7-tier revenue share explainer — this post only needs the one structural fact: the payment is downstream of a home sale to a real buyer and a real seller. Amounts vary and are not guaranteed — see eXp's income disclosure.

  • Your own income as an agent comes from your own sales — clients, listings and closed deals

  • Joining costs are a $199 one-time startup fee and $139 per month — operating fees for a brokerage platform, not payments passed up to other agents

  • No agent is paid because someone joined. Agents are paid, in revenue share, because someone sold a house

  • If every eXp agent stopped recruiting tomorrow, agents would still earn commission on sales; a pyramid scheme, by contrast, pays out nothing the day recruitment stops

But there are seven tiers — isn't that a pyramid shape?

The shape of an organisation chart is not what makes a scheme. Almost every large company is wider at the bottom than the top. What matters is the direction and source of the money. In a pyramid scheme, money flows from new recruits upward and there is no external customer. At eXp, money flows from home buyers and sellers — external customers paying commission on a regulated transaction — into the brokerage, and the company then shares part of its own revenue. Same rough shape, opposite source of funds.

Why the Question Keeps Coming Up

The question is understandable — revenue share is unusual in real estate, it involves sponsoring other agents, and it is sometimes marketed badly by individual agents. Unusual is not the same as illegitimate.

Has eXp been charged with running a pyramid scheme?

No pyramid-scheme charge is part of the public record I am aware of — and if that ever changes, it would change this answer. What exists publicly is debate, competitor criticism and individual lawsuits of the ordinary kind large brokerages attract, which are different things. I would rather give you the structural test above and let you apply it yourself than ask you to take any company's word — including eXp's, or mine.

The Fair Criticisms, Stated Honestly

Revenue share is worth little if you never sponsor anyone, most agents earn most of their money from sales, the cloud model is not for everyone, and any agent who pitches you guaranteed income is misrepresenting the model.

  • Most agents will not build a large revenue share income. It requires attracting agents who actually produce, over years. Treat it as a long-term mechanic, not a salary

  • Your split and cap matter more, sooner. For most agents the 80/20 split and $16,000 CAD cap affect this year's income more than revenue share ever will

  • The cloud model has real trade-offs. No physical office, self-directed days, technology-mediated support — some agents thrive, some genuinely prefer a traditional office

  • Hype exists in the ecosystem. If anyone — at eXp or anywhere else — promises you income for recruiting alone, walk away from that person. That pitch describes something eXp's actual structure does not pay for

Agents who join naming my senior partner as your sponsor and me as your co-sponsor are set up on a Free Lead Generation program — leads 100% yours, no split taken on them — precisely because production comes first in this business. The One-Question Test to Take Away

Ask of any brokerage opportunity — including this one — "if nobody new joined for a year, would the people here still get paid?" If yes, there is a real business underneath.

Apply the test anywhere. A scheme fails it by definition. A working brokerage passes it trivially: houses would still sell, commissions would still be earned, and at eXp revenue share would still be paid on those transactions out of company revenue — smaller growth, same structure. That single question will protect you from genuine schemes far better than any argument about any one company, and I am comfortable having eXp judged by it.
