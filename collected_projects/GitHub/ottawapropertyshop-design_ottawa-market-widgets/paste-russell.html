Russell at a Glance

Why do buyers choose a village 40 minutes southeast of downtown over a closer suburb? Space, stock and services — in that order. Russell is a police village (established 1898) inside the Township of Russell (incorporated January 1, 1857), one of the two villages of a fast-growing township in the United Counties of Prescott and Russell. The 2021 census counted 6,135 people in the village and 19,598 across the township, up 18.6% in five years, with a median household income of $123,000. The village core is fully serviced — municipal water and sewer — the schools cover all four Ottawa-area board models in two languages, and the surrounding concessions are working dairy and cash-crop farmland. Downtown is roughly a 40-minute drive in free-flowing traffic, the Highway 417 ramps about 10 minutes. It is a bedroom village with a farm-township memory — and a fair that predates Confederation’s second decade.

Living in Russell — the Village Core

Daily life in Russell runs on Concession Street. The commercial core sits along Craig Street and Concession Street — the Foodland grocery on Craig Street, convenience and fuel on Concession, professional offices in between — and the village’s institutions string along the same spine: the library, the arena, the fairgrounds, the curling club, the museum. Mill Street and Castor Street carry the older residential blocks down toward the river; First Avenue holds the Catholic parish; North and South Russell Roads run out to the schools and the subdivisions. It is a walkable village in the ways that matter — school, rink, grocery and church inside a small grid — wrapped in a township you drive. Living in Russell Ontario means choosing that mix on purpose: a village small enough to stay itself, on the edge of a city big enough to need it.

A Short History — Duncanville to the Village of Today

Settlers began arriving around 1825 — mostly Irish, Scottish and Dutch families — with the first settlement east of the present village near Loucks Cemetery, a place called Luxembourg. The village itself was first called Duncanville, after John Duncan, a cabinet- and coffin-maker; settlement is dated to 1845, the post office took the name Russell in 1848, and the community became the police village of Russell in 1898. The name honours Peter Russell, administrator of Upper Canada from 1796 to 1799 — and one modern line belongs in any honest telling: on May 2, 2022, Township Council removed Peter Russell as the namesake, and the name now honours all people named Russell. The Castor River was the first highway — deep enough in the settlement era to float logs down to the Nation River, then the St. Lawrence, and on to Québec City.

Industry followed the river: sawmills, a woollen mill, and brickyards working the local shale — Russell was noted for its shale brick, and Ottawa’s Civic Hospital was built of it. The railway made the village: the Ottawa and New York Railway arrived in 1898, leased to the New York Central, and with its station Russell became the commercial and livestock-sales centre for the township and beyond. The Great Fire of June 6, 1915 is the village’s defining memory — it started in Murray’s Tinsmith Shop, destroyed 25 buildings, and was fought with help from the Ottawa Fire Department, which arrived by that same railway; the Land Registry Office lost its roof, but records reaching back to 1852 were saved, and the building still stands. Passenger trains ended in 1954 and the last train ran on February 14, 1957 — the rail bed is today’s New York Central Recreational Trail, and the village the railway built kept its shape long after the trains left.

The Russell Fair & Agricultural Roots

The fair is the village’s signature, and it is older than the village itself. The Russell Agricultural Society began as an offshoot of the district society in 1845, and the first Russell Fair was chartered in 1858; the society assembled its own grounds in stages from 1885 — the fairgrounds beside today’s arena and curling club on Concession Street — and the modern fair runs every mid-August. Around the village, the concessions are working farmland: dairy herds and cash crops are the township’s main industry, and the fair is where that economy shows itself once a year — livestock, field crops, home crafts and the midway, run by the same society for more than a century and a half.

Homes & Neighbourhoods

Russell’s housing stock reads like its history. The core is a designated Heritage Conservation District (plan adopted 2021): Edwardian Classicism from the 1890s to the 1930s, built in the local red brick — houses like the McCaffrey House on Concession Street — on the village’s original grid. Around it sit the 1980s-to-2000s subdivisions that housed the first Ottawa commuter wave, and at the edges, farm properties on the concessions. The current growth edge is Russell Trails, on the village’s north side: Tartan Homes and Corvinelli Homes building singles and bungalows on 50-foot lots, semis and townhomes, on sale since 2017 — on street names that tell the village’s own story, York Crossing and Station Trail for the railway, Settlement Lane and Duncanville Street for the first families. Lot sizes are the quiet headline: a single-agent analysis of OREB/MLS data put the typical village lot near 0.14 acres against roughly 0.74 acres for rural township properties — the village buys you services, the concessions buy you land.

Servicing — Read Before You Buy

Every Russell purchase starts with one question: where do the water and the wastewater come from? Inside the village, the answer is the township’s pipes — Russell’s homes are on municipal water and municipal sewer, one of two independent wastewater systems the township runs (the other serves Embrun). On the concessions, the answer is a private well and a private septic system — and the township’s 2024 Water and Wastewater Master Plan counts roughly 300 households across Russell and Embrun that are not on municipal water at all, with no township wastewater planned for Marionville or the Highway 417 Industrial Park.

That split is normal, workable, and non-negotiable homework. In the village: confirm both services at the lot line. On a rural lot: a well is a quantity-and-quality question — flow rate, potability, treatment equipment, records — and a septic system is an age-and-capacity question, what was installed, when, and whether it suits the household you are bringing. The village’s water tower stores 2,300 cubic metres for the serviced core (its build date is undocumented — anyone who gives you one is guessing). Get the answers in writing, before closing, not after.

Getting to Ottawa — and the Honest Transit Picture

Russell is a car village, and this guide will not pretend otherwise. Highway 417 is the spine: roughly 10 minutes north to the Boundary Road or Rockdale Road exits, then west into Ottawa — downtown in about 40 minutes in free-flowing traffic, up to an hour at rush hour; Ottawa’s built-up edge is barely 2 kilometres west of the village, which is why the township grew 18.6% in five years. There is no commuter bus: the rural partner routes that once linked Embrun, Russell and Ottawa appear discontinued, and there is no OC Transpo service in Russell. Most residents work in Ottawa — it is a true bedroom community — and they drive. If your life needs a bus, Russell is the wrong village, and any guide that hints otherwise is selling you something.

Schools — Four Boards in One Village

Schools are the organising spine for most Russell families, and Russell holds a card few villages can match: five schools, four boards, two languages, one village. The standing warning applies — catchments are set by your exact home address and program, so confirm any specific property in the board’s school locator before you buy.

  • Russell Public School (UCDSB, JK–Grade 6) — 14 Mill Street. Part of the village fabric since 1907, with a 50/50 French Immersion program alongside its English program — the immersion entry point for village families.

  • Russell High School (UCDSB, Grades 7–12) — 982 North Russell Road. Opened in 2004 as the Upper Canada board’s first 7–12 school, home of the Timberwolves, drawing students from Russell, Embrun, Casselman and Limoges, with graduation rates the school reports consistently above 90%.

  • St. Mother Teresa Catholic School (CDSBEO, Kindergarten–Grade 6) — 1035 Concession Street. The village’s English Catholic elementary, with French Immersion from Kindergarten and a six-classroom addition funded in 2023.

  • St. Thomas Aquinas Catholic High School (CDSBEO, Grades 7–12) — 1211 South Russell Road. Opened in 2004, the township’s English Catholic secondary — Embrun’s Catholic families’ school as well as Russell’s — with Core French and French Immersion.

  • École élémentaire catholique Saint-Joseph (CSDCEO, French-language) — 1008 North Russell Road. The French Catholic elementary in the village — full French-language schooling without leaving Russell.

  • French public board (CEPEO) — the French public elementary serving the township, École élémentaire publique De la Rivière Castor, is in Embrun; state that plainly if French public schooling is your deciding factor, and confirm your address in the CEPEO locator.

Parish & Community Life

The village’s Catholic parish is Our Lady of the Miraculous Medal, at 17 First Avenue — the parish church for Russell’s Catholic families (St. Thomas Aquinas, often confused with it, is the high school). Russell United Church — formally St. Andrew’s & St. Paul’s — stands at 38 Mill Street: the red-brick church was built in 1887 as St. Andrew’s Presbyterian, and the two congregations united in 1925 when the United Church of Canada was formed. St. Mary’s Anglican Church serves the village from 139 Castor Street. The township’s memory lives two doors down Concession Street: the Keith M. Boyd Museum, at 1150 Concession Street, occupies the former church built in 1856 — the township’s oldest public building, an Anglican church, then a Baptist church until 1978, moved to its present site in 1988 and reopened as the museum in 1989 — named for Keith M. Boyd, the local historian and collector who founded it, and run by the Russell Historical Society.

The Township of Russell Public Library’s Russell Branch sits in the Jean Paul St Pierre Building at 1053 Concession Street — named for Jean-Paul St-Pierre, a long-serving councillor and police-village trustee who served one term as mayor (2010–2014) and died in office in 2014. Service clubs run deep here: the Lions Club (chartered 1947), the Kin Club, the Legion and the Curling Club all anchor the calendar. One practical line for newcomers: the township’s municipal office and its ServiceOntario counter are in Embrun, at 717 Notre-Dame Street — Russell village shares one administration with its sister village, and that is where the paperwork lives.

Recreation & the Castor River

The village’s rink is the Dr. Frank Kinnaird Arena at 1084 Concession Street, beside the fairgrounds and the Russell Curling Club; Russell High School carries the all-weather running track; and the Russell Township Sports Dome at 150 Sportsplex Street sits between the two villages. The next chapter is under construction between Russell and Embrun: a $104.3 million recreation complex — three rinks, an indoor pool, a community hall — with occupancy targeted for December 2026 for the arenas and hall and February 2027 for the pool, on a timeline the township itself says has slipped. Outdoors, the W.E. Burton Conservation Area — named for W.E. “Ernie” Burton, a past chairman of South Nation Conservation — runs about 3 kilometres of trails along the Castor River, with an overlook of the Russell dam (the original dam dates to 1916; the present weir to 1967). The river that floated the first settlers’ logs is now the village’s walking trail — and the old New York Central rail bed carries the recreational trail 7 kilometres east to Embrun.

Russell vs Embrun — One Township, Two Characters

It is the first question half of township buyers ask, and the honest answer starts with the map: Russell and Embrun are one municipality — one council, one administration, shared high schools (both of them in Russell), a shared sports dome and a shared recreation complex rising between them. The characters differ. Embrun is the larger community — 8,680 people in 2021 against Russell’s 6,135 — majority francophone, settled by French Canadians, with the township’s bigger retail strip. Russell is the smaller, predominantly anglophone village: the heritage core, the fair, the serviced streets, and the higher benchmark of the two (near $677,900 against Embrun’s $624,200 in the same January–September 2026 analysis) — the premium buys the older, tighter, fully serviced village fabric. Francophone daily life points to Embrun; the heritage village points to Russell; most buyers tour both, which is why the sister guide exists: read the Embrun Ontario Homes for Sale Neighbourhood Guide for the other half of this township.

Questions Buyers Ask About Russell

Is Russell, Ontario a good place to live?

For buyers who want a real village — a serviced core, heritage streets, four school boards and a fair that has run since 1858 — within an easy drive of the city, yes. Russell is a police village of about 6,135 people (2021) inside a township of 19,598, with municipal water and sewer in the village, a grocery-anchored main street, and lot sizes Ottawa suburbs rarely match. The trade-offs are honest ones: you will drive to Ottawa for work and big-box shopping, and there is no commuter bus to count on.

How far is Russell from Ottawa, and how long is the commute?

Downtown Ottawa is about 40 kilometres northwest, and Ottawa's city limits sit just over 2 kilometres west of the village. The drive runs up to Highway 417 — roughly 10 minutes to the Boundary Road or Rockdale Road exits — and then into the city. Allow up to an hour to downtown at rush hour, and test your own commute at your own hour before you buy.

Is Russell cheaper than Ottawa?

Yes, on the benchmark evidence available: a single-agent analysis of OREB/MLS HPI data for January to September 2026 put the Village of Russell benchmark near $677,900, against an Ottawa single-family median of $775,000 over the same period. Russell also sits above its sister village — Embrun's benchmark in the same analysis was near $624,200 — because Russell's serviced core, heritage stock and smaller supply carry a premium inside the township.

Is Russell French or English speaking?

Both, with an English tilt in the village. Russell village is predominantly anglophone — township-wide, about 61% of residents speak English most often at home and about 36% French (2021) — and every municipal service runs bilingually. The township's other village, Embrun, is majority francophone, so the pair gives buyers a genuine choice of daily language inside one municipality.

What schools are in Russell?

Four boards in one village: Russell Public School (UCDSB, JK to Grade 6, with a 50/50 French Immersion program), Russell High School (UCDSB, Grades 7 to 12, drawing students from Embrun, Casselman and Limoges), St. Mother Teresa Catholic School (CDSBEO, Kindergarten to Grade 6, with French Immersion), St. Thomas Aquinas Catholic High School (CDSBEO, Grades 7 to 12 — the township's English Catholic secondary), and École élémentaire catholique Saint-Joseph (CSDCEO, French-language Catholic elementary). The French public elementary school serving the township, De la Rivière Castor, is in Embrun. Confirm any specific address in the board's school locator before you buy.

Are Russell homes on municipal water and sewer, or well and septic?

It depends on the driveway. Village homes are on municipal water and municipal sewer — the township runs two independent wastewater systems, one for Russell and one for Embrun. Rural properties on the concessions run on private wells and septic systems, and roughly 300 households across Russell and Embrun are not on municipal water at all. On a rural purchase, well and septic homework is part of the file; on a village purchase, confirm the services at the lot line before closing.

Is there public transit from Russell to Ottawa?

No — plan around that honestly. There is no OC Transpo service in Russell, and the historic rural commuter routes that once linked Embrun, Russell and Ottawa appear discontinued, with no current operator verified. Russell is a car community: the commute runs on Highway 417, and buyers who need transit should look inside Ottawa's service area instead.

What new homes are being built in Russell?

The growth edge is Russell Trails, the subdivision on the village's north side built by Tartan Homes and Corvinelli Homes — singles and bungalows on 50-foot lots, semis and townhomes, on sale since 2017, on street names that nod to the railway (York Crossing, Station Trail) and the first settlers (Settlement Lane, Duncanville Street). The township is also building a $104.3 million recreation complex between Russell and Embrun, with occupancy targeted for December 2026 for the arenas and hall and February 2027 for the pool — expect a transformed recreation picture, on a construction timeline that has already slipped once.
