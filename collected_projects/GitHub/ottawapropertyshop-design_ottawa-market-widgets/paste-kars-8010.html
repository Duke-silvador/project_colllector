The Village Story — From Wellington to Kars

Settlement began at the mouth of Stevens Creek around 1820, where the creek meets the Rideau River. Richard Garlick, who arrived in 1821 and settled just south of the present village, is remembered as the area’s earliest settler — a lumberman whose wharves supplied the steamers, and who is credited with the township’s first school and first road. James Lindsay followed in 1829, settling by the river and building the wharf that would anchor the village’s trade for a century.

The village itself was planned by Adam J. Eastman, who built the area’s first steam-powered sawmill on Stevens Creek in 1852 and in 1856 had a surveyor lay out a six-street village on 42 acres. Eastman, a Loyalist at heart, named it Wellington, and named its streets Wellington, Nelson and Waterloo. The name did not last the year: there was already a Wellington in Prince Edward County, and in 1856 the community was renamed Kars, honouring the 1855 defence of Kars — the Ottoman fortress city whose garrison, led by Canadian-born General William Fenwick Williams, held out against a Russian siege during the Crimean War. The post office was Kars from the start, even as land registrations kept the Wellington name.

By the 1860s Kars was a busy post village of a few hundred people, with general stores, the sawmill, a brewery, a tannery, wagon shops, a hotel and two churches. The railway, when it came, passed Kars by — and that accident of geography is why the village still looks the way it does: narrow, tree-lined streets, 19th-century homes, and a tranquil rural atmosphere that has changed little since. Kars became part of the City of Ottawa with amalgamation in 2001, and the City’s own plan for the village asks that it stay slow-growing, walkable and true to its heritage character.

The Rideau River boat launch at Kars — one of two launch points in the village, and the everyday gateway to the Long Reach.

The River Was the Highway

Before there were roads worth the name, there was the river. The Rideau Canal opened in 1832, and the Long Island dam raised the Rideau enough to flood the channel all the way to the foot of Burritts Rapids — creating the Long Reach, roughly 40 kilometres of lock-free water between the Long Island Locks and Burritts Rapids, with Kars sitting mid-reach. No lock of its own was needed: deep, open water ran right past the village wharves.

That geography made Kars. Steamers plied the reach for a century, tying the village to Bytown — today’s Ottawa — and, through the Long Island Locks, to the canal and Kingston beyond. Garlick’s wharves north of James Island loaded cordwood to feed the steamers’ wood-fired boilers. Lindsay’s wharf, built by 1832 just south of the present dock, shipped the township’s wheat, timber, potash, cheese, potatoes, eggs, salt pork, cattle and horses out, and brought tea, molasses, sugar and salt back in. Farmers 15 to 20 kilometres west of the river hauled their produce to the Kars wharf, because the river was simply the cheapest road they had. Eastman’s steam sawmill at the creek mouth turned the surrounding forest into sawn lumber — millions of board feet a year — and the wharves shipped that too.

The village layout still follows the river: the grid Eastman surveyed turns its face to Rideau Valley Drive and the water, the churches and designated houses line the river road, and the dock remains the village’s front porch. When better roads and the railway age arrived, the freight moved ashore and the wharves quieted — the railway’s decision to pass Kars by froze the village at its river-era scale.

The river’s second life is recreational, and it is the reason waterfront homes here command what they do. From a Kars dock, a boater gets the full lock-free reach without locking through — up to Manotick for lunch, down toward Burritts Rapids, with Baxter Conservation Area’s beach and trails just south of the village and the W.A. Taylor Conservation Area launch across the river. The highway became a waterway again; it just carries families now instead of freight.

The Lindsay Chapter — Wharfinger, Postmaster, Neighbour

Photo montage — two photographs combined: the Lindsay Estate sign and tractor at the estate entrance, set in front of the James Lindsay House (built 1850).

The stone farmhouse at 6836 Rideau Valley Drive South was built in 1850 for James Lindsay himself — the Canadian Register of Historic Places lists it under his name, and he was its original owner. Lindsay had immigrated in 1820 and settled by the river in 1829, where he worked as a wharfinger: the man whose wharf and warehouse shipped the township’s wheat and timber out on the steamers. He was also the village postmaster, a justice of the peace and a township councillor — in a river port, the wharfinger was the economy, and Lindsay stood at the centre of it.

The family’s second designated house stands at 6722 Rideau Valley Drive South: a two-and-a-half-storey Queen Anne house built in 1905 by Charles Gavin Lindsay, who had bought the land from his brother James in 1903. Crown-motif keystones on the house mark where the post office once was. Two Lindsay houses, two generations, one street — the clearest short walk through Kars’ river-trade story that any Ottawa village offers.

A Seven-House Heritage Walk

Seven Kars properties are listed on the Canadian Register of Historic Places, all designated under the Ontario Heritage Act — a remarkable count for a village this size. Walk Rideau Valley Drive South and Lord Nelson Street and you walk the whole set:

  • James Lindsay House, 6836 Rideau Valley Drive South (c.1850) — the one-and-a-half-storey stone farmhouse with a centre gable, built for the wharfinger James Lindsay.

  • Martin House, 6732 Rideau Valley Drive South (c.1858) — a gabled house with its former rake factory still attached at the rear; its builder, Stephen Martin, later bought Eastman’s sawmill.

  • St. Louis House, 1579 Washington Street (c.1859) — one of the first houses built in the village, probably by Eastman himself, at the corner of Nelson and Washington.

  • Eastman House, 6727 Lord Nelson Street (1860s) — Eastman’s own one-and-a-half-storey, L-shaped wooden house in the Neo-classical manner, also known as the Thomson House.

  • Bryan House, 6700 Rideau Valley Drive South (1870s) — one of the oldest brick buildings in Kars, with Gothic Revival detailing in contrasting buff brick.

  • Trinity United Church, 6656 Rideau Valley Drive South (1894–95) — a rock-faced stone Gothic Revival church whose facing stone arrived, fittingly, by boat up the Rideau River.

  • Lindsay House, 6722 Rideau Valley Drive South (1905) — the Queen Anne Lindsay house described above, with the post office keystones.

The Eastman House, 6727 Lord Nelson Street — built in the 1860s for Adam J. Eastman, the mill owner who surveyed the village.

Trinity United Church, 6656 Rideau Valley Drive South — rock-faced stone, built 1894–95 to replace an earlier wooden church on the same grounds.

One more landmark belongs on the walk even though it stands outside the Register seven: St. John the Baptist Anglican Church, at 6699 Rideau Valley Drive South. Built in 1850 on land that is now Elmview Cemetery, it was cross-braced in the winter of 1904–05 and moved on skids over Stevens Creek to its present site — a church that quite literally travelled to its congregation. Its 19th-century pine pews and floorboards survive.

Housing in Kars — Village Core, Country Lots, River Frontage

Kars housing comes in three honest bands. The village core is Eastman’s compact six-street grid: detached homes on tree-lined streets, including the designated 1850s-to-1900s stock above, at a scale no new suburb imitates. Around the core, the surrounding rural area offers estate lots, country homes and working farms along the concession roads. And along the river, waterfront homes on the Long Reach — with docks and direct boating access — form the village’s premium tier.

What Kars is not matters as much: it is not a townhouse or condo suburb, and it is not a growth node. Most homes are on a private well and septic system, so those systems are part of the homework on any purchase. Buyers who want new-build choice should look to Manotick and Barrhaven; buyers who want a village that has kept its shape for 170 years start here.

Schools — The Village School and the Bus Rides Beyond

Kars on the Rideau Public School, 6680 Dorack Drive — the in-village JK–8 school, with Early and Middle French Immersion.

Schools are the organising spine for most Kars families, so here is the full picture — and the standing warning that comes with it: catchments are set by your exact home address and program, so confirm any specific property in the board’s school locator before you buy.

  • English public (OCDSB): Kars on the Rideau Public School, 6680 Dorack Drive, right in the village — JK to Grade 8, with Early French Immersion (JK–8) and Middle French Immersion (Grades 4–8). The school also receives Grade 6–8 students from Manotick and North Gower, so its community stretches from Burritts Rapids to the edge of Riverside South. For Grades 9–12, students attend South Carleton High School in Richmond — roughly 18 to 20 km away, well beyond busing thresholds, so eligible students ride the bus.

  • English Catholic (OCSB): St. Leonard School in Manotick (JK–6), whose students come from Manotick, Osgoode, Kars and North Gower, then St. Mark Catholic High School in Manotick (Grades 7–12). Catholic secondary education is open-access in Ontario, but St. Mark is the directed school for this area — confirm transportation for any non-directed choice.

  • French public (CEPEO): École publique Rivière-Rideau in Kemptville offers a single kindergarten-to-Grade-12 pathway — confirm your exact address in the CEPEO locator.

  • French Catholic (CECCE): confirm your exact address in the board locator — French Catholic designations for rural addresses follow their own sector map, and the locator is the only safe source.

  • Busing (OSTA): the Ottawa Student Transportation Authority runs busing for the public and Catholic boards. Eligibility starts at 0.8 km from school for kindergarten, 1.6 km for Grades 1–8, and 3.2 km for Grades 9–12, for students inside their designated boundary. Village-core homes may fall inside the walk zone for the Dorack Drive school; most rural addresses qualify.

  • A boundary note for planners: school boundaries in Ottawa’s south are under active review as a new Riverside South secondary school is planned, and some students may be redirected from Kars on the Rideau and South Carleton in the future. No change date applies to Kars families today — but if schools drive your purchase, ask about it before you sign.

One question every Kars parent asks: why not Osgoode Township High School in Metcalfe, which can feel just as close? Because the public board assigns a designated high school by home address — South Carleton for Kars — and the only route into a non-designated school is a capped cross-boundary transfer, granted for learning-need or exceptional circumstances if space allows, with no school-bus transportation provided. Plan on Richmond, and treat Metcalfe as the exception it is.

Parks, the River and Getting Around

The Kars Community Recreation Association — volunteers who run the RA Hall and grounds at 1604 Old Wellington Street — is the village’s social engine: programs, hall rentals, community events and the Remembrance Day service at the cenotaph. The recreation grounds on Stevens Creek hold the second of the village’s two boat launches, a quiet gravel ramp with ample parking (larger craft should use the river dock and ramp — the creek ramp passes under a low bridge). Baxter Conservation Area, just south of the village on the Rideau, offers five kilometres of trails, a public beach, picnic areas and an accessible boardwalk; W.A. Taylor Conservation Area offers one of the river’s best launch and picnic spots; and Rideau River Provincial Park, about 15 km south, adds family camping on the historic waterway.

Getting around is by car, honestly stated. Manotick is roughly 10 km north (about 10–12 minutes); Barrhaven’s Strandherd retail is roughly 17–20 km; Highway 416 is roughly 8–10 km west; downtown Ottawa is roughly 30–35 km, about 30–40 minutes in free-flowing traffic. There is no regular daily transit — only OC Transpo’s Friday-only Route 305 rural shopper bus to Carlingwood, serving Kars, North Gower and Manotick. Check its current status on octranspo.com before counting on it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Kars like as a place to live?

Kars is a small, slow-growing Rideau River village in Ottawa’s rural south — a compact historic core of a few hundred people, surrounded by farms, estate lots and riverfront homes. Daily life centres on the Kars Community Recreation Association hall and grounds, the river docks and launches, and the village school. It suits buyers who want quiet, space and boating at the door, with Manotick’s shops and services a short drive north.

How many homes are for sale in Kars?

Very few at any one time — Kars is one of the thinnest markets in the Manotick–Kars group, and the live snapshot at the top of this guide always shows the current count. When a well-kept village or waterfront home does list, it draws attention precisely because the shelf is so small. If you are waiting for a specific kind of Kars home, a private search with new-listing alerts is the practical way to catch it.

Why is the river such a big deal in Kars?

Kars sits mid-way along the Long Reach — about 40 kilometres of lock-free Rideau River between the Long Island Locks and Burritts Rapids. A dock in Kars means a long, uninterrupted run by boat in either direction, which is why river frontage carries a premium here and why the village grew up around its wharves in the first place.

Which elementary school serves Kars?

Kars on the Rideau Public School, on Dorack Drive in the village, serves JK to Grade 8, with Early French Immersion from JK to Grade 8 and Middle French Immersion from Grades 4 to 8. Catholic elementary students attend St. Leonard School in Manotick (JK to Grade 6). Always confirm your exact address in the board’s school locator before you buy — catchments are set by address and program.

Where do Kars students go to high school?

Public-board students attend South Carleton High School in Richmond for Grades 9 to 12. Catholic students attend St. Mark Catholic High School in Manotick for Grades 7 to 12. French-public students are served by École publique Rivière-Rideau in Kemptville (kindergarten to Grade 12) — confirm your exact address in the CEPEO locator, and do the same in the CECCE locator for French Catholic options.

Is there bus service or public transit in Kars?

School busing runs through the Ottawa Student Transportation Authority for eligible students: kindergarten students living 0.8 km or more from school, Grades 1 to 8 at 1.6 km or more, and Grades 9 to 12 at 3.2 km or more. For general transit, Kars has no regular daily service — only OC Transpo’s Friday-only rural shopper bus, Route 305, linking the village with North Gower, Manotick and Carlingwood. In practice, Kars is a driving village.

Are Kars homes on town water and sewers?

Most village and rural homes here rely on a private well and septic system rather than municipal water and wastewater services. That is normal for Ottawa’s villages — but it makes the well and septic part of the homework on any specific property: ask for the records, and budget for inspections before you firm up an offer.

How far is Kars from Manotick and downtown Ottawa?

Manotick is roughly 10 km north on Rideau Valley Drive — about a 10-to-12-minute drive. Downtown Ottawa is roughly 30 to 35 km away, typically a 30-to-40-minute drive in free-flowing traffic, and Highway 416 is roughly 8 to 10 km west. Distances here are approximate road distances — check your own commute at your own travel time before you buy.
