The Short Answer

A resale purchase closes on a short list: the balance of the price, land transfer tax, legal fees, and adjustments. A new construction purchase closes on that list plus a second one that resale never sees. The additions are structural, not accidental: the municipality charges the builder levies to service your new street, and the builder passes them to you under the agreement; the utilities charge to connect and enrol your home; the warranty program charges its enrollment fee; and the tax system treats a brand-new home as a taxable supply, so the full price — the home, the lot, and every upgrade you selected — carries tax that a resale home never attracts, softened by rebates that have their own conditions and deadlines. Builders also write adjustment and administration lines into their schedules that a resale agreement never contains. Almost every one of these charges is disclosed somewhere in the paperwork you signed. The failure is arithmetic: nobody totals them early, so they land as a surprise in the final weeks, when the money is least flexible. Total them the week you sign, hold the total in reserve, and closing becomes bookkeeping.

Two Closings, Two Different Bills

The same street can produce two very different closing statements. A resale buyer takes a home the tax system has already processed once: no tax on the price itself, land transfer tax on the total, a lawyer's account, registration charges, and adjustments that mostly move money between buyer and seller — prepaid taxes, a fuel tank, a rental item or two. A new-home buyer takes a home entering the system for the first time, and the first entry is the expensive one. The builder is a business selling a product, so the sale is taxable; the street is new, so the development charges that paid for its sewers, roads and parks flow through the builder's price structure and land, in whole or in part, on the buyer; the services are new, so each utility relationship starts with a connection or account charge rather than a transfer. Understanding that difference — first entry versus transfer — explains nearly every extra line. Nothing on the new-home statement is random, and almost nothing on it is negotiable after the fact. It is negotiable, when it is negotiable at all, before you sign — which is why this guide is organized the way it is.

The Tax on a Brand-New Home

New housing in Ontario carries HST at 13 percent on the purchase price — the price being the home, the lot, and every design studio upgrade you added along the way, because upgrades join the price rather than sitting beside it. Two rebates can return part of that tax. The long-standing new housing rebate refunds a portion of the tax on a home bought as a principal residence, phasing down as the price climbs and disappearing entirely at its ceiling. On top of it, Ontario's temporary relief window rebates the full 13 percent — up to its cap — on new homes under agreements signed between April 1, 2026 and March 31, 2027, where the home will be a primary residence or a long-term rental; it is open to individual buyers generally, with no first-time-buyer test. A separate first-time-buyer rebate pair runs on its own track to 2030 for buyers who meet its tests. Which of these applies to your purchase, and how much of the tax actually comes back, is the largest single variable in your closing total — the mechanics of each layer are worked through in Ontario HST Rebate on New Homes Up to 130000 Dollars and How It Stacks With the Federal Rebate, and the first-time stack in Federal First Time Home Buyer GST Rebate New Home 50000. One mechanic matters for cash planning regardless of which rebate applies: where a rebate is assigned to the builder at closing, the builder credits it against your statement; where it is not, you pay the tax in full on closing day and claim the rebate back afterward — the same money, a very different closing-day balance.

Development Levies and Municipal Charges

Every new street in Ottawa exists because somebody paid for the pipes, the pavement, the parks and the transit capacity that serve it — and that somebody, in the first instance, is the builder, through development charges levied by the city and the school boards. Builders treat those charges as a cost of the lot, and most agreements pass them through to the buyer as a closing adjustment, sometimes as a single development-levies line, sometimes spelled out board by board and charge by charge. The agreement's schedule is where the pass-through lives: some builders cap it, some estimate it, and some leave it open to whatever the municipality assesses. This is one of the few closing lines where the pre-signing questions genuinely change the number — is the levy capped, at what figure, and what happens if the assessment comes in above the cap? Ask before you sign, in those words, and get the answer in the paperwork rather than in the sales office conversation. A capped levy is a budget line; an uncapped one is a weather report.

Hook-Ups, Meters and Enrollment Fees

A resale home already has its services; your lawyer's office transfers the accounts. A new home starts them. Water and sewer connection charges, hydro and gas account setup and meter installation, and the enrollment fee for the home's warranty coverage — the Tarion enrollment that registers your home in the warranty program described in Tarion Warranty Ottawa New Homes 1 2 7 Explained — each arrives as a small line on the statement of adjustments. Individually they are the smallest numbers in this guide. Collectively they are a few thousand dollars that first-time new-home buyers consistently fail to reserve, because no single one of them is large enough to have been mentioned in a sales conversation. Builders typically collect some of them as deposits or prepayments during the build, which produces the opposite surprise: a line you already paid reappearing on the statement. It should appear as a credit where you prepaid it — check that it does.

Adjustments, Administration and the Builder's Schedule

The statement of adjustments is where the builder's agreement schedules become arithmetic. Beyond the levies and hook-ups, look for the lines that exist because the builder wrote them: administration and status fees, charges for changes and extensions, interest provisions where a closing date moves, tree planting and grading deposits or charges where the municipality requires them, and the reconciliation of anything you prepaid — deposits on upgrades, utility deposits, enrollment amounts. Layered over these are the adjustments every purchase carries, new or resale: the land transfer tax itself, which on a new home is calculated on the price that includes your upgrades; your legal fees and disbursements; title insurance; and the prorations that settle prepaid amounts between the parties. Read the adjustment schedule in your agreement before you sign it — not for the individual figures, most of which are estimates, but for the list itself. A charge that is named in the schedule will arrive; a charge that is not named is a conversation. The statement should contain no line you cannot trace back to a schedule, a statute, or a tax rule.

Condo Closings Carry an Extra Layer

A new condominium closes in two acts where a freehold home closes in one. Interim occupancy comes first: you take possession, the building is not yet registered, and your monthly occupancy payment — the phantom rent of interest, estimated taxes and common expenses — runs until registration. Final closing follows when the building registers, and that statement carries lines a freehold buyer never meets: the status certificate review, the corporation's reserve fund contribution that the agreement typically requires from the first buyers, and the deposit reconciliation for amounts held in trust under the protection rules covered in Pre Construction Condos Ottawa Tarion Deposit Protection. Budget for both dates, not one: the occupancy period's monthly burn, then a final statement with its own levy, enrollment and registration lines. Condo buyers who reserve only for a single closing discover the second one in the letter from their lawyer.

The Closing Week Cash Plan

Closing trouble is rarely about the size of the bill; it is about timing. The statement of adjustments typically reaches your lawyer in the final stretch before closing, and the balance due lands by certified funds on closing day — the same week your movers, your deposits' last installment under the schedule decoded in New Construction Deposit Structures Ottawa Decoded, and the first payments on the new home are all competing for the same accounts. The plan that prevents the scramble is set at signing, not at closing: list every closing line you can identify from the agreement's schedules, hold that total aside as a closing reserve separate from your deposit money and your moving money, and confirm with your lawyer — early, in writing — which rebates are being credited on the statement and which you will claim back afterward, because that single answer moves the largest number in the budget. Then ask the builder's office, before the final month, for their current statement template for your model and lot type. Builders issue them constantly; having yours early turns the closing week into a verification exercise instead of a revelation.

Five Checks Before You Sign

  • Find the levy line. In the agreement's schedules, locate the development-levies pass-through and ask the only question that matters about it: is it capped, and at what figure?

  • List every named charge. Work through the adjustment schedule and write down each fee it names — hook-ups, meters, enrollment, administration. Named lines arrive; your reserve should already know their names.

  • Settle the rebate route. Establish which rebates apply to your purchase and whether each is credited by the builder on the statement or claimed by you afterward. The answer changes your closing-day cash, not your entitlement.

  • Remember upgrades are in the price. Your selections at the design studio — the subject of Design Studio Upgrades Ottawa What Is Worth It — join the taxable, transfer-taxed total. Budget closing on the final price, not the base price you first fell for.

  • Condo buyers, reserve twice. If you are buying a condominium, plan for the occupancy period's monthly payments and a second, separate closing statement at registration — two budgets, two dates.

Questions Buyers Ask Me

What closing costs does a new construction home have that a resale home does not?
Four families of charges. Development levies passed through by the builder for the services on your new street. Connection and enrollment charges: utility hook-ups and meters, and the Tarion enrollment that registers your warranty. The tax: HST at 13 percent on a brand-new home, which resale homes do not carry. And the builder's own adjustment lines — administration, status and schedule charges that exist because the builder's agreement wrote them. Everything else on the statement, land transfer tax and legal costs chief among them, a resale buyer pays too.

Do I pay HST on a new home in Ontario?
Yes. A brand-new home is a taxable supply, so the purchase price — the home, the lot and every upgrade you selected — carries HST at 13 percent. Rebates return part of it for many buyers: the long-standing new housing rebate, Ontario's temporary full-rebate window for agreements signed between April 1, 2026 and March 31, 2027, and the first-time-buyer pair for those who meet its tests. Which layers apply to you decides how much of the 13 percent you actually bear.

What are development levies, and can they be capped?
They are the municipal and school-board charges that pay for the infrastructure serving a new community — sewers, roads, parks, transit — charged first to the builder and usually passed through to the buyer under the agreement of purchase and sale. Some builders cap the pass-through; others estimate it or leave it open. The cap question has to be asked and answered before you sign: is the levy capped, and at what figure? After signing, the schedule you agreed to governs.

What is the Tarion enrollment fee doing on my statement?
It is the fee that registers your new home in Ontario's new home warranty program. Builders enroll every new home, and the enrollment charge typically appears in the buyer's closing adjustments. It is a small line with a large subject behind it: the warranty clocks and coverage explained in Tarion Warranty Ottawa New Homes 1 2 7 Explained start from your possession, and the enrollment is what puts your home inside that system.

Are my design studio upgrades taxed too?
Yes, twice over. Upgrades join the purchase price rather than sitting beside it, so the HST calculation and the land transfer tax calculation both run on the total that includes your selections. That is the quiet arithmetic behind the design studio: a five-figure upgrade list adds more than its sticker price to your closing statement. Budget the closing on the final upgraded price, never on the base price.

When will I know my exact closing total?
The exact statement of adjustments is prepared in the final stretch before closing, by the builder and your lawyer. But the composition should never be a surprise: every line traces back to your agreement's schedules, a tax rule, or a charge named in this guide. Ask your lawyer to walk the agreement's adjustment schedule with you at signing, and ask the builder's office for their current statement template for your model — the total you reserve early is an estimate, and a good one beats an exact shock.

What extra closing costs does a new condo have?
A second closing, in effect. Interim occupancy runs from possession until the building registers — a monthly payment covering estimated interest, taxes and common expenses — and the final closing at registration carries its own statement: status certificate review, the reserve fund contribution the agreement requires, and registration charges. Freehold buyers close once; condo buyers should reserve for both dates.

Does the builder credit my rebate at closing, or do I claim it?
Both routes exist, and the difference is cash timing. Where the rebate is assigned to the builder, it appears as a credit on your statement and your closing-day balance drops by that amount. Where it is not assigned, you pay the full tax at closing and claim the rebate back afterward from the tax authority. Confirm which route your purchase takes — with your lawyer, in writing, well before closing week — because it moves the largest single number in your closing budget.

Which closing costs can I negotiate?
Almost none of them after signing — and a few of them before. Levy caps, administration charges and upgrade pricing all live in the agreement and its schedules, which is why the reading happens before the signature, not after. What you can always negotiate is your own preparation: the size of the reserve you hold, the questions you ask, and the professionals — your agent and your lawyer — who read the schedules with you before the numbers become obligations.

Should my agent review the closing schedules with me?
Yes — before you sign, not in the closing week. An agent who works new construction knows which schedules carry the pass-throughs, which builders cap their levies, and which lines deserve a question while a question can still change them. Bring the agreement to that conversation the way you would bring it to your lawyer: early, complete, and with the adjustment schedule flagged. It is the cheapest review in the entire purchase.
