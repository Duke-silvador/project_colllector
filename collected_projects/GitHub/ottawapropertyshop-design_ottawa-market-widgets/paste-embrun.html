From Saint-Jacques-l’Achigan to a Commuter Village — Embrun’s Story

Embrun began in 1845, when French-Canadian families — the Ayotte and Gignac families among the first — travelled from Saint-Jacques-l’Achigan, Québec, to take up lots along the Castor River. The land was rich but demanding: the low ground along the river flooded each spring, and the first decades were spent clearing forest, draining fields and building a parish out of the bush.

The parish came first, as it usually did. A first wooden chapel rose in 1856, and the community took its name the same year — Father François-Xavier Michel named it for Embrun, France (one local history gives 1857). A post office followed on November 1, 1858, and a second church in 1861. The building that still defines the village is the present Église Saint-Jacques, completed in 1891 at 1041 Notre-Dame Street: a stone church 150 feet long whose spire has been the village’s landmark ever since.

Église Saint-Jacques, 1041 Notre-Dame Street — the stone church completed in 1891, still the village’s landmark. (The banner at the top of this guide is a composite image combining this church and my photo.)

Drainage works on the Castor in 1868–70 changed the village’s economy — as the river lands dried, lumber gave way to agriculture, and farms replaced the bush lots. The railway arrived in 1898: the Ottawa and New York line, later part of the New York Central, which the villagers had helped finance with a $10,000 pledge the year before. Trains ran until February 1957, and the old right-of-way is not lost — it is today’s New York Central Recreational Trail, ten paved kilometres linking Embrun and Russell.

The Castor River at Embrun, with the Saint-Jacques steeple over the trees — the river that flooded the first farms and still shapes which lots need a second look.

The modern chapter opened in 1972, when the Highway 417 stretch between Ottawa and Vars put the village about 30 minutes from the city. Embrun spent the next decades becoming what it is now: the population doubled between 1985 and 1995, grew 26.6% between 2001 and 2006 — the fastest rate in the 613 area code — and grew again by 24.6% between 2016 and 2021, to 8,680 people, the fastest growth of any Eastern Ontario community of 1,000 or more. Roughly half the village reports French as a mother tongue. The next landmark is already on the calendar: a new Recreation Complex rising on Sportsplex Street, with occupancy targeted for its arenas and hall in December 2026 and its pool in February 2027.

The Village Today — Areas, Housing and Prices

Embrun’s housing spreads across a historic core and a ring of subdivisions, each with its own feel:

  • Centre-Ville: the original village core along Notre-Dame Street — older detached homes, the church, the Co-op and the commercial strip, on full municipal services.

  • Chantal: a family subdivision on the village’s west side — mostly single-detached homes from the growth decades, popular with buyers who want an established street near the schools.

  • Lapointe: the east-side counterpart — newer singles and semis as the village grows toward Limoges.

  • Embrun South / Bourdeau: the south end, around St-Jacques Road and the water tower — a mix of established homes and newer streets.

  • Maplevale: among the village’s more affluent pockets — larger homes and lots, a step up in finish and price.

  • Forest Park and the Panorama lands: the newest growth, including Panorama, a master-planned community by eQ Homes and SACA Homes planned for more than 1,000 homes at build-out — the clearest sign that Embrun’s growth story is not finished.

Water, Power, Waste — and What Serviced Homes Pay

Servicing is the homework that separates Embrun from Ottawa’s well-and-septic villages, so here it is plainly. Most of the village is on full municipal water and wastewater — but not all of it, and the exceptions matter when you buy.

The water tower on St-Jacques Road, in Embrun South — storage, pressure and fire protection reserve for the municipal water system.

  • Water: the municipal system stores water in the tower on St-Jacques Road, which holds pressure and fire reserve for the village. The tower was repaired in 2026 and is back in full service — a reminder to ask any seller about recent servicing notices. Treated water reaches the Township under its wholesale supply arrangement with the City of Ottawa.

  • The honest caveat: the Township’s 2024 Water and Wastewater Master Plan counts roughly 300 existing households not connected to municipal water and about 600 not connected to municipal wastewater — owners the plan notes have reaffirmed they intend to stay disconnected. A home can sit inside the village and still run on a private well, a septic system, or one of each. Confirm the specific property, every time.

  • Wastewater: the Embrun wastewater treatment plant is rated at 3,865 cubic metres a day. Growth is pushing both Embrun and Russell toward a shared regional plant with The Nation Municipality, now under study, with a site on the Embrun side emerging as the preferred option — and a $9.6 million provincial grant is already funding the Notre-Dame Street watermain and storm rebuild.

  • Electricity: Embrun is served by Coopérative Hydro Embrun Inc. — its own local distribution co-operative, at 821 Notre-Dame Street — not by Hydro One or Hydro Ottawa. Your hydro account is with the co-op.

  • Waste and recycling: curbside garbage, recycling and organics collection are Township services, and homes on private systems carry the septic side themselves — budget for pump-outs, inspections and eventual replacement, as with any rural property.

What serviced owners actually pay: on top of property tax, a connected home pays a separate Township utility bill. Under the Township’s water and wastewater rates by-law, the bill combines a metered Water Service Charge and Sewer Service Charge; the account is created automatically in the owner’s name when your lawyer registers the change of ownership at closing (a $25 administrative fee lands on the first invoice), late balances draw a 1.25% monthly penalty, and anything still unpaid at the end of February is transferred onto the property tax account. E-billing and pre-authorized payments are offered. A well-and-septic home pays none of that — its servicing costs arrive as well and septic maintenance instead. And if you are buying new: development charges are real money here — under the Township’s 2026 schedule, the Township’s own charges alone run to about $36,700 on a new serviced single-detached home, before the Prescott-Russell county and education development charges are added.

Schools — A Full French Path In-Village, English Pathways in Russell

Schools are the organising spine for most Embrun families, so here is the full catchment grid — and the standing warning that comes with it: catchments are set by your exact home address and program, so confirm any specific property in the board’s school locator before you buy.

Pavillon Saint-Jean, 1045 Notre-Dame Street — kindergarten to Grade 2, one of the two buildings of École élémentaire catholique Embrun. (Composite image.)

École élémentaire catholique Embrun (CSDCEO — French-language Catholic) is ONE school in two buildings, teaching more than 616 children from kindergarten to Grade 6, split by age between the pavillons: Pavillon Saint-Jean at 1045 Notre-Dame Street, above, takes the youngest children, kindergarten to Grade 2, and Pavillon La Croisée at 1215 St-Augustin Street, below, takes Grades 3 to 6. The school is a certified ÉcoÉcole with an Action bien-être designation, and before- and after-school daycare runs at the St-Jean/La Croisée site. If your children will learn in French in a Catholic setting, this is the village’s elementary anchor — two campuses, one school community.

Pavillon La Croisée, 1215 St-Augustin Street — the school’s Grades 3 to 6 building, which also houses the Embrun library branch. (Composite image.)

Pavillon La Croisée, on St-Augustin Street, is the school’s Grades 3 to 6 building — and a small landmark of village life in its own right, because the Embrun branch of the public library sits inside it. Between the two pavillons, the elementary years are fully covered in French, in the village, without a bus ride out of town.

École secondaire catholique Embrun, 1276 St-Jacques Road — the village’s French Catholic secondary school. (Composite image.)

École secondaire catholique Embrun (CSDCEO — French-language Catholic, Grades 7–12), at 1276 St-Jacques Road, completes the in-village French Catholic path: more than 688 students, home of the Cyclones. What students can do there goes well past the classroom — the school runs specialised program streams in sports and health, mathematics and sciences, leadership, arts, skilled trades and business, and its students graduate with a certification in bilingualism, leaving with both of Canada’s official languages formally recognised. For a francophone family, this is the payoff of the whole grid: kindergarten to graduation without leaving the village.

École élémentaire publique De la Rivière Castor, 100 Maheu Street.

École élémentaire publique De la Rivière Castor (CEPEO — French-language public), at 100 Maheu Street, is the French public elementary option, kindergarten to Grade 6 — the choice for families who want French-language education in the public (non-denominational) system, right in the village.

  • English public elementary (UCDSB): Cambridge Public School, 2123 Route 500, sits just east of the village in The Nation (the old Cambridge Township) — English instruction with French immersion, kindergarten to Grade 6, and the usual first stop for Embrun’s English-public families.

  • English public secondary (UCDSB): Russell Public School and Russell High School, in Russell village — the English public pathway continues there after the elementary years.

  • French public secondary (CEPEO): the nearest French-language public secondary school is L’Académie de la Seigneurie in Casselman, about 12 km east — the destination for French-public students after De la Rivière Castor.

The English Catholic path (CDSBEO) has no school building inside Embrun itself — it runs through Russell village, and it deserves its own look, because the question every Embrun buyer asks is whether their children can actually go there. They can:

St. Mother Teresa Catholic School, 1035 Concession Street, Russell village.

St. Mother Teresa Catholic School (CDSBEO — English-language Catholic), at 1035 Concession Street in Russell village, is the township’s English Catholic elementary school — kindergarten to Grade 6, with core French and French immersion programs. Can Embrun kids go? Yes: the board lists Embrun among the communities Mother Teresa serves, so an Embrun address sits inside its attendance area, and Embrun students ride the bus into Russell — school busing for the Catholic board is run by STEO, the Student Transportation of Eastern Ontario consortium. Confirm your exact address and bus stop in STEO’s School and Transportation Eligibility tool before you buy.

St. Thomas Aquinas Catholic High School, 1211 South Russell Road, Russell village.

St. Thomas Aquinas Catholic High School (CDSBEO — English-language Catholic, Grades 7–12), at 1211 South Russell Road in Russell village — home of the Ravens — is where the English Catholic path continues after Mother Teresa. Embrun is on its list too: the board names Embrun among the communities St. Thomas Aquinas serves, so Embrun students are inside its attendance area and bused in on STEO routes. Between the two schools, an English Catholic family in Embrun has a complete kindergarten-to-graduation run — it just starts with a bus ride to Russell. As with every catchment in this grid, confirm the specific property in the STEO eligibility tool.

The grid’s story in one line: French-language families — Catholic or public — can school their children from kindergarten to Grade 12 without leaving the village or, for the public path, the immediate area; English-language families build their school run around Russell village. Neither is a flaw. They are simply two different daily geographies, and they should be priced into your choice of street.

Connections — Russell, Ottawa and the Neighbouring Villages

Russell village is Embrun’s other half — the two villages share one township, one history along the Castor River and one municipal government, whose office sits here in Embrun at 717 Notre-Dame Street. Russell holds the English-language schools, the Russell Fair grounds and its own shale-brick heritage streets; most Embrun households shop, school or play in both villages every week. Life in Embrun — Arenas, Halls, the Co-op and the Calendar

The village’s next chapter is being built on Sportsplex Street: the Embrun Recreation Complex at 100 Sportsplex Street — three NHL-size ice pads, a 25-metre six-lane pool with a leisure pool, and a 10,000-square-foot community hall, a $104.3 million investment. Occupancy is targeted for the arenas and hall in December 2026 and the pool in February 2027 — targets, not openings, but close enough that buyers today are pricing them in.

Until then — and long after — the Palais des Sports, the Embrun Arena at 6 Blais Street, is the village’s rink: home of the Embrun Panthers junior hockey club of the EOJHL, public skating and shinny, and the upstairs hall where half the village’s meetings seem to happen. Next door, the Centre communautaire Camille Piché at 8 Blais Street is the main community hall: community suppers, bingos, pancake breakfasts and firefighters’ brunches, kids’ dances, fitness classes, Winter Carnival events — and a rentable hall for the weddings and receptions that anchor a village’s social year.

Blais Street — the Centre communautaire Camille Piché and the Palais des Sports arena, the village’s social headquarters.

The public library’s Embrun branch currently sits inside Pavillon La Croisée — about 1,100 square feet with a French-focused collection — and its future is bigger: a new 5,000-square-foot branch is under construction inside a new Co-op building on Notre-Dame Street, with no opening date announced yet. The Embrun Co-op itself (La Coopérative Agricole d’Embrun, founded in January 1944, some 3,500 members, headquartered at 926 Notre-Dame Street) is a village institution that also runs the grocery store: Co-Op Your Independent Grocer at 753 Notre-Dame Street, with Giant Tiger at 654 Notre-Dame covering the discount end. (There is no Foodland in Embrun — that store belongs to Russell and Greely.)

The calendar runs on village time: the Winter Carnival in late January, built around the arena and the Camille Piché hall; Summerfest — Célébrons Ensemble on Father’s Day weekend; and year-round, the New York Central Recreational Trail, ten paved kilometres on the old rail bed between Embrun and Russell. The village even exports a little fame: singer Véronic DiCaire was born in Embrun in 1976.

Is Embrun a French or English village?

French first, by a wide margin. At the 2021 census, about half of Embrun’s population centre reported French as a mother tongue (4,335 people) against 3,405 reporting English, and the village’s signs, parish and French-language schools reflect that. English-speaking buyers live here comfortably — the English school pathways run through Russell village — but Embrun’s everyday language and institutions are francophone, and that character is a large part of why people choose it.

What should I check about Castor River flooding and Leda clay?

Two honest homework items. First, the low lands along the Castor River flooded each spring in the settlement era, before drainage works opened the valley to farming — floodplain mapping for the river is held by South Nation Conservation, so confirm any specific property’s floodplain status before you buy. Second, the Castor valley sits on Leda clay, a sensitive marine clay common across the Ottawa region that can lose strength when disturbed or dried; on rural lots and heavier builds, a geotechnical review of the specific property is money well spent. Neither point is a reason to avoid Embrun — both are reasons to pull the reports for the exact address.

Are Embrun homes on municipal water and sewers, or well and septic?

Both exist, and the answer is property-specific. Most of the village is on full municipal water and wastewater, but the Township’s 2024 Water and Wastewater Master Plan counts roughly 300 existing households not connected to municipal water and about 600 not connected to municipal wastewater — owners the plan notes have said they intend to stay disconnected. Serviced homes pay a separate metered utility bill on top of property tax; well-and-septic homes carry no utility bill but own their systems’ maintenance. Always confirm which side of that line a specific home sits on.

Which schools serve Embrun?

French-language families have a full kindergarten-to-Grade-12 path inside the village: École élémentaire catholique Embrun (CSDCEO, K–6, in the Pavillon Saint-Jean and Pavillon La Croisée buildings), École élémentaire publique De la Rivière Castor (CEPEO, K–6), and École secondaire catholique Embrun (CSDCEO, Grades 7–12). English-language students bus to Russell village — Cambridge Public School families just east of the village, then Russell Public School and Russell High School (UCDSB), or Mother Teresa and St. Thomas Aquinas (CDSBEO). Catchments are set by your exact address and program, so confirm any property in the board’s school locator before you buy.

How is the commute to Ottawa? Is there a bus?

Plan to drive. Embrun is about 44 km from downtown Ottawa — roughly 30 minutes on Highway 417 in free-flowing traffic, using the St-Guillaume Road (Exit 88) or Limoges Road (Exit 79) interchanges. There is no regular commuter bus serving the village today, so a car is a fact of life here; the OC Transpo Park & Ride at Vars, one interchange west, covers the peak-period run into the city for drivers who want to hand off the last leg, and VIA Rail’s Casselman station is about 12 km away for intercity trains.

What do serviced homeowners pay on top of property tax?

A separate Township utility bill. Under the Township of Russell’s water and wastewater rates by-law, connected homes pay a metered Water Service Charge and Sewer Service Charge — the account opens automatically in the owner’s name at closing, bills follow your metered use, and any balance still unpaid at the end of February is transferred onto the property tax account. Well-and-septic homes skip the utility bill entirely and instead budget for their own well and septic upkeep. Electricity is separate again — Embrun is served by its own local utility, Coopérative Hydro Embrun.
