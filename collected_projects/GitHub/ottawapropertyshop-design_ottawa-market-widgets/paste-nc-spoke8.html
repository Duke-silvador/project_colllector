The Short Answer

Ontario's new-home warranty is administered by Tarion and comes with nearly every new home and condominium sold in the province. Coverage runs in three layers: one year against defects in workmanship and materials, two years against water penetration, failures in the major systems and defects in the building envelope, and seven years against major structural defects. All three clocks start on the date of possession — for a condominium's common elements, from the date the condominium is registered — and the warranty stays with the home if it is resold, with the clocks still running from the original possession date. Claims move on a ladder of forms: the pre-delivery inspection before possession, the 30-Day Form, the Year-End Form, the Second-Year Form and the Major Structural Defect Form. Two neighbouring protections are different files entirely: the deposit money you paid before closing is guarded by the trust and cap rules decoded in Pre Construction Condos Ottawa Tarion Deposit Protection and Spoke 7's deposit structures guide, and late delivery carries its own delayed-occupancy compensation, capped at $7,500 — neither one is this warranty. The tax side of the purchase runs on its own deadlines too — the rebate mechanics are worked through in Ontario HST Rebate on New Homes — so read the two files as a pair: this one guards the house, that one guards the return.

What Each Clock Actually Covers

Think of the three clocks as the house shrinking, layer by layer, from everything to the bones. The one-year warranty is the wide net. It covers defects in workmanship and materials across the whole home — the door that will not latch, the flooring that lifts, the paint and finish defects, the crack that opens as the house settles — plus violations of the Ontario Building Code, substitutions the builder made without authorization, and the baseline requirement that the home be fit for habitation. Almost everything a new owner complains about in the first twelve months lives in this layer, which is exactly why the one-year forms matter so much.

The two-year warranty is the shell and the systems. It covers water penetration through the basement and foundation walls and through the building envelope — windows, doors and caulking — because water is the failure that quietly destroys houses. It covers defects in the systems that deliver electricity, plumbing and heating through the home, and defects in the exterior cladding. Year two is about keeping weather out and services flowing, not about finishes.

The seven-year warranty is the skeleton alone: major structural defects. That means a defect in work or materials that affects the load-bearing parts of the home so seriously that the home's use is substantially affected — significant cracking, distortion or failure of structural elements, including damage from soil movement under the footings. It is deliberately narrow. A sticking door in year five is not structural; a floor structure deflecting under its load is. When buyers say "the house has a seven-year warranty," this is all they mean — and the distinction decides real money, because a year-six repair is either a covered structural claim or an owner's expense, with nothing in between.

  • Year one — everything, briefly. Workmanship, materials, Building Code, substitutions, fitness for habitation. The widest coverage you will ever have on this home, and the shortest window to claim it in.

  • Year two — the shell and the systems. Water penetration, the envelope and cladding, and the electrical, plumbing and heating systems. The failures here are slower and more expensive — which is why they get a second year of their own.

  • Years three to seven — the structure only. Major structural defects: load-bearing failure that substantially affects the home's use. Narrow by design, long by necessity — structure is where the catastrophic money lives.

Read the grid as a countdown, not a menu. Every year that passes, coverage you used to have simply stops existing — the one-year net is gone at month thirteen no matter how genuine the defect. The practical skill is knowing what is covered this month, and getting it onto the right form before the month changes.

The Clock Starts at Possession

Every date in this system counts from one day: the date of possession, when you take the home from the builder. Not the signing — buyers sign a year or more before the clocks start, in a period when the warranty does not yet exist and the deposit rules from Spokes 5 and 7 are the protection that applies. Not the end of construction, not the final walkthrough. Possession day is day one of year one, and the anniversary dates that follow — the end of the first thirty days, the end of year one, the end of year two, the seventh anniversary — are the load-bearing dates of the whole warranty. Put them in a calendar the week you move in, while they are still far away and easy to diary.

The day before possession carries its own formality: the pre-delivery inspection, the PDI. You walk the finished home with the builder's representative, room by room, and everything that is damaged, defective, unfinished or not as agreed is written onto the PDI form. That document is the first page of your warranty file. Its power is evidentiary: a scratch recorded at the PDI was there before you owned the home, full stop, while the same scratch first mentioned in month four invites an argument about moving furniture. Walk it slowly, run every tap and switch, photograph as you go, and do not let the appointment's schedule rush the record. Buyers treat the PDI as a tour; it is closer to taking a statement.

Condominium buyers meet the same clock with one extra wrinkle: the building around the unit — lobby, garage, roof, structure, the common elements — carries its own one, two and seven-year warranties, running from the day the condominium is registered, and those claims belong to the condominium corporation, not to individual owners. Two clocks, two owners, one building: the condo section below returns to it.

The Forms Are the Warranty

Here is the mechanic most owners never learn until it costs them: Tarion coverage is claimed on forms, and each form has a window. The 30-Day Form, filed in the first thirty days after possession, carries the PDI's unresolved items plus everything you find in the first month of living in the home. The Year-End Form, filed in the last thirty days of the first year, is the second — and final — chance to report one-year items, timed so the builder can make one consolidated repair visit near the anniversary, when settling cracks and nail pops have finished appearing. Items that belong to the one-year warranty but miss both forms generally miss their coverage altogether; the warranty does not do late filings.

Year two has its own instrument, the Second-Year Form, which can be filed at any time during the second year for the systems, envelope and water-penetration items the two-year warranty covers. And across the whole span sits the Major Structural Defect Form, available any time up to the seventh anniversary for the structural layer. Forms are filed with Tarion — its MyHome portal is the working channel — and the order of operations never changes: the item is noted, the builder is notified and given its repair window, and only when the builder does not perform does Tarion step in as the backstop, assessing the item against its Construction Performance Guidelines, the published standard that separates a warranted defect from an owner's preference. That backstop is the entire reason the warranty is more than a builder's promise: the builder warrants first, but the coverage does not die if the builder shrugs.

Two habits make the ladder work. First, treat the first month and the eleventh month as inspection seasons: live in every room, run every system in both directions, open every window, and write down everything, however small — a trivial item costs nothing to list and everything to omit. Second, keep the paper. PDI form, every form filed, every notice to the builder, dates on everything. Warranty files are won on records far more often than on arguments.

Condos Run Two Clocks

A condominium purchase stacks two warranties in one building. Your unit is warranted like any new home: one, two and seven years from your occupancy, claimed by you, on your forms. The common elements — structure, roof, envelope, garage, mechanical systems serving the whole building — carry the same 1-2-7 structure, but the clock runs from the condominium's registration date and the claimant is the condominium corporation, acting for all owners together. The corporation runs its own inspection and claims process for the shared building; your role is to report what you see — the garage leak, the corridor water stain — to the board and manager, because the corporation's forms are how those items get fixed.

The practical consequence: in a new condo, ask two questions, not one. What is left on my unit's clocks — and what is left on the building's? The answers are knowable — registration date plus the calendar — and they belong in the same due-diligence conversation as the status certificate. The deposit side of a condo purchase, the trust rules and the $20,000 backstop, is a separate file again: it is decoded in Pre Construction Condos Ottawa Tarion Deposit Protection, and the freehold-versus-condo deposit contrast is worked in New Construction Deposit Structures Ottawa Decoded.

What the Warranty Does Not Do

Knowing the edges matters as much as knowing the cover. The warranty is not deposit protection: it does not guard a dollar you paid before possession. If a build collapses before closing, the responding rules are the trust, cap and termination mechanics of Spoke 5 and Spoke 7 — the warranty never starts on a home you never took. It is not delay compensation either: a builder who delivers late answers under the delayed-occupancy rules, a separate coverage with its own $7,500 cap, worked through in the deposit spokes. And it is not a maintenance plan — ordinary wear and tear, damage caused by the owner or by third parties, and the appliances' own manufacturer warranties live outside it. What wears out from use is yours; what was built wrong is the warranty's.

Two more edges are worth money to remember. The warranty attaches to the home, not the buyer — resell inside the clocks and the remaining coverage passes to the next owner untouched, which makes a young resale's remaining warranty a genuine, priceable feature. And the warranty is not free: its cost rides inside the purchase as an enrolment fee on the closing statement, one line among the itemized closing costs decoded in the next spoke of this series, New Construction Closing Costs Ontario Itemized — read that guide before closing day so the fee, and everything beside it, arrives as arithmetic instead of surprise.

Five Checks, Before and After Possession

  • Confirm the builder and the enrolment before you sign. New homes are warranted because the builder is licensed and the home is enrolled — verify both in the Ontario Builder Directory before the agreement, not after a problem. No enrolment, no clocks: everything in this guide depends on that one check.

  • Work the PDI like a warranty document. Slow, room by room, everything written down, photographs as you go. The PDI form is the foundation of every later claim — an item recorded there starts proven; an item first mentioned in month four starts as an argument.

  • Diary the windows the week you move in. Day 30, the last 30 days of year one, the end of year two, the seventh anniversary. Four dates, entered once, protect the entire warranty. The owners who lose coverage almost never lose it on the merits — they lose it on the calendar.

  • Notify the builder in writing, and keep the paper. Every defect, every form, every repair visit, dated. Tarion's backstop works from the record you built — the builder's repair window, your notices, your filings. A tidy folder is a warranty tool.

  • Read the closing statement for the enrolment fee. Questions Buyers Ask Me

What does the Tarion warranty cover on a new home in Ottawa?
Three clocks, all running from possession. The one-year warranty covers defects in workmanship and materials, Ontario Building Code violations, unauthorized substitutions, and requires the home to be fit for habitation. The two-year warranty covers water penetration, the electrical, plumbing and heating systems, and defects in the exterior cladding. The seven-year warranty covers major structural defects. Tarion warranty Ottawa buyers talk about as one thing is really those three coverages, expiring one after another.

When does Tarion warranty coverage start?
On the date of possession — the day you take the home, not the day you signed. For a condominium unit the clock runs from occupancy; for the condominium's common elements, from the day the condominium is registered. The clocks never restart: buy a three-year-old new build and you inherit whatever is left of its original one, two and seven years.

What is a pre-delivery inspection?
The PDI is the formal walkthrough of your finished home with the builder's representative, held before you take possession. Everything you and the builder note — scratches, defects, items unfinished or not as agreed — goes on the PDI form, the first document of your warranty file. Treat it as evidence, not a tour: a defect never written down that day is much harder to prove later.

What is the 30-Day Form?
Your first formal warranty claim form, filed with Tarion through its MyHome system in the first 30 days after possession. It carries the items left unresolved from the PDI plus anything new you have found since moving in. You get one 30-Day Form — so the first month is an inspection month: live in every room, run every system, and write down everything before the window closes.

What happens if I miss the Year-End Form?
The Year-End Form, filed in the last 30 days of the first year, is the final opportunity to report items that only the one-year warranty covers. Miss it and those one-year items generally fall outside coverage — the two-year and seven-year warranties still stand, with their own forms, but the broad first-year net is gone. It is the last wide door before the warranty narrows.

What counts as a major structural defect?
Damage that affects the load-bearing parts of the home — a defect in work or materials that adversely affects the home's use: significant cracking, distortion or displacement of structural elements, or failure caused by soil movement, are the classic cases. It is the narrowest and longest of the three warranties, claimed on the Major Structural Defect Form at any time up to the seventh anniversary of possession.

My builder is not fixing a warranted item. What happens?
The sequence is fixed: notify the builder in writing, then give the builder its repair window. If the builder does not perform, Tarion steps in — it assesses the item against its Construction Performance Guidelines, the published standard for what counts as a warranted defect, and if the item is warranted and still unrepaired, Tarion arranges the remedy itself. Paper wins these files: dates, notices, forms.

Does the Tarion warranty transfer if I sell the home?
Yes. The warranty is attached to the home, not to the person who bought it from the builder. Sell in year three and the buyer holds the balance of the two-year remainder and the full major-structural-defect clock — without any transfer paperwork resetting or extending a single date. For buyers of nearly-new resale homes, asking what is left on the clocks is a real due-diligence question.

Is the Tarion warranty the same as deposit protection?
No — they guard opposite ends of the purchase. Deposit protection guards the money you pay before the home exists: trust rules and caps for condominiums and freehold, decoded in this series' deposit guides. The warranty guards the home itself after possession. Delayed occupancy is a third, separate coverage, with its own $7,500 cap. Three files, three rulebooks.
