Living in Vernon

Village life in Vernon runs on two streets. Lawrence Street, in the village’s east end, holds the civic core: the Osgoode Township Historical Society and Museum at 7814 Lawrence, and the Vernon Recreation Centre at 7950 Lawrence, with its hall, outdoor rink and pavilion — the room where the village gathers. Bank Street holds the heritage row: the library in the old schoolhouse at 8682 Bank, the churches, and the 1877 general store building. Between them, a few side streets of older homes — and then, in every direction, farmland. The Vernon Village Community Association keeps the social calendar, right down to a village walking club.

Two honest practicalities. First, there is no school in the village — children are bused, mostly to Metcalfe (the Schools section below maps it). Second, Vernon has had no post office since 2017: mail goes to community mailboxes, and oversize parcels are picked up at the Metcalfe post office, about twelve minutes away. Neither fact stops anyone who wants this life; both are worth knowing before you buy it.

A Short History of Vernon — the Township Story, Told from Vernon

The shared story first, because Vernon’s only makes sense inside it. Osgoode Township was established in 1798 and named for William Osgoode, the first Chief Justice of Upper Canada. Settlement began around 1827 — and who arrived first is a genuinely contested piece of local history: the McDonell family’s claim and the Archibald York family’s claim both survive in the record, and honest local history reports both rather than picking a winner. The township’s first crossroads, Baker’s Corners, is today’s Metcalfe. The township was incorporated in 1850, and on January 1, 2001 it amalgamated into the City of Ottawa.

Vernon grew up on the stagecoach road — today’s Bank Street, the old route between Ottawa and Prescott. What made this village different from a crossroads is how much of its 19th century is still standing in one place. Walk the cluster: the general store from 1877, the schoolhouse from 1882, Osgoode Presbyterian Church from 1887, St. George’s Anglican Church from 1899, the church building raised in 1923 that has housed Baptist and United congregations, and a brick farmhouse from about 1889. In September 2025 the City formally designated that whole group under the Ontario Heritage Act — a rare, official acknowledgment that Vernon’s main street is a document.

The schoolhouse deserves its own paragraph. Built in 1882, it taught the village’s children for generations, housed the village’s first library in 1900 — and since 1972 the building has been the library itself, books where the blackboards were. That same year, 1972, the historical society was founded (November 23) and set up in the old mill complex on Lawrence Street: the site had run from Campbell’s carding mill to the Moses Mill operation — grist mill, blacksmith shop and cheese box factory, employing twenty to thirty people in the early 1900s. The museum opened on September 21, 1973; its agricultural building followed in August 1989. And when the township marked its founding story with an official plaque in 2001, the unveiling happened here — at Vernon, during the 25th annual Pioneer Day, on July 28, 2001. The township’s story is, quite literally, kept in Vernon.

Homes & Housing Stock in Vernon

The village core is older detached housing — including genuine 19th-century stock from the stagecoach era — on compact village lots along Bank Street and the side streets. The concessions around it (Lawrence Street east and west, 8th Line Road, Belmeade Road to the southwest) carry the second Vernon: bungalows and two-storey homes on lots of roughly a third of an acre to two acres and more, with working farms of ten acres and up beyond them. There are no condos and no townhouse rows in district 1604 — everything is freehold, and almost everything runs on a private well and septic system.

What that stock means in practice: Vernon homes reward buyers who read houses, not floor plans. A 140-year-old village home can be the best value in the township or a restoration budget with a roof on it — sometimes both. The homework in the next section is not fine print here; in Vernon it is the purchase.

Wells & Septic — the Vernon Homework

Vernon is not on municipal water or sewer, and neither is anywhere else in the township’s villages. Every purchase here should include:

  • A potability and bacteria water test on the well, plus a flow/recovery check — village wells and farm wells behave very differently.

  • A septic inspection with the tank pumped and the bed assessed; ask for the system’s age, size and any permits on file. A replacement bed is one of the largest cheques a rural buyer can inherit.

  • Heating and services history — oil, propane and electric heat are all common out here, and each carries a different monthly reality.

  • For heritage-age homes, a hard look at foundation, wiring and insulation before conditions come off — charm and knob-and-tube are frequent roommates.

None of this is a reason to avoid Vernon — half the Ottawa valley lives happily on well and septic. It is a reason to buy with your eyes open, and it is standard practice in every offer I write out here.

Schools Serving Vernon

First, the honest headline: there is no school in Vernon. The village’s 1882 schoolhouse is the library now, and children are bused to the neighbouring villages. Here is the map as it stands:

  • Metcalfe Public School (OCDSB, Junior Kindergarten to Grade 8) in Metcalfe — the east side’s public elementary school, and the default for most Vernon addresses.

  • Castor Valley Elementary School (OCDSB) in Greely — the Early French Immersion centre for Grades 7 and 8 for the wider Osgoode area.

  • Osgoode Township High School (OCDSB, Grades 9 to 12) in Metcalfe — the township’s only public high school; its own published list of communities served explicitly includes Vernon.

  • St. Catherine School (OCSB, Junior Kindergarten to Grade 6) in Metcalfe — the Catholic elementary serving the Metcalfe side and Vernon-area families.

  • St. Mark Catholic High School (OCSB, Grades 7 to 12) in Manotick — the Catholic secondary destination for this side of the township.

  • French-language boards (CECCE and CEPEO): catchments here are address-specific — run your exact address through each board’s school locator rather than relying on any summary, including this one.

School buses are run by the Ottawa Student Transportation Authority (OSTA) for the English boards and the Consortium de transport scolaire d’Ottawa for the French boards. In a village where every school is a bus ride, the pickup time is part of the house — ask about it.

Churches & Community Life

Vernon’s five-church cluster is the village’s social architecture as much as its skyline: St. George’s Anglican (1899), Osgoode Presbyterian (1887), the Baptist and United congregations in the 1923 church building — and, on Lawrence Street, the Canada Ottawa Amitabha Buddhist temple, a reminder that Ottawa’s south end is more varied than its farm fields suggest. Add the museum’s season of events, Pioneer Day, the recreation centre’s leagues and the community association, and this small village keeps a fuller calendar than suburbs ten times its size.

Getting Around & Who Runs the Village

Bank Street — Ottawa Road 31 — is the spine: north to Greely, the south urban edge and downtown; south toward Winchester and the 401 corridor. Kenmore Road runs east to Kenmore and the Castor River country; 8th Line Road runs north to Metcalfe, the high school and the fairgrounds. There is no regular OC Transpo service — Vernon is a driving village, full stop. Municipal services are the City of Ottawa’s: Ward 20 (Osgoode) is Vernon’s seat at council, and rural services like waste collection, snow clearing and road maintenance run on the city’s rural schedules.

Vernon vs Its Neighbours

Buyers cross-shop Vernon against the other township villages, so here is the honest comparison. Greely is the big one — estate subdivisions, the highest prices, the closest thing to suburbia. Metcalfe has the fair, the high school and the arena of daily convenience. Osgoode village is the service centre — grocery, pharmacy, the Link Pathway. Vernon is the quiet one and the value one: the heritage main street, the museum, the lowest entry prices in the township — and the patience to wait for the right house, because here, supply is an event, not a flow.

Questions Buyers Ask About Vernon

Is Vernon part of Ottawa?

 

Yes. Vernon is a village inside the City of Ottawa, in Ward 20 (Osgoode). It sits in the old Osgoode Township in the city’s rural south end, bisected by Bank Street (Ottawa Road 31), with the postal code K0A 3J0. The township amalgamated into Ottawa on January 1, 2001.

Is this Vernon, British Columbia?

 

No — and if your search brought you here from the other Vernon, you are welcome anyway. This Vernon is the Ontario village inside Ottawa’s city limits, on Bank Street south of Greely. Different province, different market, same name.

Does Vernon have municipal water and sewer?

 

No. Plan on a private well and a septic system — the norm across the whole township. Budget for a water test, a septic inspection, and the ongoing care both systems need, and verify the specifics lot by lot with the City and your inspector.

Which schools serve Vernon?

 

There is no school in the village itself — the 1882 schoolhouse on Bank Street has been the public library since 1972. Most Vernon children ride to Metcalfe Public School (OCDSB, Junior Kindergarten to Grade 8), with Early French Immersion in Grades 7 and 8 at Castor Valley Elementary in Greely, and Osgoode Township High School in Metcalfe for Grades 9 to 12 — the high school’s own list of communities served includes Vernon. Catholic families: St. Catherine School in Metcalfe (OCSB, Junior Kindergarten to Grade 6) serves Vernon-area families, then St. Mark Catholic High School in Manotick for Grades 7 to 12. Always confirm the schools assigned to an exact address in the board’s locator before you buy.

How far is Vernon from downtown Ottawa?

 

Roughly 40 minutes by car down Bank Street in normal conditions. The neighbours are close: Metcalfe is about 12 minutes, Greely about 15, Osgoode village about 18, Russell about 17 and Winchester about 18 — all estimates, and winter has opinions.

What is actually in Vernon?

 

More than its size suggests: the Osgoode Township Historical Society and Museum on Lawrence Street, the public library in the 1882 schoolhouse, the recreation centre with its outdoor rink and pavilion, a five-church 19th-century cluster on and around Bank Street, and the general store building from 1877. One practical note: the village has had no post office since 2017 — mail goes to community mailboxes, and oversize parcels are picked up in Metcalfe.

What are the lots like in Vernon?

 

Two kinds. In the village core, older detached homes sit on compact village lots along Bank Street and the side streets. On the concessions around it — Lawrence Street, 8th Line Road, Belmeade Road — bungalows and two-storeys sit on roughly a third of an acre to two acres or more, with working farms beyond. There are no condos in Vernon; everything is freehold.

Is there public transit in Vernon?

 

No regular OC Transpo service reaches Vernon. This is a car village — plan on driving for work, shopping and appointments. School-age children are bused by the Ottawa Student Transportation Authority.
