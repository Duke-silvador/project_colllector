The Short Answer

A builder's design studio sells two completely different kinds of things at one table. The first kind is structure: ceiling heights, rough-ins, wiring, plumbing, insulation — work that happens inside open walls, at a stage, by trades already on site. Bought at the studio, it is fairly priced and nearly impossible to add later at any sane cost. The second kind is décor: light fixtures, faucets, cabinet handles, paint colours, window coverings. Bought at the studio, it carries a convenience premium — the same items sit in retail stores, with more selection, after you move in. Buyers get in trouble when they shop the two kinds with one wallet: they run out of budget on décor that could have waited, and skip the rough-in that could never come back. Decide the structural list before the appointment. Let the décor list fight for whatever budget is left — or wait for closing, when it can be done at retail, at your pace, room by room.

How the Design Studio Appointment Actually Works

Every Ottawa builder runs the process on the same skeleton, whatever the brochure calls it. Once your agreement is firm, the studio books you for staged appointments tied to the construction schedule — structural selections first, because framing cannot wait for you to decide about a gas line, and finishes later, because flooring and countertops are chosen against a house that already has walls. Each stage has a lock date. After it passes, a change usually means an administration fee if the builder allows it at all: materials get ordered, trades get scheduled, and some changes become physically impossible once the rough-in stage is behind you.

Two things about the room itself are worth knowing before you sit down. First, the studio works from a price book, and the prices are real but they are not the market — they are one supplier's prices, quoted to a captive buyer, bundled with installation and warranty. Some will beat a retail quote once installation is counted; many will not. Second, the model home you fell in love with at the sales centre is the studio's best salesperson: it is typically finished with a long list of the very upgrades on the table. The honest way to see a model is with its upgrade list in your hand, priced. Ask for it — builders will provide it — and the gap between the base house and the model stops being a feeling and becomes a number you can plan around.

Selections also join the money side of your purchase. Builders commonly collect a deposit on upgrades, or fold them into the deposit schedule decoded in New Construction Deposit Structures Ottawa Decoded — so the studio bill can be due, in part, long before closing day. Walk in knowing that, and the appointment stays a planning exercise instead of becoming a surprise invoice.

Structural Upgrades: The Ones Worth Their Price

The test for every item on this list is the same: if you skip it now, adding it later means demolition. These are the upgrades that earn the builder's price.

  • Ceiling height and door heights. Nine-foot ceilings where they are offered change how every room feels, and they cannot be retrofitted at any price a sane person would pay. If the budget holds one splurge, this is the classic candidate.

  • Basement bathroom rough-in. The drains and vents set in the concrete before the floor is poured. Retrofitting one means cutting the slab. If a finished basement is even a maybe, the rough-in is the cheapest insurance in the entire price book.

  • Gas-line rough-ins. For the stove, the dryer, a future fireplace or a barbecue line: inexpensive while the walls are open, disruptive forever after. Buy the ones matched to a real plan, not every stub on the list.

  • Electrical capacity and conduit. A larger panel, extra circuits where a workshop, hot tub or EV charger may land, conduit runs for future wiring, and pot-light layouts in ceilings that will be closed. The fixtures can come later; the pathways cannot.

  • Plumbing rough-ins beyond the plan. A water line to the fridge location, a pot-filler rough-in over a gas range, laundry moved or added where the design allows. Small money inside open walls; large money through finished ones.

  • Insulation and soundproofing. Upgraded insulation in key walls and floors, and sound treatment between a primary bedroom and the rooms beside it, are invisible in the showroom and unforgettable in daily life. They are also permanently sealed in once the drywall closes.

  • Window and door openings. Larger windows, a wider patio door, a garden door where a window was drawn — openings are framing decisions. Once the wall stands, the conversation is over.

  • Garage access and height. An interior access door from garage to house where the design offers it, and a taller garage door if you drive anything oversized: both are structural, both are much harder later, and the access door gets used every single day.

None of this needs a blank cheque. The discipline is to price the structural list first, in full, and only then look at finishes. A house with the right bones and standard décor can be dressed up any weekend. A house with beautiful faucets and no basement rough-in is beautiful faucets over a slab you will pay to cut.

The Kitchen: Where the Money Goes

No room absorbs upgrade money like the kitchen, and the studio knows it. The way through it is to split the kitchen into the same two kinds. The layout is structure: taller wall cabinets that reach the ceiling, an island where the plan allows one, the gas rough-in, the water line, and the wiring for under-cabinet lighting are decided once and lived with for decades — price those at the studio without apology. The surfaces are a judgement call: a stone countertop through the builder arrives installed, warranted with the house, and financed inside the purchase; the same stone bought later is a separate project with separate quotes, sometimes cheaper, always more disruptive in a kitchen you are already cooking in. Flooring follows the same logic — a main-floor material that runs continuously through several rooms is painful to redo underfoot, while a single bedroom floor is an easy later project. What plainly belongs to the after-closing list is everything that unscrews: faucets, cabinet hardware, light fixtures, and the appliance package whenever the builder's offering is a brand or tier you would not choose in a store.

Better Done After Closing

Keep this list beside the structural one at the appointment. Each of these is legitimate to want — just rarely worth the studio's price for it.

  • Light fixtures, faucets and showerheads. Swapped in an afternoon with a screwdriver and a wrench, from a retail selection ten times the studio's, at prices that compete.

  • Cabinet hardware and paint colours. Handles and knobs are a hardware-store trip; paint beyond the builder's standard palette is the cheapest transformation in any house and the easiest to redo when taste changes.

  • Window coverings and closet organizers. Measured for your windows and your clothes, after you have lived with the light for a month — not guessed at a studio table a year before possession.

  • Backsplash tile and smart-home devices. A backsplash over a finished counter is a one-day tiling job; thermostats, doorbells and switches install in minutes and improve every year. Neither needs to ride on a 25-year mortgage.

That last point is the quiet one: every upgrade rolled into the purchase is borrowed money, repaid with interest for as long as you carry the mortgage. Décor bought after closing is paid once, at retail, when you actually want it. The studio's convenience is real — but it should be priced as convenience, not mistaken for necessity.

The Money Math Nobody Shows You

Upgrades do not sit beside your purchase price; they join it. Every selection becomes part of the price of the home, and the closing math runs on the total: the land transfer tax calculation, and the HST that applies to new housing, both run on the price including upgrades — and the new-housing rebate calculation moves with it. The full statement is worked line by line in New Construction Closing Costs Ontario Itemized; for the studio appointment, the takeaway is simpler: a five-figure upgrade list is not a five-figure decision. It carries tax, and if it is financed, it carries interest too.

Two more money facts belong in the room. First, appraisals do not credit upgrades dollar for dollar: a lender's valuation of the finished house follows the market for comparable homes, not your invoice from the studio, so a very heavy upgrade load can open a gap between the price you pay and the value the financing sees — the deeper mechanics sit in New Construction Closing Costs Ontario Itemized as well. Second, builder-installed work arrives inside the home's warranty envelope — the coverage explained in Tarion Warranty Ottawa New Homes 1 2 7 Explained — while the same work done by your own trades after closing stands on their warranties alone. That is worth something, and it is worth the most exactly where the structural rule already points: on the work inside the walls.

Five Checks Before Your Appointment

  • Get the price book early. Ask for the upgrade price list when you sign, not the week of the appointment. Decisions made against real prices, at your kitchen table, are better than decisions made against a clock in a showroom.

  • Price your top ten at retail. Take the ten items you want most and get two real quotes or store prices for each, installed. The comparison tells you which studio prices are fair and which are convenience charges — item by item, not by gut feeling.

  • Lock the structural list first, in writing. Decide the rough-ins, openings and capacity items before the first sitting, and treat every stage deadline as final. This is the same discipline the flagship prescribes for the whole purchase — Buying New Construction in Ottawa and the One Mistake You Cannot Reverse — applied to the one appointment where the house is still changeable.

  • See samples in daylight, and photograph everything. Studio lighting flatters flooring and stone. Carry samples to a window, photograph your selections beside each other, and keep the photos with the paperwork.

  • Get every selection confirmed in writing. Names, finishes, locations, prices and the stage each belongs to — signed by both sides. On closing day, that sheet is the difference between an error the builder fixes and an argument about what was said.

Do those five things and the design studio becomes what it should be: the one genuinely enjoyable appointment of the purchase, where the standard house turns into yours — on your terms, at prices you chose with your eyes open.

What Ottawa Clients Say

Questions Buyers Ask Me

Which design studio upgrades are worth it in a new Ottawa home?
The structural ones — anything that would mean opening a wall, a floor or a ceiling to add later. Taller ceilings, a basement bathroom rough-in, gas-line rough-ins, electrical panel capacity, insulation and soundproofing, and larger window and door openings are worth pricing at the studio because the builder does them while the house is open. Decorative items — fixtures, faucets, hardware, paint, coverings — can almost always be done after closing, with more choice and usually for less.

What is a rough-in, and which rough-ins are worth paying for?
A rough-in is the hidden plumbing, gas or electrical work installed behind the walls before they are closed, ready for a fixture you add later. The rough-ins worth their price are the ones matched to a real plan: a basement bathroom rough-in if you will finish the basement, a gas line if you will ever cook on gas or add a fireplace, and a water line where a future fridge or pot filler will sit. A rough-in with no plan behind it is just a capped pipe you paid for.

Why are structural upgrades treated differently from decorative ones?
Because the cost of adding them later is not the item — it is the demolition. A pot light is inexpensive; opening, patching and repainting a finished ceiling to wire it is not. Structural work rides on labour the builder already has on site, inside walls that are already open, inspected as part of the build. Decorative work needs no open wall at all, which is why the studio premium on it buys convenience, not necessity.

Should I upgrade flooring through the builder or after closing?
Decide by the floor, not the room. Hardwood or tile on a main floor that runs continuously through several rooms is disruptive to redo later, and the builder's price includes the subfloor prep and the warranty on the installation — often worth it. Flooring in a single bedroom, or anything you may replace when styles change, is usually better priced after closing, when flooring retailers compete for the job and you can match the exact product.

Do upgrades change my closing costs or my taxes?
Yes — upgrades form part of the purchase price of a new home, so the total your closing math runs on rises with every selection: the land transfer tax calculation, and the HST that applies to new housing, both run on the price including upgrades. Heavy upgrade loads also show up in the new-housing rebate calculation. The companion guide in this series, New Construction Closing Costs Ontario Itemized, works through that statement line by line.

Does the builder charge for changes after my selections lock?
Builders stage their selections — structural choices first, finishes later — and once a stage locks, a change usually means an administration fee if it is possible at all, because materials may already be ordered and trades scheduled. Some changes become physically impossible once framing or rough-in passes. Treat every stage deadline as final, decide the structural list before the first appointment, and get every selection confirmed in writing.

Are model homes a fair picture of the standard house?
No — a model home is the builder's showroom, and it is typically loaded with the upgrades the design studio sells: taller cabinets, stone counters, upgraded flooring, extra pot lights, finished basements. The gap between the model and the base house is exactly the studio's price book. Before your appointment, ask the builder for the model's upgrade list with prices. Standing in the model with that list in hand is the single most useful preparation there is.

Do expensive upgrades pay back at resale?
Partially, and unevenly. Structural quality — ceiling height, a roughed-in basement bathroom, insulation — supports value because buyers can feel it and cannot easily add it. Highly personal décor choices, unusual colours and niche luxury items return the least, because the next buyer prices them as their own project. Upgrade for the years you will live in the house first; treat any resale return as a bonus, not the reason.

Can I do upgrades myself between the builder's stages?
No. During construction the site is the builder's — buyers cannot bring their own trades into an unfinished house, for liability, warranty and scheduling reasons. Work you want done by your own people happens after closing and possession. That is the practical reason the structural list matters so much: anything you want inside the walls must go through the builder, at the builder's stage, or wait until the house is yours and the walls are finished.

Should I bring my own agent to the design studio process?
You can, and on the big structural decisions it helps to have someone in the room whose only job is your side of the table — the studio's job is to sell selections. An agent who works new construction can flag which choices carry deadlines, which prices deserve a second quote, and which upgrades your future buyers will actually value. It costs you nothing at the studio; the guidance rides on the representation you arranged before the purchase, which is the subject of this series' flagship guide.
