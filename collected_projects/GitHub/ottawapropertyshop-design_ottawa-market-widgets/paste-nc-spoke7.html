The Short Answer

A deposit structure is the payment timetable buried in the builder's agreement: an initial deposit at signing, instalments at set points after signing and as construction advances, and the rest of the cash picture settled at closing. Two protections stand under it, and they are not the same. A condominium deposit sits in trust under the Condominium Act, with Tarion backstopping $20,000 of it. A freehold deposit has no trust rule at all — Tarion's caps do all the work: $60,000 on a home at $600,000 or less, and 10% of the price to a maximum of $100,000 above that. Read the schedule's total against those numbers before you sign: whatever a freehold schedule asks beyond the cap is your exposed money. The Anatomy of a Deposit Schedule

Every builder's schedule has the same skeleton, whatever the amounts. Read any pre-construction agreement and New construction deposits Ottawa buyers pay resolve into the same moving parts:

  • The deposit with the offer. The first cheque moves when the agreement is signed — before a shovel is in the ground, and long before there is a home to inspect. On a condominium, this is also where your 10-day cooling-off window starts: the agreement and disclosure statement are yours to read while the clock runs.

  • The staged instalments. Further deposits fall due at points the agreement sets — counted in days from signing and tied to the build's progress. Cheque by cheque, a condominium deposit builds to five figures and often six, paid over to a builder for a home that does not exist yet.

  • The upgrades money. Money you put down on design-studio selections and extras joins the same stream — and it counts inside the same protection, because Tarion's coverage includes money paid toward upgrades and other extras.

  • The balance at closing. The deposit is not the purchase. It is the front end of the cash plan: the remainder of the price — with the closing costs itemized in New Construction Closing Costs Ontario Itemized — comes due when the home is finished and the keys change hands.

What changes from builder to builder is the weight on each part: the size of the first cheque, how much is staged and how fast, and the total the schedule reaches before closing. Condominium Schedules: Trust First

Start with the rule everything else hangs on. Under Ontario's Condominium Act, the deposit you give a condo builder must be placed in trust — held in trust until it is paid to the person entitled to it, or the amount of your deposit is insured. In practice, your deposit cheques are made out to the trustee — usually the builder's lawyer, in trust — not to the builder's operating account. The builder does not get to spend your deposit on construction.

So how does deposit money above $20,000 ever reach the builder? Only when that excess is covered by deposit insurance arranged for the project — excess deposit insurance. That makes the buyer's question obvious, and almost nobody asks it: before your deposits pass $20,000, ask for evidence that the project's excess deposit insurance is in place. And if the builder terminates your purchase agreement — a cancelled project is the case everyone fears — your deposit must be returned to you in full within 10 days, unless the termination results from your own default. Freehold Schedules: Tarion's Caps Do the Work

What the Schedule Does to Your Cash

A deposit schedule is a commitment of liquid money, years ahead of owning anything. Three consequences follow, and none of them appear in a sales centre presentation. First, the money is committed, not parked: it cannot be redirected to another opportunity, a rate change, or a change in your own plans, without defaulting on the agreement. Second, the protection has edges: deposit coverage responds to a short list of events — the builder goes bankrupt, the builder fundamentally breaches the agreement, or you exercise a legal right to terminate. Outside that list, walking away is a default, and a defaulting buyer's deposit is the builder's claim. Third, the deposit is only the front of the cash plan: the balance of the price, the tax position worked in Ontario HST Rebate on New Homes Up to 130000 Dollars and How It Stacks With the Federal Rebate, and the closing layer all land at the end — the plan has to survive to closing, not just to the first cheque.

Keep every receipt, every notice and your Statement of Critical Dates: deposit claims run on paper, and the buyers who get paid fastest are the ones who can prove every dollar and every date. And keep the categories separate in your head — deposit protection guards the money before closing; the home itself is guarded after closing by the 1-2-7 warranty in Tarion Warranty Ottawa New Homes 1 2 7 Explained, and a delayed occupancy date is a third coverage again, with its own $7,500 cap.

Five Checks Before the Cheques Move

  • Who is the trustee? On a condominium, your deposit cheques should be payable to the trustee — typically the builder's lawyer, in trust. Get the trustee's name in writing before the first cheque.

  • Is the excess deposit insurance in place? Ask for evidence the project carries it before your deposits pass $20,000 — that is the exact point where Tarion's own coverage stops and the insurance becomes your protection.

  • Is the builder licensed and the project enrolled? Check the builder in the Ontario Builder Directory, the HCRA's public registry. Deposit protection exists in its current form because illegal, unlicensed selling is where buyers actually lose deposits.

  • What is the schedule's total against the cap? Add every instalment, including the upgrades money. On a freehold purchase, everything above $60,000 — or 10% of the price, to $100,000 — is exposed money. Know that number before signing, not after.

  • Which clock are you on? A condominium gives you a 10-day cooling-off window to read the deposit terms, the occupancy dates and the Statement of Critical Dates. A freehold purchase gives you a different clock instead: 45 days from signing to notify Tarion and keep maximum coverage. Frequently Asked Questions

How much deposit does a builder ask for on a new home in Ottawa?
It varies more than buyers expect. Across Ottawa builders, deposit structures run from about 3% of the price, staged over the build, to $80,000–$100,000 on a single detached home. That spread is wide enough to decide which builder a buyer can actually sign with — and the only version that matters is the schedule written in your own purchase agreement.

Is a new construction deposit one cheque?
No. A deposit structure is a schedule: an initial deposit with the agreement, further instalments at set points after signing and as construction advances, and the balance of the deposit picture settled by closing. On a condominium, each date and trigger is also set out in the disclosure package and the Statement of Critical Dates.

Where does my condominium deposit actually go?
Into trust. Under the Condominium Act, a condo deposit must be held in trust until it is paid to the person entitled to it or the amount is insured. In practice, your deposit cheques are made out to the trustee — usually the builder's lawyer, in trust — not to the builder's operating account, and the builder does not get to spend the money on construction.

What is excess deposit insurance?
The insurance a builder arranges so that deposit money above Tarion's $20,000 can be released from trust and used by the builder. A builder who wants to draw on your deposits beyond the first $20,000 can only do it when that excess is covered by deposit insurance arranged for the project — which is why the practical step is to ask for evidence of it before your deposits pass $20,000.

How much of a freehold deposit does Tarion cover?
Up to $60,000 on a home priced at $600,000 or less, and 10% of the purchase price to a maximum of $100,000 above that — so $65,000 on a $650,000 home and $80,000 on an $800,000 home. The coverage includes money paid toward upgrades and other extras. A freehold deposit has no trust rule, so the cap is the whole protection.

What is the 45-day rule for freehold deposits?
Starting April 1, 2026, buyers of new freehold homes must notify Tarion of their purchase within 45 days of signing to qualify for maximum deposit coverage. Register late or not at all and coverage falls to a separate fund capped at $15 million a year. Condominium purchasers are not required to notify Tarion — the trust system already does the watching.

What happens to my deposit if the project is cancelled?
If the builder terminates your purchase agreement, your deposit must be returned to you in full within 10 days, unless the termination results from your own default. Deposit protection responds when a builder goes bankrupt, fundamentally breaches the agreement, or you exercise a legal right to terminate: Tarion pays up to $20,000 of any condominium amount not returned, and the trust and the excess insurance carry the balance.

Can I get my deposit back if I change my mind?
Outside a legal termination right — the condominium cooling-off window, a builder breach, a missed outside date — no. Walking away from an agreement is a default, and a defaulting buyer's deposit is the builder's claim, not Tarion's file. Deposit protection is not a refund policy for buyer's remorse, which is why the schedule has to be read before the first cheque, not after it.

Is deposit protection the same thing as the Tarion warranty?
No. Deposit protection guards the money you pay before the home exists; the 1-2-7 warranty guards the home itself after closing — one year on most workmanship and materials, two years on the envelope and systems, seven years on major structural defects. A late occupancy date is a third, separate coverage, with its own cap of $7,500, handled under the delayed-closing rules rather than the deposit rules.
