What are closing costs when buying a house in Ontario? They are everything you pay on top of the purchase price before the keys are yours — and on a typical Ottawa purchase they add up to roughly one and a half to four percent of the price. Almost every buyer budgets the down payment to the dollar and then meets these costs for the first time on a statement of adjustments a few days before closing. This guide maps every line in advance: what each cost is, what it typically runs, who collects it, and when — so the statement your lawyer sends holds no surprises, whether this is your first purchase or your fifth time buying and selling real estate in Ottawa.

Closing Costs In Ontario — The Short Answer

What are they?

The taxes, fees and one-time charges due on top of the purchase price when a home purchase closes — land transfer tax, your lawyer’s bill, title insurance, registration fees, and, depending on the purchase, an appraisal, a status certificate, sales tax on a mortgage insurance premium, or the new-build layer of HST, Tarion and development charges.

What is the biggest line?

Ontario land transfer tax — a provincial tax charged on the purchase price in graduated brackets. On a $700,000 Ottawa home it is $10,475. It is collected by the Province of Ontario, through your lawyer, at registration — not by the CRA, and not by the City of Ottawa.

Does Ottawa add a second tax?

No. Toronto is the only Ontario city with its own municipal land transfer tax stacked on top of the provincial one. An Ottawa buyer pays the provincial tax once. This is the single most common closing-cost confusion in the province, and it costs Toronto buyers roughly double.

How much should you budget?

The working rule is 1.5% to 4% of the purchase price, in cash, on top of your down payment — and it must be cash. Most of it cannot be added to the mortgage. Eligible first-time buyers get up to $4,000 of the land transfer tax refunded, which pulls a first purchase toward the low end of the range.

Land Transfer Tax — The Biggest Line On The Statement

Ontario land transfer tax is a provincial tax on the purchase price, charged once, when the transfer is registered. It is calculated in graduated brackets — each slice of the price is taxed at its own rate, the way income tax brackets work:

Who collects it: the Province of Ontario, through the Ministry of Finance. Your lawyer calculates it, collects it from you with the closing funds, and pays it inside the Teraview electronic registration on closing day — the tax is remitted as part of registering the transfer itself. Two blame-corrections worth making, because buyers get both wrong: it is not a federal tax (the CRA does not touch it), and it is not a City of Ottawa charge (the city collects development charges on new homes, which is a different line entirely, covered below).

The first-time buyer refund: eligible first-time buyers get up to $4,000 of the tax refunded — enough to erase the tax entirely on a purchase up to about $368,333. The refund is usually applied right at closing through your lawyer, so it reduces the cash you bring rather than arriving later. The eligibility rules, in plain form:

  • You must be a first-time buyer — you have never owned a home, or an interest in a home, anywhere in the world.

  • You must be at least 18 and a Canadian citizen or permanent resident.

  • The home must become your principal residence within nine months of the transfer.

  • A spouse’s history counts — if your spouse owned a home while you were spouses, the refund is reduced or lost; if they owned and sold before you became spouses, your own share can still qualify.

One Ottawa-specific trap hides in that last bullet and in the downsizing direction: the refund belongs to people who have never owned. A senior selling the family home and buying smaller does not get it back a second time — a point my seniors program makes in full, because the tax on the smaller purchase is a real line in a downsizing budget.

No, Ottawa Does Not Have A Second Land Transfer Tax

Say it plainly, because every year buyers moving from the GTA budget for a tax that does not exist here: Ottawa has no municipal land transfer tax. Toronto is the only city in Ontario that charges one — a second, city-level tax on the same purchase, at the same graduated rates, collected alongside the provincial tax since 2008. A Toronto buyer effectively pays the land transfer tax twice. Move the same purchase to Ottawa and the municipal half simply is not there — on the $700,000 example above, that is roughly another $10,475 a Toronto buyer would owe and an Ottawa buyer does not.

The confusion runs the other way too: Ottawa buyers sometimes assume the provincial tax is a Toronto thing. It is not — it applies on every purchase in Ontario, Ottawa included. One province-wide tax, collected once, and in Ottawa that is the end of the land-transfer-tax story.

Your Lawyer’s Bill — Fees, Disbursements And The $85 Registrations

The legal line confuses buyers because one invoice carries three different kinds of money. Understanding the split is most of what there is to understand:

  • The legal fee — the lawyer’s own charge for the work: searching title, reviewing the agreement and the mortgage, preparing the documents, registering the transfer. Many Ottawa firms quote a flat fee, commonly in the $1,000–$2,500 range on a purchase, and 13% HST applies to the fee.

  • Disbursements — money the lawyer pays out to third parties on your behalf and bills back at cost: the title search, execution searches, courier and banking charges, and the government registration fees. These are not profit and are mostly not negotiable — they are the tolls on the road every closing travels.

  • Third-party charges that ride the same bill — above all the title insurance premium, which the lawyer orders but the insurer collects.

The registration toll is worth knowing exactly, because it is one of the few fixed numbers in the whole map: registering a document electronically in Ontario’s land registration system costs $85 per document (Teraview’s published pricing — a $71.55 statutory fee, an $11.90 electronic-services fee and $1.55 in tax, per the province’s current fee schedule). A standard purchase with a mortgage registers two documents — the transfer and the mortgage charge — so the line is usually $170. The collector is the province’s land registration system; your lawyer is the courier.

And one invisible line inside the lawyer’s statement: adjustments. If the seller prepaid property taxes or utilities past the closing date, you reimburse them for the part that belongs to you; if they underpaid, you receive the credit. Either way it runs through the statement of adjustments your lawyer prepares — which is why two buyers of identical homes on different dates can owe different amounts on the day.

The Line That Cannot Go On Your Mortgage — Sales Tax On Default Insurance

If you buy with less than 20% down, your lender must have mortgage default insurance — the subject of my full guide to mortgage default insurance in Canada, which explains who pays the premium and who it actually protects. The premium itself is normally added to your mortgage and paid off over the amortization, so it does not feel like a closing cost at all.

The tax on it does. Ontario charges 8% provincial sales tax (PST) on the default insurance premium — and unlike the premium, the tax cannot be added to the mortgage. It is due in cash, at closing, through your lawyer. On the worked example’s insured variation that was $1,562.40 — money first-time buyers in particular often have not set aside, because every calculator shows the premium going onto the loan and quietly leaves its tax behind. Budget the premium and its tax, and neither surprises you.

Title Insurance — One Premium, Paid Once

The title insurance line is the other insurance on the statement, and it behaves the opposite way: a one-time premium, roughly $250 to $500 on a typical purchase, paid at closing and never renewed, collected by the insurer your lawyer orders it from — FCT, Chicago Title, Stewart Title or TitlePLUS are the four you will meet in Ontario. It protects your ownership against title defects, survey problems and fraud, and it comes in two policies that protect two different parties — the lender policy your bank requires, and the owner policy that protects you. That distinction earns its own guide in this series: what does title insurance cover in Ontario. Here it is simply the cheapest line on the map for the years of coverage it buys.

Buying A Condo — The $100 Line

A condo purchase adds exactly one standard line to the map, and it is the only one with a legislated price cap. Before a condo deal firms up, your lawyer orders the status certificate — the corporation’s official disclosure of the unit’s financial standing, the corporation’s budget and reserve fund, its rules, and any legal claims against it. The condominium corporation collects the fee, and the Condominium Authority of Ontario’s cap holds it to $100 including taxes. A hundred dollars for the single most informative document in a condo purchase — order it early in the conditional period, because your lawyer needs time to actually read it, and what it reveals is a subject the condo guides in this program take up in full.

Buying New — The Extra Layer

Buy a brand-new home instead of a resale and a second layer of costs lands on top of everything above. This is the layer that produces the famous new-build closing shock, and it is where the organizations multiply:

  • HST — collected by the CRA, through the builder. A new home carries 13% HST (a 5% federal part and an 8% provincial part). A resale home is exempt — you never pay HST on a used home. Part of the tax can come back: the long-standing new housing rebates return up to about $6,300 of the federal part (phasing out between $350,000 and $450,000) and up to $24,000 of the Ontario part, and builder prices are usually quoted net of those rebates, with the rebate assigned to the builder at closing. On top of those sit the newer first-time buyer and enhanced rebate programs — worth up to far more, and time-limited — which my new construction guide takes apart in full: buying new construction in Ottawa.

  • Tarion enrolment fee — collected by Tarion, through the builder. Every new home is enrolled in Ontario’s new home warranty program before it is sold. Tarion’s fee is set by a price-based schedule (the current one took effect September 1, 2025): $585 on a home up to $300,000, climbing with price to roughly $2,190 at $1,000,000, plus 13% HST. The builder is charged the fee and passes it to you on the statement of adjustments — check for it there.

  • Development charges — collected by the City of Ottawa and the school boards, through the builder. These pay for the growth infrastructure around a new community — roads, sewers, transit, parks — and they are the reason two otherwise identical homes, one resale and one new, close so differently. On the City of Ottawa’s current schedule (April 1, 2026) the charge on a single or semi-detached home is $57,827 inside the Greenbelt and $64,634 outside it, plus an education development charge of $4,507 per unit for the school boards. The builder pays them and builds them into the price you pay — but uncapped levies in a purchase agreement can also be added at closing, which is why the protective move is to negotiate a hard dollar cap on levies and adjustments before you sign, and have your lawyer confirm the cap is in the agreement.

  • Utility hook-ups and meters — collected by the utilities, through the builder. Water, hydro and gas connections and meters are commonly billed to the buyer at closing on a new build. The amounts vary by project; they belong inside the same cap conversation.

None of that makes a new home a bad buy — the warranty, the rebates and a home built to current code are real value. It makes the new-build budget a different document from the resale budget, and the buyers who get hurt are the ones who priced a new home with a resale checklist.

Home Insurance — The Binder Your Lender Wants First

The last collector on the map is the one buyers arrange earliest. Your lender will not advance the mortgage until your home insurance is in place — evidenced by a binder, the insurer’s temporary proof of coverage, delivered to your lawyer before closing. The premium is paid to your insurer (insurers in Ontario are regulated by FSRA, the same regulator that appears throughout this series), on the insurer’s own schedule rather than through the closing statement. Arrange it the week the deal firms up, not the week it closes: a binder scramble in the final 48 hours is the most avoidable closing-week stress there is, and on a new build the policy must also line up with the occupancy and closing dates the builder sets.

The one-sentence version: Closing costs in Ontario run about 1.5% to 4% of the price on top of your down payment — the province collects the biggest line (land transfer tax, at registration, through your lawyer), Ottawa adds no second tax, first-time buyers get up to $4,000 back, and the only line that routinely ambushes buyers is the 8% sales tax on a mortgage insurance premium, because it is the one amount that cannot go on the mortgage.

Questions Ottawa Home Buyers Ask About Closing Costs In Ontario

What are closing costs when buying a house in Ontario?

The charges due on top of the purchase price when the purchase closes: Ontario land transfer tax, your lawyer’s fees and disbursements, the title insurance premium, land registration fees, and — depending on the purchase — an appraisal, a condo status certificate, sales tax on a mortgage default insurance premium, and the new-build layer of HST, Tarion enrolment and development charges. Budget 1.5% to 4% of the price, in cash, on top of your down payment.

Who collects land transfer tax in Ontario?

The Province of Ontario. Your lawyer calculates the tax, takes it from you with the closing funds, and remits it inside Ontario’s Teraview electronic registration system on closing day, to the Ministry of Finance. It is not collected by the CRA — a common mix-up — and in Ottawa it is not collected by the city either.

Is there a municipal land transfer tax in Ottawa?

No. Ottawa buyers pay the Ontario land transfer tax only. Toronto is the sole Ontario city with a second, municipal land transfer tax stacked on the provincial one — which is why a Toronto purchase carries roughly double the land transfer tax of the same purchase in Ottawa.

How much land transfer tax do I pay on a $700,000 home in Ottawa?

$10,475. The graduated arithmetic: 0.5% on the first $55,000 ($275), 1% on the next $195,000 ($1,950), 1.5% on the next $150,000 ($2,250), and 2% on the remaining $300,000 ($6,000). An eligible first-time buyer subtracts the $4,000 refund and pays $6,475.

Do first-time buyers get land transfer tax back in Ontario?

Yes — a refund of up to $4,000, which wipes out the tax entirely on purchases up to about $368,333. You must be at least 18, a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, a true first-time buyer who has never owned a home anywhere in the world, and the home must become your principal residence within nine months. Your lawyer usually applies the refund right at closing.

Can closing costs be added to my mortgage?

Almost none of them. Land transfer tax, legal fees, registration fees and the rest are cash costs due at closing. The one big exception is the mortgage default insurance premium, which is normally added to the mortgage — but Ontario’s 8% sales tax on that premium cannot be added and is due in cash. This is exactly why lenders ask for proof of closing-cost funds, not just a down payment.

Do I pay HST on a resale home in Ontario?

No. Resale homes are exempt from HST. The 13% HST applies to brand-new homes (and substantially renovated ones), collected by the builder for the CRA at closing — partly offset by the new housing rebates, and by the newer first-time buyer and enhanced rebate programs covered in my new construction guide.

What extra closing costs come with a new build?

The new-build layer: 13% HST on the price (less any rebate), the Tarion enrolment fee (price-based — $585 up to $300,000, roughly $2,190 at $1,000,000, plus HST), development charges the builder passes through (in Ottawa, $57,827 inside the Greenbelt or $64,634 outside for a single or semi on the current schedule, plus a $4,507 education charge), and utility hook-up and meter fees. Protect yourself by negotiating a hard cap on levies and adjustments before signing.

What does a condo buyer pay that a freehold buyer does not?

The status certificate — $100 including taxes, a fee capped by regulation, paid to the condominium corporation during the conditional period. In exchange you get the corporation’s own audited picture of the unit’s standing, the building’s finances and reserve fund, and its rules, for your lawyer to review before the deal firms up.

Why is there sales tax on my mortgage insurance premium?

Because Ontario taxes insurance premiums. If you buy with less than 20% down, the default insurance premium (2.80%–4.00% of the mortgage, set by CMHC, Sagen or Canada Guaranty) can be added to your mortgage — but the 8% provincial sales tax on it must be paid in cash at closing. My mortgage default insurance guide in this series covers the premium itself, and who it protects, in full.

Where do closing costs sit in the whole purchase?

At the end — on closing day, mostly flowing through your lawyer’s trust account to the province, the registry, the insurers and the seller’s adjustments.
