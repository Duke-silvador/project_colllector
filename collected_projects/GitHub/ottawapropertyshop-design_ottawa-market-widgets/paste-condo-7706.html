The Longfields Condo Stock By Type

Longfields condos come in three shapes, and they live differently:

  • Apartment condos. One-level units in condo buildings — an elevator or a short stair, underground or surface parking, and amenities that range from a party room to a gym. In Longfields you find them at The Station on Longfields Drive and on Claridge Drive, steps from the Transitway: the commuter’s condo, and the downsizer’s one-floor answer.
  • Condo townhomes. Full townhouses — your own front door and a garage — on private streets where the exterior, the roof, the road and the grounds belong to a condominium corporation and you pay a monthly condo fee instead of maintaining them yourself. Tayside Private and Via Verona are the classic Longfields examples.
  • Stacked townhomes. Two homes stacked in one building form — an upper and a lower unit, each with its own entrance. Tribeca Private is Longfields’ stacked bench: townhome living at an apartment-condo price, and the usual first rung of the Longfields ladder.

One warning that belongs in every condo guide: a townhouse is not automatically a condo, and a condo is not automatically a townhouse shape. Longfields is full of freehold townhomes that look identical to condo towns from the street. The difference is legal, not visual — it decides whether you pay a monthly fee, who insures the building, and what you must review before you buy. That is why this guide’s searches filter to condominium listings only.

Condo Fees Explained — What The Monthly Fee Actually Buys

The condo fee is the number buyers ask about first and understand last. In a Longfields condo corporation, the monthly fee typically covers:

  • Building insurance on the structure and common elements — your own policy covers your unit’s interior and contents.
  • Common-element maintenance: roofs, exterior walls, windows in many corporations, hallways, elevators, parking, snow clearing, landscaping and garbage. On the private streets of Tayside and Via Verona it also carries the road itself — a cost freehold streets hand to the city and private-street corporations carry themselves.
  • Water in most Barrhaven corporations (heat and hydro vary — this is a status-certificate question, never an assumption).
  • Amenities where they exist: gym, pool, party room, guest suite.
  • The reserve fund contribution — the share of every fee that is saved, by law, for the major repairs the building will certainly need: roof, windows, asphalt, balconies, garage concrete.

Two fees that look $80 apart can be worlds apart in value: a higher fee with water, heat and a healthy reserve fund can cost you less than a “cheap” fee headed for a special assessment. My differentiator is simple — fee transparency before the offer, not after: for every condo I show, we look at the fee, what it includes, and the corporation’s reserve-fund position up front, so the monthly number is never a surprise at the lawyer’s office.

Buying A Resale Condo In Longfields — The Status Certificate Decides

Every resale condo offer I write is conditional on one document: the status certificate. It is the corporation’s own disclosure — its financial statements, its budget, the exact fee for the unit and what it covers, any arrears, lawsuits, or planned fee increases, and the reserve fund study that projects the building’s major repairs for decades ahead. A well-funded reserve means the roof is already being paid for, a little every month, by everyone who has lived there. A thin reserve fund plus a low fee is the classic condo trap: the price looks gentle and the special assessment arrives after closing.

We also read the corporation’s rules before you commit — pet limits, rental restrictions, parking and locker assignments, and whether the unit’s parking spot is owned, assigned or leased. None of this is fine print to skip; in a condo, the corporation is part of what you are buying — on a private street in Longfields, doubly so.

Questions Buyers Ask About Longfields Condos

What is the difference between a condo townhome and a freehold townhome? Ownership. A freehold townhome is yours wall-to-wall and lot-line to lot-line, with no monthly fee and no corporation. A condo townhome sits in a condominium corporation: you own your unit, the corporation owns and maintains the common elements, and you pay a monthly fee. On the private streets of Tayside Private and Via Verona the corporation also maintains the road. They can look identical to freehold from the street — the listing’s condominium status and the status certificate tell the truth.

Are condo fees in Longfields money thrown away? No — a fee is prepaid maintenance plus building insurance plus a forced savings plan (the reserve fund). The right question is never “how much is the fee?” but “what does the fee include, and is the reserve fund healthy?” That is answered by the status certificate, which we review on every purchase.

Why does Longfields carry more condos than the rest of Barrhaven? Because of when and how it was built. Longfields grew up in the 2000s around the Transitway, and its builders wove condo towns, stacked homes and apartment buildings between the freehold streets from the start — where older Barrhaven areas were built almost purely freehold. Density followed the transit, and the resale bench followed the density.

Can a condo corporation stop me from renting my unit or keeping a dog? Yes, within its registered rules — many corporations set minimum lease terms, and pet rules vary by corporation. This is exactly why the rules are read before the offer firms up, never after.

Can I rent a Longfields condo instead of buying? Yes — condo units across Barrhaven come up for lease, and renting first is a sound way to test the Transitway commute before you commit.
