First Things First: Confirming Your Legal Authority to Sell

A buyer's lawyer will ask: by what authority is this property being sold? If your parent is alive, the answer is a Continuing Power of Attorney for Property. If your parent has died, it is a Certificate of Appointment of Estate Trustee.

Selling While Your Parent Is Alive: The Continuing Power of Attorney for Property

Ontario splits power of attorney into two documents under the Substitute Decisions Act, 1992. A Continuing Power of Attorney for Property covers financial and legal decisions — banking, real estate, taxes. A Power of Attorney for Personal Care covers health, housing, and nutrition. One cannot do the other's job.

"Continuing" is the word that matters. When a family needs a power of attorney to sell a parent's house in Ontario, only the Continuing version keeps working after incapacity — a non-continuing (general) power of attorney ends the moment capacity is lost, which is exactly when most families need it most. These limits define every power of attorney sell parent's house Ontario case, so plan ahead while your parent is still capable, with an Ontario estates lawyer.

Five POA Limits Every Adult Child Should Know

1. A power of attorney ends at death. The document dies with your parent — authority passes to the estate trustee.

2. Your parent must have had capacity when signing. The Act sets a capacity test; a document signed after capacity was lost can be challenged.

3. It must be in writing with two witnesses. Verbal understandings with siblings carry no legal weight.

4. You cannot make a will for your parent. Your role is managing their affairs as they are.

5. You are a fiduciary. Act in your parent's best interests, keep their money separate, and keep records.

With no power of attorney and capacity already lost, the fallback is a Superior Court guardianship application — assessments, a management plan, a judge's order, ongoing oversight. Expensive and slow. Talk to your estate lawyer.

Selling After a Parent's Death: The Estate Trustee and Probate

When your parent dies, selling the house becomes the estate trustee's job — the person named in the will or appointed by the court. In Ontario, "probate" means applying to the Superior Court of Justice for a Certificate of Appointment of Estate Trustee; estates up to $150,000 can use a Small Estate Certificate.

For estate trustee selling property Ontario, the timing rule comes from ontario.ca: the certificate "should be obtained before anyone enters into an Agreement of Purchase and Sale." The property can be prepared and marketed, but the binding agreement should wait. A buyer's lawyer will insist on it.

When Probate Is (and Is Not) Required to Sell the House

A will does not automatically eliminate probate. Even with a valid will, the land registry and the buyer's lawyer will typically still require the certificate before a sale of real property closes — one of the most common and costly misunderstandings in selling inherited house Ontario probate situations.

The main exception is joint tenancy: the property passes to the survivor by right of survivorship, no probate required. If you are learning how to sell a deceased parent's house Ontario, start by confirming how title was held; your real estate lawyer can check in minutes.

Estate Administration Tax: What Probate Costs in Ontario

Ontario's Estate Administration Tax — the probate fee — is $0 on the first $50,000 of the estate's value, then $15 per $1,000 (or part) above $50,000, roughly 1.5%. These costs belong in every selling inherited house Ontario probate budget: values round up to the nearest thousand, paid as a deposit when filing. On a $1,000,000 Ottawa estate, that is $14,250. The estate trustee must also file an Estate Information Return within 180 days of receiving the certificate. Processing is often quoted as typically within 15 business days — do not plan your closing around that. General information; talk to your accountant and estate lawyer.

Capital Gains and the Principal Residence Exemption

When a parent dies, the Income Tax Act deems them to have disposed of each capital property at fair market value immediately before death, with the gain reported on their final tax return. There is no automatic "no tax when a parent dies" rule.

The relief is the principal residence exemption: when a deceased parent's home qualified as their principal residence for every year owned, the principal residence exemption deceased parent provision wipes out the gain. Since 1981, only one principal residence per family unit per year is allowed — a house plus a cottage meant choosing which got the exemption for which years. Since 2016, the sale must be reported even when fully exempt, or the exemption can be denied. Talk to your accountant about the designation history.

The Retirement-Residence Move: Tax Rules When the House Sits Empty or Gets Rented

If the house is rented, that change in use triggers a deemed disposition at fair market value — unless a section 45(2) election is filed: a signed letter attached to the tax return for the year of the change (no CRA form), preserving principal residence designation for up to four more years while rented — provided no capital cost allowance was ever claimed.

A vacant house triggers no deemed disposition, but later years generally cannot be designated since it is no longer "ordinarily inhabited." Never claim capital cost allowance on a building that might need principal residence years — that year's gain can never be sheltered, and recapture is fully taxed. The 45(2) election must be filed with the return for the year of the change — file on time, and ask your accountant. General information only, not tax advice.

Timing the Sale: Bridge Financing, Vacant-Home Insurance, and Carrying Costs

If a new purchase is involved, bridge financing is the standard tool: a short-term loan against the current home's equity for the new down payment, typically 6 to 12 months, repaid from sale proceeds via an Assignment of Proceeds of Sale. Rates run higher, and most lenders require a firm sale agreement first. Talk to your mortgage professional early.

While the house sits empty, insurance is urgent — and an estate trustee selling property Ontario should line up coverage and cleanout vendors before the certificate even arrives. Most Ontario policies cover unoccupied or vacant homes for only 30 to 60 days before limiting or excluding vandalism, theft, and water damage — the limit is set by your individual policy, not statute. Vacancy is a "material change in risk," so notify the insurer in writing or coverage can be voided. In Ottawa, the dominant winter risk is frozen pipes: keep the heat on and arrange regular check-ins. Carrying costs — taxes, utilities, insurance, snow clearing — continue whether anyone lives there or not.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my parent's house with a power of attorney?

Only with a Continuing Power of Attorney for Property, which covers real estate. A Power of Attorney for Personal Care does not. Questions about power of attorney sell parent's house Ontario eligibility come up in almost every first call — the answer is always the Continuing document, and only while your parent is alive. No power of attorney survives death — authority passes to the estate trustee.

How do I sell a deceased parent's house in Ontario?

How to sell a deceased parent's house Ontario starts with the estate trustee obtaining a Certificate of Appointment of Estate Trustee (probate) — before anyone enters into an Agreement of Purchase and Sale. Joint tenancy is the main exception, passing by right of survivorship.

Do I need probate if my parent left a will?

Usually yes, for selling real estate. A will names the estate trustee, but the land registry and the buyer's lawyer typically still require the court-issued certificate. Do not assume a will alone is enough — confirm with your estate lawyer.

Will I pay capital gains tax on an inherited house in Ontario?

The deceased is deemed to have disposed of the property at fair market value immediately before death, with the gain on their final return. If the home qualified as your parent's principal residence for every year owned, the principal residence exemption deceased parent provision wipes out the gain. Talk to your accountant about the designation history.

What does probate cost in Ontario?

The Estate Administration Tax is $0 on the first $50,000, then $15 per $1,000 (or part) above $50,000 — roughly 1.5%. On a $1,000,000 estate that is $14,250, paid as a deposit when filing, with an Estate Information Return due within 180 days. Talk to your lawyer and accountant.

Can the house be rented out while we wait to sell?

Yes, but renting triggers a change-in-use deemed disposition unless a section 45(2) election is filed with the return for the year of the change. It can preserve principal residence designation for up to four more years while rented, provided no capital cost allowance was ever claimed. A vacant house triggers no deemed disposition, but those years generally cannot be designated. Talk to your accountant first.
