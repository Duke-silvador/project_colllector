Metcalfe at a Glance

Why do buyers choose a village 30 minutes south of downtown over a closer suburb? The answer is the same one Greely gives — the lot — with a different flavour. Metcalfe offers estate lots Ottawa inside city limits: roughly half-acre village lots in the core, one-acre lots in Premwood Estates, multi-acre rural properties beyond, and working farms at the edges. What Metcalfe adds is the institutional weight of the old township seat: the high school is here, the arena is here, the fairgrounds are here, the pharmacy is here, and the 1891 township hall still works as the village's civic front door. Downtown is roughly a 30-minute drive, Greely ten minutes, Manotick's larger retail fifteen. It is Ottawa’s fair village — country in every practical sense, city on the tax bill.

Living in Metcalfe

Daily life in Metcalfe runs on two streets. Victoria Street is the heritage main street — older homes, the pharmacy, the pub, the churches and the township hall, on village lots of roughly half an acre. 8th Line Road is the working street — the schools, the fairgrounds, the community centre and arena strung along it. Around the core, the estate subdivisions carry the growth: Premwood Estates, in the village off Ginger Crescent and Metcalfe Farm Drive, is built around one-acre lots of custom homes beside McKendry Park's 14.5 acres; rural estate properties like the Maple Ridge area run to roughly 2.7 acres. Beyond them, the district is working farmland — the cash crops in the fair's prize classes (corn, soybeans, wheat, barley, hay) are the fields you drive past. Moving to Metcalfe Ontario means choosing that mix on purpose: a village small enough to stay itself, inside a city big enough to need it.

A Short History — Baker's Corners to Ottawa Village

Metcalfe’s first settler families — the McDonells and the Yorks — arrived in 1827, drawn by farmland and the white pine and oak; the crossroads where the township's first two roads met was known as Baker's Corners, and the Ontario Heritage Trust plaque at Victoria and Albert Streets marks the founding of Osgoode Township here. The township itself was named for William Osgoode, first Chief Justice of Upper Canada, and incorporated in 1850 — with Metcalfe as its municipal seat. The village was a stagecoach stop on the Bytown–Cornwall route, and by the 1860s it was a working post village: a few hundred people, general stores, trades, a division court at Victoria Hall, schools and three churches.

The Welcome to Metcalfe sign — the village entrance on the Bank Street corridor it grew up on.

The village was renamed Metcalfe in 1877, for Charles Theophilus Metcalfe, Governor General of Canada 1843–1846 (its earlier name is reported inconsistently — Hawley's Corners in some accounts, Osgoode in others — and this guide leaves that question to the township museum). It became a Police Village in 1898. Two transport stories shaped everything after: the railways bypassed Metcalfe — the Ottawa & Prescott line ran west through Osgoode Village, the Cornwall line east through Russell — which capped the village's growth against its rail-served neighbours; and the road took over, as King's Highway 31 — long called "Metcalfe Road" — ran through the village from 1927 until it was downloaded in 1998, today Ottawa Road 31 / Bank Street. On January 1, 2001, Osgoode Township amalgamated into the City of Ottawa, and Metcalfe became what it is now: an Ottawa village with a farm-township memory.

The Metcalfe Fair & Farmers' Market

The fair is the village’s signature, and it is older than Canada. The County of Russell Agricultural Society was formed in 1856 and rotated its fair among the townships before taking a permanent home in Metcalfe in 1861; renamed the Metcalfe Agricultural Society in 1912, it has run the fair here ever since — one of Eastern Ontario's major fall fairs, filling the 8th Line Road fairgrounds each fall with agricultural halls, show pavilions, the midway and the demolition-derby crowd. The grounds work all year: the Family Pavilion doubles as the curling club, and the Metcalfe & District Lions Club is based here.

The Metcalfe Fairgrounds, 2821 8th Line Road — the fair's permanent home since 1861, and the village's year-round gathering ground.

The fair’s 150-years wall at the fairgrounds — the fair began in 1856, so its 150th anniversary year was 2006; the fair story runs a full 170 years and counting.

One of the fair’s agricultural halls — the buildings that house the exhibits, the livestock and the home-craft classes every fall.

The same grounds host the second institution: the Metcalfe Farmers' Market — "Homegrown Handmade" since 1993 — runs Saturdays, 9 to 1, from mid-May to mid-October (pausing for fair weekend), and has been voted Best Farmers' Market at the Ottawa Awards six years straight. For buyers, the pair matter beyond the calendar: a village whose fair and market still draw the whole township is a village whose centre holds.

Wells & Septic — the Buyer-Critical Section

Every Metcalfe purchase starts with one question: where do the water and the wastewater come from? The answer, everywhere in the village and district, is a private well and a private septic system. There is no municipal water or sewer in Metcalfe — the City of Ottawa's 2025 septic impact assessment for a fairgrounds application states it directly: the village is not connected to municipal services, and the neighbouring properties are serviced by private wells and private sewage systems. Unlike its twin Greely, Metcalfe has no communal-servicing pocket; city limits do not mean city pipes here.

That is normal, workable, and non-negotiable homework. A well is a quantity-and-quality question — flow rate, potability, treatment equipment, records. A septic system is an age-and-capacity question — what was installed, when, and whether it suits the household you are bringing. A building lot is a feasibility question — what can be drilled, dug and approved on that specific land. The fairgrounds expansion file shows the same discipline at civic scale: even the fair's growth runs through a septic impact assessment. Get the answers in writing, before closing, not after — and budget the inspections before you firm up, on every Metcalfe purchase.

Schools — the High School Is Here

Schools are the organising spine for most Metcalfe families, and Metcalfe holds the trump card of the twin villages: the high school is in the village. Osgoode Township High School, 2800 8th Line Road — opened 1954, the shared public high school for seven villages.

  • Osgoode Township High School (OCDSB, Grades 9–12) — 2800 8th Line Road. Opened in 1954 under the Carleton Board and in the OCDSB since 1998, OTHS draws from Metcalfe, Greely, Osgoode, Kenmore, Vernon, Edwards, Carlsbad Springs and portions of Manotick — the twin-village pattern in daily form, with Greely's teenagers busing south to Metcalfe. Community, Faith & the 1891 Hall

The civic anchor is the building on the banner's street: Osgoode Township Hall, 8243 Victoria Street, built in 1891 to the design of Ottawa architect James Mather as the seat of Osgoode Township. It never retired — today it houses the City's Metcalfe Client Service Centre (open Tuesdays) and the Ottawa Public Library's Metcalfe branch, which began in the hall's back room in 1968 and moved back in after the old 8th Line branch was found to have major structural issues. One building, 135 years of the village's paperwork, books and business.

Osgoode Township Hall (1891), 8243 Victoria Street — now the Metcalfe Client Service Centre and the OPL Metcalfe branch.

The faith map is a village map. St. Catherine of Siena Roman Catholic Parish, 2718 8th Line Road — beside the school that carries the shorter name — holds Sunday Masses at 9, 11 and 7. Holy Trinity Anglican Church, 8140 Victoria Street, is the village's oldest congregation story: a first wooden church in 1856, the current building raised in 1897, and a parish that spans Metcalfe, Greely and Vernon. St. Andrew's United Church stands on 8th Line Road. Sport runs through the Metcalfe Community Centre and Larry Robinson Arena, 2785 8th Line Road — the rink renamed for Robinson in 1993, home of the Metcalfe Jets (NCJHL) and the Metcalfe & District Hockey Association, which serves Metcalfe, Greely, Vernon, Kenmore and Edwards. Hockey families should note the arena's horizon: a City renovation and addition is expected to run from spring 2026 to fall 2027 — plan around construction seasons, and expect a renewed building at the end of it.

Getting Around & Who Runs the Village

Metcalfe is a car village, and this guide will not pretend otherwise. Bank Street (Ottawa Road 31) is the spine — north through Greely and Findlay Creek to downtown in roughly 30 minutes; 8th Line and Victoria Street run the local trips; Marionville Road and Mitch Owens Road carry the farm-country drives east toward Russell and Embrun. There is no regular OC Transpo route — the City funds rural transportation partners for villages without regular service, so check octranspo.com for current options — and school busing, through the Ottawa Student Transportation Authority, is the exception that matters to families. Governance is the City of Ottawa's Ward 20, Osgoode, represented by Councillor Isabelle Skalski; provincially and federally the village sits in the Carleton ridings. The old township identity persists where it counts — in the ward name, the high school's name, the care centre and the hall on Victoria Street.

Metcalfe vs Greely — the Twin Villages

No dedicated comparison page ranks for Metcalfe vs Greely today, which is strange — it is the first question half of south-Ottawa buyers ask. The two villages are twins of the old Township of Osgoode: they share Osgoode Township High School (in Metcalfe), the Metcalfe & District Hockey Association, the fall-fair culture and the Bank Street corridor, and their residents split their weeks across both villages and Manotick without thinking about it. Greely's market Christmas events even run at the Greely Community Centre for a market whose home is Metcalfe's fairgrounds.

The differences are character and stock. Greely is the larger, faster-growing village — estate-lot subdivisions, lake communities and a golf-course community, with the grocery plaza (Foodland) on its main street, and prices driven up by lot size and custom builds. Metcalfe is the smaller, steadier village: the population centre counted 1,811 people in 2021, near-flat for twenty years, with the arena, the high school, the fairgrounds and the pharmacy in the village, a tighter heritage core on Victoria Street, and the more accessible end of the twin-village market. Is Metcalfe a good place to live? For buyers who want the fair-town core, the school in the village and land for the money — and who accept wells, septic and a car for every trip — it is the stronger answer of the two. Buyers who want lakes, new custom construction and a grocery store start in Greely; most end up touring both, which is why each village carries its own guide in the set below.

Questions Buyers Ask About Metcalfe

Is Metcalfe part of Ottawa?

Yes — Metcalfe has been a village inside the City of Ottawa since the January 1, 2001 amalgamation, when Osgoode Township joined the city. It sits in Ward 20, Osgoode, with the postal code K0A 2P0. What amalgamation did not change is the servicing: Metcalfe kept its rural character and its private wells and septic systems. Note this is the Ottawa village — not Adelaide-Metcalfe near Strathroy, and not Metcalfe Street downtown.

Does Metcalfe have municipal water and sewer?

No. The entire village and district run on private wells and septic systems — there is no municipal water or sewer anywhere in Metcalfe, a fact the City of Ottawa's own 2025 septic impact assessment states plainly. For a buyer that means well and septic homework on every purchase: flow and potability on the well, age and capacity on the septic, and inspections budgeted before you firm up.

How far is Metcalfe from downtown Ottawa?

About 30 minutes by car in free-flowing traffic, straight up Bank Street (Ottawa Road 31) through Greely, Findlay Creek and Riverside South. Greely is roughly 10 minutes north and Manotick roughly 15 minutes west. These are road-time estimates — test your own commute at your own hour before you buy.

Which schools serve Metcalfe?

Metcalfe Public School (OCDSB, JK to Grade 8) and St. Catherine School (OCSB, JK to Grade 6) are in the village, and Osgoode Township High School (OCDSB, Grades 9 to 12) — the shared public high school for Metcalfe, Greely, Osgoode, Kenmore, Vernon, Edwards, Carlsbad Springs and portions of Manotick — is here too. Catholic secondary students bus to St. Mark Catholic High School in Manotick, and Early French Immersion for the area runs through Castor Valley Elementary in Greely. Confirm any specific address in the board's school locator before you buy.

When is the Metcalfe Fair?

Every fall — late September into early October — at the fairgrounds on 8th Line Road. The fair has run since 1856, took its permanent Metcalfe home in 1861, and is run by the Metcalfe Agricultural Society; it is one of Eastern Ontario's major fall fairs. The same grounds host the Metcalfe Farmers' Market on Saturdays from mid-May to mid-October.

How big are the lots in Metcalfe?

Village homes sit on roughly half-acre lots in the core. The estate subdivisions run larger: Premwood Estates, in the village, is built around one-acre lots, and rural estate properties like the Maple Ridge area run to roughly 2.7 acres. Working farms complete the district. That range — village lot to farm acreage, all inside city limits — is the Metcalfe signature.

Is there public transit in Metcalfe?

There is no regular OC Transpo route serving the village, and buyers should plan around that honestly: Metcalfe is a car-dependent village. The City funds rural transportation partners for the villages without regular service — check octranspo.com for the current rural options. School busing runs separately, through the Ottawa Student Transportation Authority for eligible students.

Why is it called Metcalfe?

The village was renamed Metcalfe in 1877, honouring Charles Theophilus Metcalfe, Governor General of Canada from 1843 to 1846. What it was called before is reported inconsistently — some accounts say Hawley's Corners, others say Osgoode — so this guide states the renaming and the namesake, which are solid, and leaves the earlier name to the township museum to settle.
