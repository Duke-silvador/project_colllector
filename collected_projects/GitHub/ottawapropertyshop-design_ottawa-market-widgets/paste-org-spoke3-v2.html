Mortgage default insurance in Canada is the one charge on a home purchase that surprises almost every buyer: you pay the premium, but the policy does not protect you — it protects your lender. If you buy with less than 20 per cent down, this insurance is mandatory, one of three insurers will stand behind your mortgage, and you will likely never learn which one unless you ask. This guide explains who the three insurers are, who chooses between them, what the premium costs, and how default insurance differs from the other two insurances that sound almost the same.

Mortgage Default Insurance In Canada — The Short Answer

Who pays

The borrower pays the premium — usually added to the mortgage at closing. In Ontario, 8 per cent provincial sales tax on the premium is paid in cash at closing and cannot be added to the mortgage.

Who is protected

The lender. If the borrower defaults and the sale of the home does not cover the loan, the insurer pays the lender’s shortfall — and the insurer can then pursue the borrower for that amount.

Who chooses the insurer

The lender chooses — not the borrower. The three insurers charge essentially the same regulated premium schedule, so the choice rarely changes what you pay.

When it is required

Whenever a federally regulated lender lends more than 80 per cent of the price — a down payment under 20 per cent — on an eligible home priced under $1.5 million.

The Three Insurers Behind Mortgage Default Insurance In Canada

Exactly three organizations insure Canadian mortgages against borrower default, all under the same federal framework:

  • CMHC — the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation (cmhc-schl.gc.ca), a federal Crown corporation and the best-known of the three. CMHC also does housing research and programs — a separate job from its insurance arm.
  • Sagen — Sagen Mortgage Insurance Company Canada (sagen.ca), Canada’s largest private mortgage insurer, formerly Genworth Canada.
  • Canada Guaranty — Canada Guaranty Mortgage Insurance Company (canadaguaranty.ca), the third insurer, a private Canadian-owned company.

Why three doors that look identical? Because their underwriting appetites differ at the edges — property types, programs for self-employed and new-to-Canada borrowers, and turnaround. A decline from one insurer is not always the end of a purchase, which is one practical reason buyers work with a mortgage broker who can place a file with lenders dealing with all three. The brokers themselves are licensed in Ontario by FSRA — my earlier guide to Ontario’s mortgage-broker licensing crackdown covers that side of the money chain.

Who Chooses, Who Pays, Who Is Protected — The Three Answers Most Buyers Get Wrong

The lender chooses. File by file, your lender selects CMHC, Sagen or Canada Guaranty. You do not shop insurers, and on a standard file there is nothing to shop: the premium schedule is standardized across all three.

The borrower pays. The premium is a percentage of the mortgage amount, set by how much you put down: 4.00 per cent at 5 to 9.99 per cent down, 3.10 per cent at 10 to 14.99 per cent down, and 2.80 per cent at 15 to 19.99 per cent down (standard homeowner rates, amortization of 25 years or less). The premium is normally added to your mortgage — which means you pay interest on it over time — and in Ontario the 8 per cent PST on the premium is due in cash at closing.

The lender is protected. This is the sentence to remember. Default insurance guarantees the lender, not you. If you default and the home sells for less than you owe, the insurer makes the lender whole — and the insurer may then come after you for the shortfall. What the premium buys you is access: it is what makes a 5-per-cent-down purchase possible at a federally regulated lender at all, and insured borrowers typically qualify for lenders’ best rates, because the lender’s risk has been transferred.

The $1.5 Million Cap And The December 2024 Thirty-Year Rule

Two numbers frame every insured mortgage today, and both changed recently:

  • Price cap under $1.5 million. Mortgage default insurance is available only on homes priced under $1.5 million. At $1.5 million or more, no insurer will cover the loan — the buyer must put at least 20 per cent down. The cap rose from $1 million effective December 15, 2024.
  • Thirty-year amortizations since December 15, 2024. Insured mortgages were long capped at a 25-year amortization. Since December 15, 2024, first-time buyers (on any eligible insured purchase) and any buyer of a newly constructed home may amortize an insured mortgage over 30 years — lower monthly payments, more interest over the life of the loan. Choosing the longer amortization adds a premium surcharge of 0.20 per cent of the mortgage amount on top of the standard premium above.

The minimum down payment itself is unchanged: 5 per cent on the first $500,000 of the price, plus 10 per cent on the portion above $500,000.

Three Insurances, Three Different Jobs — Do Not Confuse Them

Default insurance

Protects the lender if the borrower defaults. Paid by the borrower, chosen by the lender. This guide. A full comparison is coming in a later guide in this series: The Three Insurances When You Buy A Home Default Title And Home.

Title insurance

Protects against title risks — defects, fraud, survey and registration problems. Arranged by your lawyer at closing for a one-time premium; a lender policy and an owner policy are two different things.

Home (property) insurance

Protects the property itself — fire, water damage, liability. Your lender requires it to be in place before funding your mortgage, and you choose that insurer yourself.

One name, three policies, three different protected parties. When a buyer tells me “I already have insurance,” my first question is always: which one?

The one-sentence version: With less than 20 per cent down, mortgage default insurance is mandatory — your lender picks one of CMHC, Sagen or Canada Guaranty, you pay the premium, and the policy protects the lender; that is the price of access to a small-down-payment purchase, and for most Ottawa buyers it is worth understanding rather than resenting.

Questions Buyers Ask About Mortgage Default Insurance In Canada

Does CMHC insurance protect me if I cannot pay?

No. It protects your lender. You pay the premium; the lender holds the protection; and after a default shortfall the insurer can pursue you for the amount it paid out.

Can I choose between CMHC, Sagen and Canada Guaranty — or shop for a cheaper premium?

No. Your lender chooses the insurer, and the standard premium schedule is essentially identical across all three. What you can compare is lenders’ rates and terms — and a broker’s access to lenders who deal with all three insurers.

How do I avoid the premium entirely?

Put 20 per cent or more down. At 80 per cent loan-to-value or less, no default insurance is required — though some lenders still insure low-ratio loans at their own cost for their own protection.

What changed on December 15, 2024?

Two things: the insured price cap rose from $1 million to $1.5 million, and 30-year insured amortizations became available to first-time buyers and buyers of newly built homes, with a 0.20 per cent premium surcharge for the longer amortization.

Is the premium a one-time cost?

Yes — a single premium at closing, usually added to your mortgage, plus Ontario’s 8 per cent PST on the premium paid in cash at closing. It is non-refundable, though insurers offer top-up and portability calculations when you refinance or move an insured mortgage.
