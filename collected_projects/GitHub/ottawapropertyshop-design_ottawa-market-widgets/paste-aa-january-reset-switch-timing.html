Two Clocks: Anniversary Year Versus Calendar Year

Your eXp cap runs on your personal anniversary year, while your market, your taxes and your business plan run on the calendar year — January is when the two clocks line up.

Every capped brokerage runs its cap on some cycle. Some use the calendar year. Some use a company fiscal year. eXp uses your anniversary date: if you join on January 12, your cap year runs January 12 to January 11, every year, and the $16,000 CAD cap — reached at $80,000 GCI on the 80/20 split — resets on that date.

Your business life, meanwhile, runs on the calendar. Your tax year ends December 31. Ottawa's market builds through the spring. Your goals, your marketing budget and your lead generation plan almost certainly run January to December. Join in January and your cap year sits within a few days of the calendar year for your entire career at the brokerage — one clean cycle to plan around. Join in September and you will forever be doing cap arithmetic across two calendar years, two tax years and two spring markets.

The Double-Cap Problem, Explained Plainly

If you leave one capped brokerage after paying into its cap and join another with a fresh cap in the same production year, you pay cap dollars twice for one year's work — timing the move to a cycle boundary avoids it.

Here is the trap. Suppose your current brokerage has a cap, your cycle there resets in the autumn, and you switch in the spring after already paying a large share of that cap. Your production at the old brokerage bought you a capped rate you will barely use. Then you arrive at your new brokerage with a brand-new $16,000 cap to fill — at eXp, another $80,000 of GCI at 80/20 before you reach 100% minus the post-cap per-deal fees of $285, then $75.

Nothing about either brokerage is dishonest here. It is simply two cycles overlapping on one year's income. The cost is real — potentially thousands of dollars of split paid twice on the same season's production — and it is entirely avoidable with a calendar.

  • Find your current brokerage's cap reset date and how much you have paid toward it this cycle

  • Map your pending and likely closings for the next 90 days — deals in flight usually close where they were listed, but confirm your agreement's terms

  • Choose a start date at the new brokerage that begins your fresh cap year right after the old cycle's value is used up

  • January is the natural landing spot for most agents because the old calendar-year picture is complete and the new cap year starts clean

What if my current brokerage has no cap at all?

Then there is no double-cap problem — an uncapped split stops costing you the day you leave, and every month you stay is another month at full split cost. For uncapped agents the timing question flips: it is not about avoiding overlap, it is about how many high-production months you want under a cap. Joining before your busiest season, rather than after it, is usually the better arithmetic.

Why January Specifically Works

A January move puts your full cap year ahead of you, your slowest administrative month behind the paperwork, and the entire spring market inside your new structure.

January has four practical advantages for an Ottawa agent. First, the reset alignment described above: your anniversary lands in January permanently. Second, January is administratively quiet — board transfers, licence paperwork, sign and lockbox changes, and client notification all land easier in the slowest month than in April. Third, the spring market is entirely ahead of you: the production that fills your cap comes from the strongest months, so you reach the cap sooner in your cap year and spend more of the year at the post-cap rate. Fourth, your numbers are fresh: you have just closed out a full calendar year, so the fee audit on your last 12 months is exact, and you can verify within one year whether the move saved what the arithmetic said it would.

Is January right even if I am mid-deal in January?

Usually, yes — a small number of deals in flight is normal at any date, and waiting for a mythical quiet moment with nothing pending can cost you a full season. What matters is reading your current agreement on how pending deals and listings are treated when you leave, and sequencing the move around your closings. That is a one-hour review, not a reason to lose a year.

Month-by-Month Timing Guidance

January is ideal, late autumn is the smart alternative, and mid-spring is the most expensive time to move — but a well-timed "wrong" month still beats another year on the wrong structure.

  • January to February: the clean start — full cap year, quiet admin, spring market ahead. The default recommendation

  • March to May: the costliest window — you move at peak production, pay transition friction during your best months, and start a cap year mid-stream

  • June to August: workable — production is strong but the administrative load is lighter than spring; watch cottage-season client availability for notifications

  • September to October: reasonable — the fall market is a second production peak, and your cap year will span two calendar years, which is a bookkeeping nuisance rather than a financial problem

  • November to December: the planning window — run your fee audit on the nearly complete year, sign and prepare, and start clean in January

Your Pre-Move Checklist, Whatever Month You Choose

Audit last year's fees, read your exit clauses, pick the date against both cap cycles, and notify clients like a professional — the move itself is a week of paperwork, not a leap.

  • Complete the 12-month fee audit so the decision is based on your real total cost

  • Read your current agreement for pending-deal, listing and tail-fee clauses

  • Confirm both brokerages' cap reset dates in writing

  • Plan client communication: a short, positive note — same agent, same phone number, new brokerage

  • Budget the known one-time costs at eXp: a $199 startup fee, then $139 per month, $29 per-deal broker review
