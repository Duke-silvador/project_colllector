Bank of Canada Raises Rates to 2.50 Percent: What It Means for Ottawa Buyers and Sellers

By Tomasz R. Witek | Real estate salesperson with eXp Realty, Brokerage

Ottawa mortgage rates are on the move again — this morning, Wednesday, October 28, the Bank of Canada raised its policy rate by a quarter of a percentage point, to 2.50 per cent. It is the Bank’s first increase since July 2023 and its first change of direction in a year, after holding at 2.25 per cent since October 2025. Yesterday I wrote about whether Ottawa buyers should buy before or after this decision — today the waiting is over, and this post is the follow-up: what the decision actually changes for Ottawa buyers and for sellers, in dollars, not headlines.

Here is the part that matters: a hike does not hit every mortgage the same way. Variable rates follow the Bank. Fixed rates follow the bond market. And the size of the change on a typical Ottawa payment is real but manageable — about $79 a month on the worked example below. Read the math first, then decide what it means for your move.

1. What the Bank of Canada Did This Morning

The Bank raised its target for the overnight rate by 25 basis points to 2.50 per cent, with the Bank Rate at 2.75 per cent and the deposit rate at 2.45 per cent. The reason, in the Bank’s own account, is inflation that has proven higher and more persistent than it expected in July: consumer price inflation has run around 3 per cent since April, the Bank now expects it to rise to about 3.5 per cent by the end of the year and to average about 3 per cent in 2027, before returning to the 2 per cent target in mid-2028. Governing Council said it raised the rate to reduce the risk that continuing high energy prices spread broadly into other prices.

The forward guidance matters as much as the move itself. The Bank signalled that “there could be a need to increase the policy rate further,” while also saying Council agreed to proceed carefully given the uncertainties. In plain terms: this may not be the last hike, and nobody should build a buying plan on the assumption that a cut is coming soon. The next scheduled decision is December 9, 2026.

2. What Happens to Variable Ottawa Mortgage Rates and Prime

If you hold or take a variable rate: your rate follows the Bank, through your lender’s prime rate. Big-bank prime has stood at 4.45 per cent; lenders typically announce a matching quarter-point rise to about 4.70 per cent on decision day, effective the next business day. On an adjustable-rate variable mortgage, your payment rises at the next payment date. On a fixed-payment variable mortgage, the payment stays the same and more of it goes to interest — check your remaining amortization and how close you are to your trigger point. Home equity lines of credit reprice the same way.

If you hold a fixed rate mid-term: nothing changes today. Your payment is locked until renewal. What can change is the rate you will be offered at renewal — and that is priced off the bond market, not off this morning’s announcement (section 4).

3. The Payment Math: What This Hike Costs on a $600,000 Mortgage

Feelings about rates get clearer with arithmetic. Take a $600,000 mortgage — a mortgage amount, not a house price — on a 25-year amortization, using the Canadian standard of semi-annual compounding. Ratehub’s rate table on the morning of October 28 listed the lowest five-year variable rate in Canada at 3.43 per cent. Run that mortgage before and after a quarter-point pass-through:

At 3.43 per cent, the payment is $2,973.50 a month. At 3.68 per cent — the same mortgage after today’s 0.25-point increase flows through — the payment is $3,052.91 a month. The decision itself is therefore worth about $79 a month on this mortgage, roughly $953 over a year. Real money, worth budgeting for. It is not, on its own, a reason to buy the wrong house or to lose the right one.

One honest caveat: 3.43 per cent is the lowest advertised insured variable rate, not every borrower’s rate. Your own variable rate is your lender’s prime minus (or plus) your personal discount — but the quarter-point change, and roughly the same $79-a-month step on a $600,000 balance, applies the same way whatever your starting rate is. If your balance is smaller, scale it down: very roughly, about $13 a month per $100,000 of mortgage for each quarter point.

4. The Fixed-Rate Scenario: Why Fixed Rates Do Not Move With Today’s Decision

Fixed Ottawa mortgage rates are priced mainly off the five-year Government of Canada bond yield, and bond investors trade on expectations — which is why fixed rates rose through the fall while the Bank was still holding, and why they may barely move this week even though the Bank has now hiked. Ratehub’s table on the morning of October 28 listed the lowest five-year fixed rate in Canada at 4.34 per cent. On the same $600,000, 25-year example, a mortgage locked at 4.34 per cent costs $3,267.68 a month — and that payment does not change at all when the Bank moves, for the whole five-year term.

That is the real fixed-versus-variable question after a hike: the variable example above still starts about $215 a month cheaper than the fixed example, but it now carries the Bank’s own warning that a further increase could follow, with the next decision on December 9. The fixed example costs more today and buys certainty. Neither answer is right for everyone — it depends on your buffer, your time horizon, and how a second $79 step would feel in your budget. Run both numbers before you choose, not after.

5. What This Means for Ottawa Buyers

Your rate hold just became valuable. If you were pre-approved with a rate hold before today — typically 90 to 120 days, depending on the lender — that held rate is a ceiling that protects you while you shop; confirm in writing exactly what was held and until when. If you do not have a hold and you are buying this fall or renewing before late winter, getting one this week is the single most useful move available to you, because the Bank has left the door open to another increase on December 9.

Your qualification shrank a little. The same quarter point that adds about $79 a month also reduces the mortgage you qualify for under the stress test. Buyers whose budget only worked at the old rate need a fresh pre-approval at today’s numbers before they write an offer — not after. And this is the same money chain my map of who is involved in buying a house in Ottawa lays out organization by organization: lender, insurer where applicable, lawyer, and the rest each play their part in sequence.

The “wait for cuts” plan is over for now. For two years the default advice was to wait for the Bank to cut. The Bank has now moved the other way and warned it could do so again. Buyers who find the right home at a payment that works at TODAY’s rate — with a buffer for one more step — are in a sound position. Buyers who need a cut to make the payment work are not waiting on a plan; they are waiting on a hope.

6. What This Means for Ottawa Sellers

Sellers do not need to reprice their homes because of one announcement — Ottawa houses for sale do not get cheaper or dearer by Friday. What changes is the buyer on the other side of the table. Fall buyers who were waiting for rate clarity now have it, and the direction is up, not down: some will move faster, before a possible December step; others will trim their budgets by roughly the amount their qualification just shrank. Well-priced homes that respect the new payment reality will meet the first group. Homes priced for last spring’s market will meet the second.

The sellers who do well after a hike are the ones who price to the payment buyers can actually carry, present the home properly, and stay negotiable on terms — closing date, inclusions, a clean file — that cost them little and matter a lot to a buyer stretching at a higher rate. That has been true in every rate environment I have worked in since 2005.

7. Why Your Street Still Matters More Than One Decision

The Bank of Canada sets one rate for the whole country. It does not know your street. A quarter point is worth about $79 a month on a $600,000 mortgage everywhere in Canada — but the difference between the right home and the wrong one in Greely, Manotick, Riverside South, Barrhaven, Findlay Creek, Kanata or Stittsville is worth far more, and it lasts far longer than any single rate decision. The home is the asset; the rate is a term of the loan. Buyers and sellers who keep that order straight tend to do well in either rate direction.

So, What Should You Do After the Hike?

Read the two lists honestly. If you are buying, the work this week is unglamorous and entirely in your control: confirm your rate hold, refresh your pre-approval at 2.50-per-cent-world numbers, and decide fixed versus variable with both payments in front of you. If you are selling, price to the buyer’s new payment, not to the headline. Either way, the decision is made, the numbers are knowable, and December 9 is the next date to circle — not a reason to freeze until then.
