The Short Answer

A quick possession home — also called an inventory home or a spec home — is a new home the builder has built, or nearly finished building, before a buyer was attached to it. You buy the finished product: the lot, the elevation and the finishes are already decided, and you close on the builder’s date, often 30 to 120 days after signing. In exchange for giving up the design choices, you get the three things a to-be-built purchase cannot offer: you walk through the actual home before you buy it, you skip the construction wait entirely, and you shop where the builder’s incentive money concentrates — because a finished home costs the builder money every month it stands. The home is still new in the eyes of the tax rules and the warranty: HST applies, the new-home rebates may apply, and Tarion coverage stands in full. Shop it like a new home, compare it like a resale home, and check the closing date twice — the builder’s dates are firmer than a to-be-built schedule.

Three Ways to Buy a Home That Is New

Start with the three lanes, because inventory only makes sense against the other two. A to-be-built home is bought from plans: you choose the lot, the elevation and the finishes, you attend the design studio appointments, and you wait — through permits, construction and the builder’s closing framework — for a home that exists, at signing, only on paper. A resale home is the opposite end: it exists completely, you walk through it before you offer, and someone has already lived in it, with everything that history carries. A quick possession home stands between them. Like a resale home, it can be walked through and judged as it stands. Like a to-be-built home, it has never been lived in, carries a new-home warranty, and is bought from the builder under a builder’s agreement — with a builder’s closing date and new-home tax treatment.

One more distinction matters, and it is the one buyers mix up at kitchen tables: a model home is a sales tool, staged to show what a community can look like; an inventory home is a product, built to be bought and lived in. Sometimes a model home eventually joins the inventory list when a community winds down — and then the staging question becomes a real one, because the finishes in a model were chosen to photograph well, which is exactly the walk-through discipline covered in Design Studio Upgrades Ottawa What Is Worth It: judge every finish by living with it, not by how it presents.

Why Builders Hold Inventory

Inventory is not an accident; it is a by-product of how communities are built. Builders build ahead of demand on purpose — a spec home started before its buyer appears keeps crews working, holds a street’s momentum, and gives late-arriving buyers something to walk through. Model homes join the list when a phase closes and the next sales office opens elsewhere. Cancelled deals return homes to inventory: a purchase that fell through at financing, at a life change, or at a closing that never happened leaves a finished or nearly finished home with no buyer attached. And year-end lot releases and phase transitions free up homes a builder would rather convert to a closing than carry — a finished home on the books is capital standing still, and standing capital is the one thing a builder’s operation is organized to avoid.

Read that list again from the buyer’s side and the leverage explains itself. Every home in the inventory column exists because the builder’s plan for it already changed once. The builder is not desperate — the home is priced, and the price is real — but the builder’s preference is a closing over a carrying cost, and preferences are what negotiations are made of.

The Advantages of Buying the Finished Home

The first advantage is certainty. A to-be-built purchase is a promise described in plans, samples and a model home’s suggestion of the result; an inventory purchase is an inspection. The kitchen you will cook in is the kitchen you are standing in. The light in the primary bedroom is the light at the hour you visit. The finishes that a to-be-built buyer chooses from a wall of samples are already installed, and the question the design studio asks — what will this look like? — is answered by opening the door.

The second advantage is the calendar. A quick possession closing is set by the builder and is usually near: often 30 to 120 days from signing, against the seasons or years a to-be-built contract can run. For a buyer coordinating a sale, a school year, a lease end or a relocation, that compression is worth money all by itself — and it removes the delay framework that hangs over every to-be-built closing date.

The third advantage is where the incentives sit. Builders put their incentive money where their carrying costs are, and carrying costs live in standing inventory. The categories are consistent — a price adjustment on the home, upgrades already included in the build at no added line on the statement, a rate buy-down or a closing-cost credit where the builder offers one — but the specific offer is set home by home and builder by builder, and anything promised in a headline has to survive into the agreement in writing. And through all of it, the home’s warranty position is a new home’s: Tarion enrolment stands, and the coverage periods and deadlines explained in Tarion Warranty Ottawa New Homes 1 2 7 Explained run for the buyer who closes, exactly as they would on a to-be-built purchase.

The Trade-Offs, Stated Plainly

The shortcut is real, and so is its price. The first trade-off is choice: the colours, the finishes, the structural options — the entire design studio menu — were decided by the builder before you arrived. If the kitchen is not the kitchen you would have chosen, the answer is the one you can see, not the one you can change. The second trade-off is the lot and the elevation: an inventory home stands where it stands, facing the way it faces, and no premium conversation moves it. The third is the closing date itself. A to-be-built date is a target inside a framework with notices and compensation rules; an inventory date is the builder’s date, set because the home is ready, and it is firmer — your financing, your movers and your own sale have to fit it, not the other way around.

The fourth trade-off is tax, and it is the one that surprises buyers who file inventory under resale in their heads: an inventory home is a new home, and new homes carry HST. The price on the builder’s sheet is not the whole arithmetic — the rebates that return part of that tax are the same ones the to-be-built buyer claims, worked through in the two rebate guides:

Where the Incentives Concentrate

Put the whole picture in one frame. A to-be-built home costs the builder nothing to hold — it is a contract and a schedule. A finished home costs taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance and the capital locked inside it, every month it stands. That asymmetry is why the incentive conversation around an inventory home starts further ahead than the same conversation around a future build: a price adjustment here, included upgrades there, a buy-down or a credit where the builder’s program offers one. None of it is owed to you, and none of it is uniform — it is set by the home, the builder and the month on the builder’s own calendar. What is uniform is the discipline: every incentive that matters ends up written into the agreement, and the ones that stay verbal stay worthless.

How to Shop the Inventory Lists

  • Ask for the list, not the advertisement. Every builder holds an inventory and quick possession list, and what is advertised is a selection from it. Ask each builder — and each community — for the complete list: the quiet entries are where a cancelled deal or a returned home is sitting.

  • Compare on total cost, not sticker price. An inventory home and a resale home in the same price band are not the same purchase: HST, the builder’s adjustments and the new-home closing lines itemized in New Construction Closing Costs Ontario Itemized belong on the inventory side of the sheet before the two are compared.

  • Walk the actual home with a finishes list. Establish what is included in the price and what was a design studio extra — in the model, upgraded finishes sell the community; in the inventory home, confirm which of those finishes are in the home you are buying and which were the model’s staging.

  • Pin the closing date, in writing. The exact date, and whether it can move at all. An inventory closing is the builder’s date; treat any flexibility as real only when the agreement says so.

  • Confirm the deposit and the enrolment. The deposit schedule on an inventory purchase is the builder’s, set in the agreement — usually shorter than the staged schedules decoded in New Construction Deposit Structures Ottawa Decoded, because closing is near. Six Checks Before You Sign

  • Walk the home, not the model. Buy the unit you stood in: its finishes, its lot, its light, its street. If any part of the conversation drifts to the model home down the road, bring it back to the address on the agreement.

  • Get the inclusions in writing. Every finish, appliance and upgrade that is part of the price, listed. A finished home’s contents are a fact — make the agreement recite the fact.

  • Confirm the closing date and its firmness. The builder’s date, whether it can move, and what your own sale, financing and movers have to do to meet it. The date is the deal’s spine.

  • Run the full new-home arithmetic. Price, HST, your rebate position, the builder’s adjustments and the closing statement — total cost, compared against the resale alternative on the same basis.

  • Verify the Tarion enrolment. The home’s enrolment and the warranty clocks that start at possession, confirmed before signing — not discovered at the pre-delivery inspection.

  • Put every incentive in the agreement. Price adjustments, included upgrades, buy-downs, credits — if it was offered across the desk, it gets written into the document, or it does not exist.

What Ottawa Clients Say

Questions Buyers Ask Me

What is a quick possession home?
A new home the builder has already built — or nearly finished — before a buyer was attached to it. Builders also call them inventory homes or spec homes. You buy the finished product from the builder: the lot, the elevation and the finishes are already decided, nobody has ever lived in the home, and you close on the builder’s date rather than waiting through a construction schedule.

How fast can I move into an inventory home?
On the builder’s closing date — often 30 to 120 days from signing, because the home is finished or close to it. That date is firmer than a to-be-built closing target: it is set because the home is ready, so your financing, your own sale and your movers have to be planned against it from the day you sign.

Is a quick possession home still covered by Tarion?
Yes. An inventory home is a new home, and Tarion coverage applies in full: the home is enrolled, and the warranty periods run from possession for the buyer who closes. Confirm the enrolment before you sign, and treat the pre-delivery inspection with the same care as on a to-be-built purchase — the clocks start at closing either way.

Do I pay HST on an inventory home?
Yes. Quick possession does not change the home’s tax status: it is a new home, bought from the builder, and HST applies to the price. The new-home rebates may return part of that tax on the same rules as any new-home purchase — the first-time-buyer pair and Ontario’s full-rebate window are tested on your facts and on your agreement’s signing date.

Can I choose the finishes in a quick possession home?
No — that is the trade at the centre of the lane. The colours, finishes and structural options were chosen by the builder before the home was offered, and they are already installed. What you walk through is what you get, which is also the advantage: there is no sample wall and no guesswork about how a selection will look at full size.

Are inventory homes cheaper than to-be-built homes?
Not automatically, and no headline should be taken at face value either way. The price is the builder’s price for that home. The leverage sits beside the price — in the incentives that concentrate on standing inventory, and in a total-cost comparison against the to-be-built equivalent and the resale alternative, run on the same basis with HST and closing costs included.

Why would a builder put an incentive on an inventory home?
Because a finished home costs the builder money every month it stands — taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance and the capital locked inside it. A to-be-built contract costs nothing to hold. That is why price adjustments, included upgrades, rate buy-downs and closing credits, where they are offered at all, gather on the inventory list. The specific offer is set home by home, and only what lands in the agreement counts.

Is buying an inventory home an assignment sale?
No. An assignment sale is the resale of a purchase contract from one buyer to another, under the builder’s consent rules. An inventory purchase is a direct sale: a fresh agreement between you and the builder for a home the builder owns. The assignment gates do not apply, because no contract is changing hands — the home is.

What deposit does a quick possession home need?
The builder’s agreement sets it. Because closing is near, the schedule is usually shorter and the timeline compressed next to a to-be-built purchase, where deposits are staged across the construction period. Read the exact schedule in the agreement before signing — on a 30 to 120 day runway, an installment date arrives quickly.

What should I check before signing for an inventory home?
Five things: the exact closing date and whether it can move; the inclusions list, so the finishes and upgrades in the price are written down; what was a model or staging extra, so you know what is not included; the home’s Tarion enrolment; and the total cost — price, HST, your rebate position and the closing statement — compared against your alternatives on the same basis.
