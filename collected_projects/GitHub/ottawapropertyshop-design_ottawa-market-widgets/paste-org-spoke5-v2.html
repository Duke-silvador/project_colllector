What does title insurance cover in Ontario? It is the one line on your closing statement almost nobody can explain — a few hundred dollars, paid once, for something called “title insurance,” arranged by your lawyer in the final days of buying and selling real estate in Ottawa. Here is the part that matters: there are two different policies hiding behind that one line. One protects your lender. The other protects you. They are not the same policy, they do not cover the same person, and having the first does not mean you have the second. This guide explains both, what title insurance covers, what it does not, what it costs, and the questions to ask before closing day.

Title Insurance In Ontario — The Short Answer

What is it?

An insurance policy that protects property owners and their lenders against losses related to the property’s title or ownership — in the words of Ontario’s insurance regulator, the Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA). Unlike home insurance, it covers problems that already existed on closing day but had not been discovered — plus title fraud that happens after you buy.

Two policies, not one

A lender policy (also called a loan policy) protects only the mortgage lender. An owner policy protects you, the buyer, for as long as you own the home. Seeing “title insurance” in your closing documents does not tell you which one you have.

Is it mandatory in Ontario?

No. FSRA states it plainly: title insurance is not mandatory in Ontario. What is common is different — most lenders require a lender policy as a condition of the mortgage. The owner policy is your choice, and Ontario’s Law Society rules require your lawyer to explain title insurance and the option of closing with or without it.

What does it cost?

A one-time premium paid at closing — no monthly or annual renewals. Published Canadian guidance puts a residential purchase policy at roughly $250 to $500; an Ontario law firm’s guide puts an owner-and-lender policy at roughly $300 to $1,000 depending on the property’s value. The premium is based on property value, per First Canadian Title (FCT).

Owner Policy vs Lender Policy — Two Policies, Two Different Jobs

Almost every misunderstanding about title insurance starts here, so take the two policies one at a time. When a purchase closes with a mortgage, the lender almost always requires a lender policy as a condition of lending. It protects the lender’s interest in the property — the mortgage — against title problems: a prior lien that was never discharged, a defect that threatens the lender’s priority, a fraud that wipes out the security. The amount of a lender policy is the mortgage amount, and because it tracks the loan, its coverage shrinks as the balance is paid down and ends when the mortgage does.

The owner policy is the separate policy that protects the buyer. Its amount is usually based on the purchase price, and it stays in force for as long as you — or your heirs — own the property. It is the policy that responds when a covered problem costs you money: the previous owner’s unpaid taxes now charged against your home, a deck the city orders removed because it was built without a permit, a forged transfer registered against your title years after you moved in.

FCT, the largest of the providers below, warns about the trap in its own words: a homeowner may think they are properly covered because they see title insurance in their closing documents, when what is actually there is only the loan policy. If you remember one thing from this guide, make it this: ask your lawyer which policies you are getting, by name.

What Does Title Insurance Cover In Ontario

A residential owner policy covers losses from title and off-title problems that existed at closing but were not discovered, and most title fraud that occurs after you buy. The standard coverage, as FSRA and the providers describe it, includes:

  • Unknown title defects — errors and defects in the title itself: a mistake in the public record, a prior interest that was never properly discharged, a break in the chain of ownership that surfaces years later.

  • Title fraud and forgery — someone forging your signature to transfer your home or register a fraudulent mortgage against it. This is the coverage no search can give you, because the fraud happens after closing. FSRA also notes the policies insure the “gap” between the moment your purchase finalizes and registration completing in Ontario’s land registration system.

  • Survey errors and encroachments — problems an up-to-date survey would have shown: a structure sitting over the lot line, a neighbour’s building encroaching on your land, an error in an existing survey. FSRA lists this as survey coverage, and notes it is acceptable to most lenders as an alternative to a new survey.

  • Zoning and municipal non-compliance, and outstanding work orders — a homeowner policy protects you against loss from an outstanding work order, and against being forced to remove or fix a structure that does not comply with zoning or municipal requirements, per FCT’s homeowner policy summary.

  • Work built without a permit — with a precise limit worth understanding. FCT explains it directly: the policy does not cover the structure itself; it covers the loss when a municipal authority requires the removal or repair of something a previous owner built or modified without a permit, including compensation for lost property value from the compliance work. FCT’s own claim story is a deck, deck addition and finished basement built without permits, where the city’s compliance demands ran to over $50,000 — and the policy covered the cost of the forced removal.

  • The previous owner’s unpaid taxes and utilities — if the seller left property tax or utility accounts in arrears, a homeowner policy can cover the outstanding amounts now charged against your property, per FCT.

  • The legal costs of defending your title — FSRA lists legal coverage: the policy pays for most legal expenses involved in defending your home’s title. If someone challenges your ownership, the insurer defends or settles the claim — you are not funding that fight alone.

  • Your lawyer’s legal services (TitlePLUS policies) — a TitlePLUS policy, described in the providers section below, also insures the legal services your lawyer provided on the transaction — if the lawyer made a mistake, the policy responds. TitlePLUS describes this legal services coverage as included in its Ontario residential purchase policies at no extra charge.

One discipline applies to the whole list: you must be able to show an actual loss, and every policy carries its own limit, effective date, exclusions and exceptions. Coverage is broad, but it is a contract, not a promise that nothing can go wrong.

What Title Insurance Does Not Cover

The exclusions matter as much as the coverage, and the most common confusion is the simplest one: title insurance is not home insurance. FCT draws the line in its own guide — title insurance does not cover flood damage, the theft of items from your property, or medical and accident liability. Those belong to your home insurance policy, which protects the physical property. Title insurance protects your ownership of it. You need both, for different risks.

  • Physical damage to the home — mould behind a wall, a leaking roof, worn-out anything. FCT’s example is exact: discovering mould during a renovation is a home insurance conversation, not a title claim — unless a covered title risk is also in play.

  • Defects you already knew about — problems disclosed or known to you before the policy was issued are the standard first exclusion in Ontario policies.

  • Problems you create yourself — a zoning violation or encroachment created by your own work after closing is yours, not the insurer’s.

  • Things outside the public record that only a new survey or inspection would reveal going forward — the policy insures the title risk; it does not walk your lot lines or inspect the building. That is why the survey section below matters.

  • Environmental hazards and matters the policy names as exceptions — soil contamination, and any risk your specific policy schedules as an exception, sit outside standard coverage. This is why reading your own policy’s exception list — not a brochure — is the buyer’s job.

And one more limit, stated plainly: title insurance does not fix a problem in advance or guarantee it away. When a covered problem surfaces, the insurer investigates the claim and either fixes it — paying to resolve the defect — or compensates you for the loss. It is a financial safety net under your ownership, not a certificate that the title was perfect.

What Does Title Insurance Cost In Ontario

The premium is paid once, at closing, and the policy never renews — there is no monthly or annual bill for as long as the coverage lasts. FCT states the pricing basis plainly: premiums are based on property value and vary by province. For the range, two published sources bracket it: RATESDOTCA’s consumer guide puts title insurance at generally a one-time fee of about $250 to $500, and an Ontario law firm’s detailed guide (Ownright) puts a typical Ontario residential owner-and-lender policy at roughly $300 to $1,000, with published residential rate-sheet reference points of about $440 for a $500,000 home and about $715 for a $750,000 home. In plain terms: a few hundred dollars on a typical Ottawa purchase, once, for coverage that lasts the whole time you own the home.

Two cost notes complete the picture. First, your lawyer orders the policy as part of closing — you do not shop for it the way you shop for home insurance, though you can ask which insurer your lawyer uses and why. Second, you are not limited to buying at purchase: existing owners can obtain a policy later. FCT invites homeowners who already own to order a policy at any time, and TitlePLUS sells an Existing Owner policy for exactly that situation — worth knowing if you bought years ago without one, because a paid-off mortgage is precisely when a home can look attractive to a fraudster.

The Four Providers You Will Meet In Ontario

Four insurers write the great majority of Ontario residential title insurance. Your lawyer typically orders the policy and selects the insurer based on the file — but the names on your closing statement will be one of these:

  • FCT — First Canadian Title (fct.ca). The largest title insurer in Canada, issuing separate owner and lender policies, with the homeowner coverage described throughout this guide.

  • Chicago Title Insurance Company Canada — the Canadian arm of a long-established title insurer, issuing its own residential owner’s policy alongside lender coverage.

  • Stewart Title Guaranty Company — the Canadian title insurer behind the “Gold” owner policy line, again with separate owner and lender policies.

  • TitlePLUS — LawPRO (titleplus.ca). The all-Canadian product of the Lawyers’ Professional Indemnity Company — LawPRO, the insurer owned by the Law Society of Ontario that also provides Ontario lawyers’ mandatory professional liability insurance. Its distinguishing feature is the legal services coverage built into the policy: the work your lawyer did on the transaction is insured along with the title.

For most buyers the practical difference between providers is narrow — the coverage families are similar, and your lawyer’s recommendation carries real weight. The question that matters is not which logo is on the policy. It is whether the policy in your file is the one that protects you.

Title Insurance vs A Survey — They Answer Different Questions

Buyers often meet these two in the same conversation, because title insurance can stand in for a survey at closing. FSRA describes the mechanism: survey coverage in a title policy may eliminate the need for a new up-to-date survey of your property, and it is acceptable to most lenders as an alternative to a survey. TitlePLUS makes the same point from the savings side — a policy can save money by eliminating certain searches and the need for an up-to-date survey. A new survey in Ontario costs considerably more than the policy, and on a standard city lot with a clean history, the trade is usually sensible.

But hear the difference precisely, because the two products answer different questions. A survey — a Surveyor’s Real Property Report prepared by a licensed Ontario land surveyor — shows you the property: the lot lines, the easements, where the house, the fence, the driveway and the deck actually sit against them. It reveals a problem before it is yours. Title insurance does not show you anything. It pays if a covered problem costs you money after closing. Insurance compensates; a survey informs. The hub guide in this series puts it in one line: a licensed surveyor’s plan answers the boundary questions title insurance only insures over — the two are complements, not substitutes.

So when is the survey still worth ordering? When the lot itself is part of the decision: a rural property where the lines are genuinely uncertain, an older home where additions and fences have crept over decades, a planned renovation, pool or addition that will need the lines proven, or any property where you can already see a fence or a shed sitting where it may not belong. In those cases, buy the information and the insurance. How Title Insurance Works With Your Lawyer’s Opinion On Title

Title insurance did not replace the real estate lawyer — in Ontario, it cannot. Traditionally, your lawyer searched the title and the off-title records (zoning, work orders, tax and utility status, and more) and then delivered an opinion on title: the lawyer’s professional certification that you were getting good title. The Ontario Bar Association’s and Law Society’s home-buying guide is candid about the limit of any opinion: no lawyer can completely assure a purchaser that there is no error in government records, that no undisclosed claim exists against the property, or that a previous owner’s signature was genuine and no fraud occurred. Those gaps are exactly what title insurance was built to fill.

Ontario regulation keeps the lawyer at the centre of it: a policy of title insurance cannot be issued without an opinion from a lawyer who is not employed by the insurer, so the lawyer still searches title, still advises you, and gives an opinion to the insurer in a purchase where a policy is obtained. And under Law Society rules, a lawyer acting for a purchaser must inform the client about title insurance — its advantages, conditions and limitations — and offer the choice of closing with or without it. When you sign at closing, that conversation is a required stop, not a sales pitch.

What changes with a policy in place is the search list and the remedy. Because the insurer takes on the covered risks, the lawyer need not complete every off-title search that a traditional opinion once required — a saving in time and disbursements that offsets part of the premium. And if a covered problem appears later, your remedy is a claim against a policy with a duty to defend your title, rather than an argument about whether your lawyer was negligent years ago. With a TitlePLUS policy, even that last gap closes: the lawyer’s legal services themselves are insured under the policy.

The Questions To Ask Your Lawyer Before Closing

Title insurance is arranged in the busiest week of the purchase, which is why it so often goes unexamined. Five questions, asked at the signing appointment, fix that:

  • “Am I getting an owner policy, or only the lender policy?” The single most important question in this guide. If the answer is lender-only, ask what adding the owner policy costs — on most purchases it is a modest difference on the same premium.

  • “Which insurer is issuing it, and what is my policy limit?” FCT, Chicago Title, Stewart Title or TitlePLUS — and a limit that should reflect your purchase price.

  • “Are there any exceptions or exclusions on my policy?” Policies can carry property-specific exceptions. You want to hear them before closing, not during a claim.

  • “Do I get a copy of the actual policy?” Brochures summarize; the policy governs. Keep it with your closing documents for as long as you own the home.

  • “If I already owned my home, could I still buy this?” The answer is yes — existing-owner policies exist — and asking it tells you whether the person across the desk knows the product they just sold you.

None of this makes title insurance a substitute for the rest of due diligence. It sits alongside the home inspection from the previous guide in this series and the deposit and closing-cost guides still to come — one layer of protection, doing one specific job, inside a purchase that needs several.

The one-sentence version: Title insurance is a one-time-premium policy that protects your ownership against title defects, survey problems, zoning surprises and fraud — but only the owner policy protects you; the lender policy on your closing statement protects your bank, so ask your lawyer, by name, which one you are paying for.

Questions Ottawa Home Buyers Ask About Title Insurance In Ontario

What does title insurance cover in Ontario?

Losses tied to your property’s title that existed at closing but were not discovered — unknown title defects, errors in surveys and public records, encroachments, zoning and municipal non-compliance, outstanding work orders, the previous owner’s tax and utility arrears — plus most title fraud committed after you buy, and the legal costs of defending your title. The covered list is set out in full in this guide.

Is title insurance mandatory in Ontario?

No. Ontario’s regulator, FSRA, states that title insurance is not mandatory in Ontario. What is mandatory in practice is narrower: most mortgage lenders require a lender policy as a condition of the loan. The owner policy that protects you is a choice — one your lawyer is required to explain to you.

What is the difference between an owner policy and a lender policy?

The lender policy protects only the lender, covers the mortgage amount, declines as the balance is paid down, and ends with the mortgage. The owner policy protects you, is usually set at the purchase price, and lasts as long as you or your heirs own the property. They are separate policies doing separate jobs — and only one of them has your name on it.

How much does title insurance cost in Ontario?

A one-time premium at closing, never renewed. Published guidance puts a residential purchase policy at roughly $250 to $500 (RATESDOTCA), with an Ontario owner-and-lender policy typically running $300 to $1,000 depending on the property’s value (Ownright). The premium is based on property value, per FCT.

Does title insurance replace a survey?

For the lender’s purposes, usually — FSRA notes title insurance’s survey coverage is acceptable to most lenders as an alternative to a new survey. For your purposes, they answer different questions: a survey shows you where the lot lines and structures actually are; title insurance pays if a covered problem costs you money. On rural lots, older properties and anything you plan to build on, the survey is still worth ordering.

Does title insurance cover fraud that happens after I buy?

Yes — that is one of its defining features. A forged transfer or a fraudulent mortgage registered against your title after closing is a covered risk under an owner policy, which is precisely the risk a closing-day title search can never catch.

Is title insurance the same as home insurance?

No. Home insurance protects the physical property against fire, flood, theft and liability. Title insurance protects your ownership against title defects, fraud, survey and zoning problems. FCT puts it plainly: you need both, because neither does the other’s job.

I already own my home. Can I still get title insurance?

Yes. FCT accepts orders from existing homeowners at any time, TitlePLUS sells an Existing Owner policy, and FSRA suggests current owners consider it too. It can matter most after a mortgage is paid off, when nothing stands between a fraudster and your title but the policy.

Who chooses the title insurance provider?

Usually your real estate lawyer, who orders the policy as part of closing and selects the insurer — FCT, Chicago Title, Stewart Title or TitlePLUS — based on the file. You can always ask which insurer is being used and why; the choice that matters more is the owner policy itself.

Does title insurance replace my lawyer’s opinion on title?

No — it works with it. Ontario rules do not allow a title policy to be issued without a lawyer’s opinion, your lawyer still searches title and advises you, and the policy then backs the result with insurance, including coverage for the risks no lawyer can certify away, like a forgery in the chain of title.
