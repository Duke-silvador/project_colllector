The Short Answer

Ottawa school catchments are set by each school board, program track by program track — English, Early French Immersion, and special programs can each carry a different line for the same school. Every Ottawa board publishes its official boundary maps and a locator for its own schools — the OCDSB’s are its secondary boundary index and elementary boundary index, and its School Locator, listed as up to date for the 2026–2027 school year, answers for one exact address at a time — and each guide in this series links its own board’s sources the same way. This series takes 23 high-demand Ottawa catchments — five in Kanata, four in Barrhaven, one in Beacon Hill / Gloucester, four downtown, and nine in Orléans, across the English public (OCDSB), English Catholic (OCSB), French Catholic (CECCE) and French public (CEPEO) boards — and builds each one a full homes-for-sale guide from its official line.

How a Catchment Search Actually Works

A catchment (the board calls it an attendance boundary) is the area a school draws its students from. Four things about it matter to a buyer:

  • The board decides, not the map. A line that looks obvious — the school at the end of the street — is not evidence. The board’s locator result on the exact address is.

  • Every track has its own line. English and Immersion entries are published separately on the OCDSB index, and a program like Colonel By’s International Baccalaureate carries a third, separate boundary.

  • Every board has its own lines. The Catholic and French-language boards draw from the same streets differently. An OCDSB answer says nothing about the school across the road run by another board.

  • Lines move. Growth, new schools and enrolment pressure all trigger reviews — which is why this series re-checks its catchments every September.

Catchment boundaries in this series are drawn from each board’s official boundary sources — for the OCDSB catchments, the board’s official boundary indexes, retrieved November 2, 2026. School assignment is the board’s decision — verify your exact address with the board’s school locator before you firm up an offer.

The 23 Catchments, Zone by Zone

Kanata — five catchments. Earl of March Secondary School is the zone’s English anchor (Fraser Institute rating 8.6 out of 10, #39 of 747 in the 2025 report card; its OCDSB English and Immersion boundary entry was updated July 9, 2025). The two elementary feeders flow into it: W. Erskine Johnston Public School (9.7 out of 10, #42 in Ontario) and Stephen Leacock Public School (9.4 out of 10, #67 in Ontario). The French boards complete the zone: Paul-Desmarais (CECCE) is the designated French Catholic secondary for Kanata + Stittsville and the best-rated French secondary serving Kanata at 7.5 out of 10 (#142 of 747), and Maurice-Lapointe (CEPEO), the only French public secondary in Kanata, rates 6.8 — with a five-year band of 7.7 that tells its longer story. A family with a child in either elementary and a teenager at Earl of March is shopping one connected zone — so the series builds it as one.

Colonel By — the prestige catchment. Colonel By Secondary School serves Beacon Hill and Gloucester, carries a Fraser rating of 8.9 out of 10 (#23 of 747 in the 2025 report card), and is the strongest single school-search magnet in the city. Its spoke treats the International Baccalaureate boundary as what it is — a separate line, published separately on the OCDSB index (both entries updated January 10, 2025) — never as a line assumed to match the English and Immersion one.

Barrhaven — four catchments. John McCrae Secondary School (7.2 out of 10, #178 of 747) and Longfields Davidson Heights Secondary School (7.0 out of 10, #220 of 747) — both OCDSB English and Immersion entries updated January 10, 2025 — are the two secondary catchments Barrhaven families weigh against each other, the pair behind the Wazoson and Cambrian Road questions. Both are rated in the Fraser Institute’s 2025 report card, and each spoke publishes the full rating picture from its own verified sources. The French boards sit inside the same suburb: Pierre-Savard (CECCE, 7.1 out of 10, #201 of 747) stands on Longfields Drive in Barrhaven itself — with a split to state honestly: part of Davidson Heights is assigned to Franco-Ouest in Nepean, and the Pierre-Savard spoke sets that line out from the board’s own locator — and Pierre-de-Blois (CEPEO, 6.4 out of 10, #326 of 747) opened in September 2021 on Chapman Mills Drive with an IB programme.

Downtown — four catchments, two of them gifted magnets. Lisgar Collegiate Institute, on Lisgar Street just south of Parliament Hill, is the board’s designated English gifted site for a long list of central and east-end home schools — its congregated gifted classes have run since 1957, the first of their kind in Ontario — and it offers Advanced Placement courses including the AP Capstone diploma. Glebe Collegiate Institute, the OCDSB’s largest secondary school at about 1,500 students, is the designated French Immersion gifted site. A gifted placement is not the same thing as a home catchment, and each downtown spoke states the two lines separately, from the board’s own index (both combined English and Immersion entries updated January 10, 2025). Their Fraser ratings sit close together: Lisgar 8.1 out of 10 (#79 of 747) and Glebe 7.5 out of 10 (#142 of 747) in the 2025 report card. The French boards hold the other two downtown catchments: De La Salle (CEPEO), in Lowertown, is home to the Centre d’excellence artistique de l’Ontario arts magnet and the CEPEO gifted centre (6.3 out of 10 in the 2025 report card, five-year band 7.1), and Saint-Jean-Baptiste (CECCE), on avenue Empress at LeBreton, opened in September 2024 and grows a grade per year — too new for a Fraser rating, and this series prints it as unrated rather than inventing one.

Orléans — nine catchments, four boards. Orléans is the one Ottawa community where all four boards run a full secondary offer, and the top of its table is French: Garneau (CECCE) is the highest-rated secondary serving Orléans at 7.9 out of 10 (#95 of 747), with Mer Bleue (7.6, #130 of 747) and Béatrice-Desloges (7.5, #142 of 747) behind it. The English Catholic pair is St. Matthew (6.9, #238 of 747) and St. Peter (6.5, #311 of 747); the French public pair is Louis-Riel (6.6, #294 of 747, from its Blackburn Hamlet campus) and Gisèle-Lalonde (6.5, #311 of 747); and the English public pair is Sir Wilfrid Laurier (6.1, #367 of 747) and Cairine Wilson (5.9, #401 of 747 — with a five-year band of 6.8 that its spoke prints beside the current-year figure). Five of the nine Orléans spokes are French-board schools, and each of those publishes with a French-language version.

How Each Spoke Is Verified Before It Publishes

Every guide in this series passes the same three steps, in order, before it publishes:

  • Boundary source saved. The school’s official boundary entry from its own board, with its retrieval date, filed with the spoke.

  • Cross-checked two ways. The board’s boundary entry is read against the school’s own site and its transportation source. Where the sources disagree, the guide says so on the page — it never picks one quietly.

  • Three addresses checked. One deep inside, one at the edge, one just outside — run through the board’s school locator. The spoke publishes only if the boundary and the locator agree on all three.

Questions Families Ask First

Does buying a home inside a catchment guarantee my child a place at that school?
No. School assignment is always the school board’s decision, boundaries can change, and programs fill. A catchment line is a strong starting point for a search — never a guarantee. Confirm the schools assigned to the exact address with the board’s school locator, and directly with any other school you are considering, before you firm up an offer.

How do I check which school an Ottawa address belongs to?
Use the Ottawa-Carleton District School Board’s official School Locator, which the board lists as up to date for the 2026–2027 school year. Enter the exact address — not just the street or the neighbourhood — because a boundary can run along a street and put one side in a different catchment from the other.

Are the English and French Immersion boundaries for the same school the same line?
Not necessarily. The OCDSB publishes separate boundary entries by program track, and they do not have to match. Colonel By Secondary School is the clearest example in this series: its International Baccalaureate program has its own attendance boundary, separate from its English and Immersion boundary. Each spoke in this series states the track it covers, from the board’s own index.

What about Catholic or French-language schools at the same address?
Different boards draw different lines — and this series covers all four Ottawa boards: the OCDSB (English public), the Ottawa Catholic School Board, the CECCE (French Catholic) and the CEPEO (French public). The same address can fall in a different zone for each board, and every guide in this series states its board and links that board’s own boundary and locator sources. Whatever board your family is considering, verify the exact address with that board.

When will each catchment guide in this series be published?
One at a time, and only after its verification is complete: the official boundary saved, the boundary cross-checked against the school’s own sources, and sample addresses checked against the board’s locator. This hub links all 23 guides from day one — each link opens its guide the day that guide publishes.

Do school catchment boundaries change?
Yes. Boards review boundaries as neighbourhoods grow and schools open, fill, or close. That is why every page in this series names the board source it was drawn from, why the Shortlist carries a September re-check date, and why the verification step — the board’s locator, on the exact address — belongs in every purchase, every time.

Why does a home on my street belong to a different catchment than the school at the end of it?
Because catchment lines are drawn by the board to balance enrolment across schools, not to follow what looks natural on a map. A line can split a neighbourhood, run down the middle of a street, or send one pocket of homes to a school that is not the closest building. Proximity is a hint; the locator result on the exact address is the answer.
