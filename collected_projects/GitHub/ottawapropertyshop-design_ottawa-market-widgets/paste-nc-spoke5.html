Layer One: Your Deposit Lives in Trust

Start with the rule everything else hangs on. Under Ontario's Condominium Act, the deposit you give a condo builder must be placed in trust — Tarion's own information sheet for new condominiums puts it plainly: the money is held in trust until it is paid to the person entitled to it, or the amount of your deposit is insured. In practice, your deposit cheques are made out to the trustee — usually the builder's lawyer, in trust — not to the builder's operating account. The builder does not get to spend your deposit on construction.

The trust rule has teeth on the way out, too. If the builder terminates your purchase agreement — a cancelled project is the case everyone fears — your deposit must be returned to you in full within 10 days, unless the termination results from your own default. That 10-day clock is the trust doing its job: the money was never the builder's, so there is nothing to negotiate.

Pre-construction condos Ottawa builders sell are deposit-heavy by design — the staged schedules themselves, cheque by cheque and builder by builder, are decoded in New Construction Deposit Structures Ottawa Decoded. Whatever the schedule, every cheque you write lands in the same place: the trust account. Which raises the obvious question — what stands behind the trust if something goes wrong with it?

What Tarion Itself Pays: the $20,000 Backstop

Tarion — the not-for-profit that administers Ontario's new home warranty and protection plan — is the backstop, and its number surprises most condo buyers: $20,000. If your deposit was not placed in trust, or the builder does not return it, Tarion protects the return of your deposit up to $20,000 — and that protection includes the money you put down towards upgrades and other extras, not just the base deposit.

Run the example that matters. A $650,000 condo with deposits totalling $97,500 — 15% of the price, staged over the build. If the trust system failed entirely and you were left with Tarion's cheque alone, $20,000 of that $97,500 is what Tarion pays; the other $77,500 is exactly why the trust account and the insurance layer above $20,000 exist, and why the next section matters more than this one. Compare the freehold side at the same price: a $650,000 freehold home carries a Tarion cap of $65,000 — 10% of the price — because a freehold deposit has no trust rule at all and Tarion's cheque is the whole protection. Different structures, different numbers: the condo system puts the law first and Tarion second. Layer Two: Excess Deposit Insurance

So how does deposit money above $20,000 ever reach the builder? Tarion's information sheet gives the mechanism in one phrase: deposits sit in trust until they are paid to the person entitled to them — or the amount is insured. A builder who wants to draw on your deposits beyond the first $20,000 can only do it when that excess is covered by deposit insurance arranged for the project — excess deposit insurance. No insurance, no release: the money stays in trust.

That makes the buyer's question obvious, and almost nobody asks it: before your deposits pass $20,000, ask for evidence that the project's excess deposit insurance is in place. A sales office that cannot answer is telling you something. The trust protects you only while the money is actually in it, and the insurance protects you only if it actually exists — both are checkable facts, not matters of faith.

If the Builder Fails or the Project Is Cancelled

Tarion's deposit protection responds to a short list of events — Tarion's freehold information sheet states them for new homes generally: your builder goes bankrupt, your builder fundamentally breaches your purchase agreement, or you exercise a legal right to terminate it. The cooling-off rescission covered below is one of those legal rights; a builder's failure on its outside occupancy date can be another, under the delay rules in your agreement's Statement of Critical Dates.

In the cancellation case the sequence is mechanical: the builder terminates, the full deposit is due back within 10 days from the trust, and only if it does not come back does the file become a Tarion claim — Tarion paying up to $20,000 while the trust and the excess insurance carry the balance. Keep every receipt, every notice and your Statement of Critical Dates: deposit claims run on paper, and the buyers who get paid fastest are the ones who can prove every dollar and every date.

What Deposit Protection Does Not Do

The limits matter as much as the coverage. Four of them:

  • It does not refund a change of heart. Outside a legal termination right — the cooling-off window, a builder breach, a missed outside date — walking away from a condo agreement is a default, and a defaulting buyer's deposit is the builder's claim, not Tarion's file.

  • Tarion's own cheque stops at $20,000. Everything above that rides on the trust account and the excess deposit insurance being real. That is why the checks in the next sections are not paperwork theatre — they are the protection.

  • It is not delay compensation. A late occupancy date is a different coverage with its own cap — $7,500 — handled under the delayed-closing rules, not the deposit rules. The warranty side of the file is covered in Tarion Warranty Ottawa New Homes 1 2 7 Explained.

  • It is not the warranty. Deposit protection guards your money before closing. Defects in the finished unit — the 1-2-7 warranty — start at occupancy and run on their own forms and deadlines.

The 10 Days That Matter Most

Separate from Tarion entirely, the Condominium Act gives every buyer of a new condo a 10-day cooling-off period: 10 calendar days from when you receive the disclosure statement, the condominium guide and the executed purchase agreement. Inside those 10 days you can cancel in writing, for any reason or no reason, and the vendor must refund everything you paid, with interest. It is the one window in the whole transaction where the law hands you time — spend it on a lawyer's review of the agreement and the disclosure, not on picking finishes.

One clock condo buyers do NOT have: the new 45-day rule. Starting April 1, 2026, buyers of new FREEHOLD homes must notify Tarion of their purchase within 45 days of signing to qualify for maximum deposit coverage — register late or not at all and coverage falls to a separate fund capped at $15 million a year, with the coverage changes themselves deferred in transition to January 1, 2027. Tarion built that rule for freehold deposits, which have no trust account; condominium purchasers are not required to notify Tarion, because the trust system already does the watching. If an article or a sales rep tells you, as a condo buyer, that your protection depends on a 45-day registration, you are reading the freehold rule in the wrong file. Five Checks Before You Sign

None of this requires a law degree. It requires asking five questions before the deposit cheques start moving:

  • Who is the trustee? Your deposit cheques should be payable to the trustee — typically the builder's lawyer, in trust. Get the trustee's name in writing before the first cheque.

  • Is the excess deposit insurance in place? Ask for evidence the project carries it before your deposits pass $20,000 — that is the exact point where Tarion's own coverage stops and the insurance becomes your protection.

  • Is the builder licensed and the project enrolled? Check the builder in the Ontario Builder Directory, the HCRA's public registry. Tarion's whole 2026 deposit-protection push exists because illegal, unlicensed selling is where buyers actually lose deposits.

  • Spend the cooling-off days on a lawyer. The 10 days are for the agreement and the disclosure statement — deposit terms, occupancy dates, the Statement of Critical Dates. Finishes can wait; the clock cannot.

  • Keep the paper. Every receipt, every notice, every amendment. If a deposit claim ever has to be made, it is proven with documents, not memories.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my pre-construction condo deposit protected?

Yes — by layers. Under the Condominium Act your deposit must be held in trust until it is owed to someone or the amount is insured. On top of the trust, Tarion backstops the return of your deposit up to $20,000 — including money put down on upgrades and extras — if the builder does not return it.

What does Tarion itself pay if something goes wrong?

For a condominium, up to $20,000. Freehold deposits carry higher Tarion caps because they have no trust rule: up to $60,000 on a home priced at $600,000 or less, and 10% of the purchase price to a maximum of $100,000 above that. In every case the coverage includes money paid towards upgrades and other extras.

My condo deposit will total $97,500. Is only $20,000 of it protected?

No — the $20,000 is only Tarion's own cheque, the last layer. The full $97,500 sits in trust under the Condominium Act, and the builder can only draw on amounts above $20,000 when that excess is covered by deposit insurance arranged for the project. The practical step: ask for evidence of that insurance before your deposits pass $20,000.

What happens to my deposit if the project is cancelled?

If the builder terminates your agreement, your deposit must be returned in full within 10 days — it was held in trust, so it was never the builder's spending money. Deposit protection responds when a builder goes bankrupt, fundamentally breaches the agreement, or you exercise a legal right to terminate: Tarion pays up to $20,000 of any amount not returned, and the trust and the excess insurance are what carry the balance.

What is excess deposit insurance?

The insurance a builder arranges so that deposit money above Tarion's $20,000 can be released from trust and used by the builder. Without it, amounts over $20,000 have to stay in trust. It is a project-level fact you can ask about — and should, in writing, before your deposits pass $20,000.

Does deposit protection cover me if I just change my mind?

No. Deposit protection responds to builder failure, builder breach, or your legal right to terminate — it is not a refund policy for buyer's remorse. Your legal exit without penalty is the 10-day cooling-off period after you receive the disclosure package; after it closes, walking away is a default.

How does the 10-day cooling-off period work?

Under the Condominium Act you have 10 calendar days from receiving the disclosure statement, the condominium guide and your executed purchase agreement to cancel in writing, for any reason. The vendor must refund all money paid, with interest. The right use of those days is a lawyer's review of the agreement and the disclosure — not the design studio.

Do I have to notify Tarion within 45 days of signing?

Not as a condo buyer. The 45-day notice rule, starting April 1, 2026, applies to new FREEHOLD purchases: freehold buyers who notify Tarion within 45 days qualify for maximum deposit coverage, and those who register late or not at all fall to a separate fund capped at $15 million annually. Condominium purchasers have no such duty — the trust system is their protection.

Is deposit protection the same thing as the Tarion warranty?

No. Deposit protection guards your money before closing. The 1-2-7 warranty covers the finished home after occupancy — defects, water penetration, structural issues — on its own forms and deadlines. Delayed-occupancy compensation is a third, separate coverage, capped at $7,500. The warranty guide in this series covers the 1-2-7 side in full.
