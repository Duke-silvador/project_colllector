An Immigrant Family, and Ottawa

My family emigrated from Poland, and we later made Ottawa home — the starting point for everything that followed.

I come from a Polish immigrant family. We emigrated from Poland as a family, and in time we moved to Ottawa. Anyone who has made that kind of move knows what it teaches you: you learn to read a new country's systems quickly, to ask direct questions, and to build a life from work rather than from connections you were born into.

That background has never left my real estate practice. A large part of my work has always been helping people who are themselves in transition — moving countries, moving cities, downsizing, or buying a first home in a place that is still becoming familiar. I know what it is to sign important papers in a system that was not built with you in mind, and to need someone who will explain it straight.

Carleton, Monterrey, and Warsaw

I completed a four-year Bachelor of International Business at Carleton, with exchanges in Mexico and Poland, then a Carleton master's degree.

I studied at Carleton University in Ottawa, completing a four-year Bachelor of International Business. In my third year I went on exchange to the Universidad de Monterrey in Mexico. In my fourth year I studied at the Warsaw School of Economics — SGH — in Poland. Few programs could have suited an immigrant kid better: business, studied on three continents, in the languages and markets my own family story crossed.

After that I completed a master's degree at Carleton in a cooperative program between European and Russian Studies and the Sprott School of Business. My thesis, supervised by Prof. Piotr Dutkiewicz, examined east–west Poland regional disparities — why one part of a country pulls ahead of another, and what policy and investment have to do with it. I did not know it then, but regional economics is not a bad education for someone who would spend his career reading local housing markets street by street.

Government, Then a Licence in 2005

I worked three years in government before getting my real estate licence in 2005 — and I have sold Ottawa homes ever since.

After university I worked for three years in government. It was good, serious work, and it taught me how institutions actually function — files, process, accountability. It also clarified what I wanted: a career where my results were my own, built directly with the people I served.

I earned my real estate licence in 2005 and have sold homes in Ottawa since that year. Two decades in one market teaches you its cycles in your bones: booms, corrections, bidding wars, and quiet years when patience was the whole job. Early in that career, about 18 years ago, I trained as an Accredited Senior Agent — training that later became the foundation of the seniors and downsizing work that is now a major part of my practice. An agent who has helped families through every kind of market, and every kind of life transition, brings something to a brokerage conversation that a newcomer cannot: he knows exactly what he needs a brokerage to do, and what he does not.

What did 20 years at work in Ottawa teach you about choosing a brokerage?

That the brokerage is infrastructure, not identity. Clients hire the agent. What I need from a brokerage is a fair cost structure, support that answers when a file needs it, and systems that do not get in the way of the work. Everything else — the brand on the sign, the office — matters far less than agents are taught to believe when they are starting out.

The Reasons Are Usually the Same Ones

Ask ten agents who switched and you will hear the same short list — it is my list too.

A lot of the reasons agents join are similar, because the frustrations are similar. Agents tell me they were paying for a bricks-and-mortar office they rarely used. Their fees kept climbing while the support stayed the same. They produced year after year and owned nothing at the end of it — no stock, no share of anything, just the next commission. They wanted training they could take at midnight, numbers they could check without calling an office manager, and a referral community bigger than one city. And they wanted to keep more of each commission once they had paid their share for the year.

Those were my reasons too. What settled it for me was simpler than any single line on that list: the foundation matched the business I had been trying to build since 2005 — one that truly serves clients, with the technology, the cost structure, and the community to back it up.

The program is part of what I can offer agents who join naming my senior partner as your sponsor and me as your co-sponsor: they are set up for the Free Lead Generation program, the leads generated are 100% theirs, and there is a weekly mastermind behind it. It is a practical offer, not a philosophy — an agent considering a move should weigh it alongside the split, the cap, and the fees, in that order.

What I Would Tell an Agent Considering the Same Move

Run your own numbers, talk to agents who made the move, and do not decide on anyone else's story — including mine.

My story explains how I got here; it should not make your decision. Your production, your fees, your clients, and your stage of career are different from mine in 2005, and different again from mine when I joined eXp. The honest advice from someone who has been licensed since 2005 is boring and reliable: calculate your effective keep rate where you are, compare full structures rather than headline splits, and ask what support you will actually use.

If I were choosing again, I would start where I started in 2005: with the clients. The right brokerage is the one whose foundation lets you do your best work for the people you serve. Since June 30, 2020, eXp has been that foundation for me.
