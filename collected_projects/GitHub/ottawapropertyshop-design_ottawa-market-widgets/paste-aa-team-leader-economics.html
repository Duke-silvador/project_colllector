The Team Leader's Real Question

A solo agent compares one split. A team leader compares a system: what the brokerage takes from every member, on every deal, at every volume.

When you lead a team, your brokerage economics are no longer about your own production. They are about the total flow of commission through your team, what percentage of that flow leaves the team permanently, and what you get for it. A structure that was tolerable for a solo agent can become the single largest cost in a team's business — larger than admin salaries, larger than lead spend.

The Agent Attraction hub covers bringing a team in general terms. This post goes into the arithmetic a team leader actually runs: cap stacking, leakage, and the fixed costs that sit underneath both. All figures below are the verified Canadian eXp structure: 80/20 split, $16,000 CAD cap at $80,000 GCI per agent, post-cap $285 per deal for 20 deals (maximum $5,700) then $75 per deal, $139 monthly cloud fee per agent, $199 startup fee, and $29 per deal for broker review. For teams there is a second layer, set out in eXp Canada’s own team policy: the leader always caps at the full $16,000, while members on a Traditional Team may elect a half cap of $8,000 and members on a Mega Icon Team a quarter cap of $4,000 — in exchange for paying the team leader a minimum of 25 percent of their GCI. The cap resets on each agent's own anniversary date.

The Four Team Structures in Canada, and What Each Member Pays

eXp Canada’s team policy — the “Canada Teams Defined” page in eXp’s own knowledge base — sets four team structures. The cap is per agent in all of them, but the member cap is set by the structure, and two of the four let members cap at a fraction of the full amount:

Two consequences matter for the arithmetic below. First, a member on a reduced cap stops paying the brokerage split much earlier: a half-cap member caps once eXp has collected $8,000 — 20 percent of $40,000 of that member’s own share of GCI, after the team split divides the commission. Second, the reduction is a trade, not a gift: reduced-cap members pay the team leader at least 25 percent of their GCI, and they are not eligible for the capping equity award or the ICON Agent Award — both require paying a full cap, so members chasing ICON stay on $16,000 by choice.

Per-Agent Cap Stacking: How Caps Add Up Across a Team

Each agent caps individually — but not every agent caps at $16,000. A team's maximum split contribution to the brokerage is still knowable in advance: the leader's $16,000, plus each member's elected cap — $16,000, $8,000 or $4,000 depending on the team's structure.

Cap stacking is the mechanic most team leaders miss on a first reading, and the reduced member caps are the part most often missed inside it. The cap is per agent, not per team — but the amount differs by seat. Take a Traditional Team of a leader and four producing members: if every member elects the half cap, the team’s total cap exposure is $16,000 plus four times $8,000 — $48,000, not the $80,000 a full-cap reading suggests. On a Mega Icon Team, the same headcount at quarter caps totals $32,000. Each agent then moves to the post-cap schedule. The brokerage’s total split take from your team in that year is bounded and calculable before the year starts.

Compare the shape, not a forecast: with no cap, the brokerage's take rises in proportion to team GCI with no ceiling. With caps, it rises until each agent caps, then flattens to small per-deal amounts. The larger the team, the wider the gap.

Does my team share one cap, or does each agent cap separately?
Each agent caps separately, on their own production and their own anniversary date — the one exception is a Domestic Team, where two partners share a single $16,000 cap between them. But separate does not mean identical: the leader's cap is always the full $16,000, while members may sit at $16,000, $8,000 or $4,000 depending on the team's structure and their own election. When you model your team's year, add up each person's elected cap, then add the post-cap per-deal fees for production above those caps. That is your ceiling case.

Team Split Leakage: The Percentage That Never Comes Back

Leakage is the share of team GCI that leaves the team at every layer — brokerage split, franchise royalty, and internal splits — before the team pays its own costs.

Team leaders usually negotiate their internal split with members carefully and their brokerage split casually. It should be the other way around. Consider the layers on a single deal:

  • The brokerage split taken off the top of the full commission

  • Any franchise royalty or brand fee taken in addition to the split

  • Per-deal transaction, administration, and broker review fees

  • Monthly per-agent fees — desk, technology, and office charges multiplied by team size

  • Only then, your internal team split with the member who produced the deal

Every layer above your internal split is leakage: money the team's work generated that the team will never deploy. Divide everything the brokerage kept or charged across all members by total team GCI. That is your leakage rate — often higher than the headline split, because per-agent fees multiply with headcount and per-deal fees with volume.

Under the eXp schedule, the equivalent list is short and fixed: the capped split, $139 per agent monthly, and $29 per deal. Whether it beats your current leakage rate is a calculation, not an opinion — run it on your last 12 months.

One more mechanic changes the leader’s side of this ledger. Under eXp’s rules, a commission is divided between the team leader and the team member first, and eXp’s company dollar and fees are then deducted from each agent individually. And a member on a reduced cap must pay the leader a minimum of 25 percent of their GCI on every transaction. At a traditional brokerage, the leader’s share of member production is negotiated against the house’s take. Here the house’s take is capped — and smaller per member — while the leader’s minimum share of member GCI is set by policy. That is the structural reason team economics invert at eXp: the brokerage’s slice is the fixed, bounded one, and the leader’s is not.

Admin and Lead Costs: The Layer the Brokerage Comparison Misses

Your brokerage choice does not remove admin and lead costs, but it changes how much margin is left to fund them.

A team's other two big costs — administration and lead generation — sit below the brokerage line, and they are yours under any model. Coordinators, marketing help, CRM and lead spend do not disappear when you change brokerages. What changes is the margin available to pay for them well.

When a Team Has Outgrown Its Split

Three signals say the structure no longer fits: most members cap-able, leakage rising with headcount, and recruiting limited by the offer you can make.

Watch for these signs in your own operation:

  • Most of your producing members would hit a cap on their own production — $80,000 of their own GCI share at a full cap, $40,000 at a half cap — so a per-agent cap would bind for nearly the whole team

  • Your total brokerage cost rose faster than team GCI last year, driven by per-agent and per-deal fees

  • You are subsidising members' fees out of your own split to keep your offer competitive

  • Recruiting conversations stall on economics you do not control — the brokerage's take, not yours

  • You are paying office costs for space a cloud-based team uses a few days a month

None of these signals obliges you to move. Together, they oblige you to run the comparison properly — team GCI, every member's production, every fee, both structures, one spreadsheet. eXp's scale is relevant context for a team that recruits and refers across markets: over 82,000 agents across 29 countries, with Q2 2026 reporting 87,338 agents including approximately 4,900 from NextHome. A team's members keep one brokerage, one system, and one cap structure wherever they work.

If the arithmetic favours a move, sequence it as a team: production and anniversary dates mapped member by member, clients notified by their own agent first, and admin systems migrated before the public announcement. A team move done in that order is a business decision. Done in the reverse order, it is a disruption.
