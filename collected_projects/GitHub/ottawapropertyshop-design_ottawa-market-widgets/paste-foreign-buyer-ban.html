Canada’s foreign buyer ban was supposed to disappear on January 1, 2027. That was the deal when the Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act was extended in 2024: two more years, then the door reopens. Budget 2026, tabled in Ottawa on November 5, 2026 by Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne, proposes to tear up that deal. The ban on foreign purchases of existing homes would run two more years, to January 1, 2029 — while brand-new homes are carved out of it entirely.

Read that again, because it splits the Ottawa market in two. If you own a resale home and were quietly waiting for foreign demand to return in 2027, the budget just cancelled your cavalry. If you build homes — or buy them new — the same budget hands you the only key to a buyer pool nobody else can touch. And if you are a newcomer, a permanent resident, or a Canadian wondering whether any of this touches you, the answer is more useful than the headlines suggest.

One thing before anything else: this is a proposal, not law. No bill has been tabled. The budget does not define what counts as a “new residential property,” gives the exemption no start date, and says nothing about presale contracts or assignments. The law on the books today still repeals the ban on January 1, 2027. Everything below separates what Budget 2026 proposes from what is actually in force — because planning your sale or your purchase around a headline, instead of the law, is how people lose money.

The Question Everyone Asks First — Is the 25 Percent Foreign Buyer Tax Still On?

Yes. Completely, and Budget 2026 does not touch it.

Ontario’s Non-Resident Speculation Tax — the NRST — is separate provincial law. It has charged 25 percent province-wide since October 25, 2022, it has no expiry date, and it applies to foreign nationals, foreign corporations and taxable trustees buying land with one to six single-family residences — on top of ordinary land transfer tax. The two regimes do different jobs: the federal ban decides whether a non-Canadian CAN buy; the NRST decides what they PAY when they can. A federal exemption for new homes does not wipe out a single dollar of Ontario tax.

Toronto buyers face an extra 10 percent municipal version of the tax on top — 35 percent combined there. That is Toronto only. Ottawa has no municipal NRST; the provincial 25 percent is the whole stack here.

So if the new-home exemption becomes law, what does a foreign buyer actually pay in Ottawa? Here is the honest math on an $800,000 new home — and it is not the math in most headlines. On a purchase signed inside Ontario’s current rebate window, the 13 percent HST comes straight back. The tax that actually bites is the 25 percent NRST, and nothing rebates that.

How the HST Rebate Works

Two different HST-relief regimes are running at the same time, on different rules — and most coverage of this budget blends them into one. There is a third, older rebate underneath both. One distinction matters first: Ontario’s temporary rebate window is already law — it was enacted in Ontario’s Budget 2026 (Bill 114) — while the foreign-buyer changes in the federal budget are still only a proposal. The rebate rules below are in force today.

The Ontario window — the $130,000 one. For agreements of purchase and sale signed between April 1, 2026 and March 31, 2027, Ontario rebates the entire 13 percent HST on a new home. Two programs stack to do it: the Ontario Enhanced New Housing Rebate covers the 8 percent provincial part, up to $80,000, and the Ontario New Homes Affordability Payment covers the 5 percent federal part, up to $50,000 — set out in Ontario Regulation 196/26 and administered by the Canada Revenue Agency (GST/HST Notice 346). The maximum rebate is $130,000: the full amount on homes up to $1,000,000, the $130,000 cap holding flat to $1,500,000, then tapering to $24,000 at $1,850,000 and above.

The conditions are about the buyer and the use of the home — and this is the part that changes the foreign-buyer math. The window is open to all individual buyers: first-time buyers, move-up buyers, downsizers and investors alike. There is no first-time-buyer test and no citizenship or permanent-residence test. The buyer must be an individual (corporations do not qualify); the home must be bought for use as a primary place of residence — the buyer’s or a relation’s — or as a long-term residential rental property; the agreement must be signed inside the window; and on builder purchases, construction must begin by December 31, 2028 and be substantially completed by December 31, 2031. The rebate runs through the CRA’s new housing rebate process, and on most builder sales the buyer assigns it to the builder, who credits it against the price at closing — so the buyer never fronts the tax and claims it back.

So run the foreign-buyer case again with the rules as they actually are. A non-exempt foreign buyer who buys a new home inside the window, for a primary residence or a long-term rental, gets the whole 13 percent HST back — the HST is effectively neutralized, exactly as the table above shows. What still bites, in full, is the 25 percent NRST; no rebate touches it. Until the window closes on March 31, 2027, the honest tax story on a new home is the NRST alone. After it closes, the full HST stack returns for everyone who missed it.

The first-time-buyer pair — the layer with a status gate. Running to the end of 2030 is a different pair of programs: the federal First-Time Home Buyers’ GST/HST Rebate (Bill C-4, law since March 12, 2026) returns 100 percent of the 5 percent federal part, up to $50,000, on homes up to $1,000,000 and phasing out by $1,500,000 — and Ontario matches it on the 8 percent provincial part, up to $80,000 — for agreements signed from March 20, 2025 to the end of 2030. This pair carries the tests the Ontario window does not: the buyer must be 18 or older, a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, and a genuine first-time buyer — not have lived in a home owned by the buyer or their spouse in the current or any of the previous four calendar years, anywhere in the world — buying a primary residence they will be the first to occupy, once in a lifetime. This is the layer that helps a newcomer who has their permanent residence. A non-resident foreign buyer does not qualify for it at all.

Underneath both sits the long-standing GST/HST New Housing Rebate — the pre-2026 program, tied to a primary residence, its federal part phasing out entirely by a $450,000 home value and its Ontario part worth up to $24,000. It is the floor the newer programs were built on; for most Ottawa purchases, the window and the first-time pair are worth far more.

So Does Anything Actually Change?

For resale, almost nothing measurable — and that is precisely the point sellers need to hear. The banned demand was always small. In Metro Vancouver, foreign buyers were on title in 182 of 32,219 purchases from January to September 2026 — 0.56 percent, per B.C. property transfer tax data. Statistics Canada put non-resident ownership at 2.2 percent of Ontario residential property back in 2020. Royal LePage concluded in 2024 that the ban had “virtually no impact” on prices, and CIBC deputy chief economist Benjamin Tal said in October 2026 that the ban “doesn’t make any sense.”

So the resale extension does not remove a large buyer pool — there never was one in Ottawa. What it removes is an expectation: the January 2027 reopening that some sellers were holding out for. An expectation can hold a price up for a while. When it is cancelled by a federal budget, the smart move is to reprice to the market that actually exists — today’s Ottawa buyers, today’s Ottawa numbers.

For new homes, the change is real — and larger than the headlines suggest. Once legislation and regulations pass, builders gain a buyer pool for presales and new inventory that resale sellers cannot access. And for purchases signed inside Ontario’s rebate window, that pool’s tax bill is the NRST alone: the 13 percent HST is rebated in full on a primary-residence or long-term-rental purchase, which is why the table above lands at $212,475 in total taxes, not $316,475. The full stack only applies if the window is missed — sign after March 31, 2027, or outside the rebate conditions, and the HST lands again in full. Inside the window, expect real interest. The 25 percent NRST is what keeps it from becoming a rush.

Why the Government Is Doing It

The logic, stated fairly, is channel, don’t ban. Foreign money that finances a home that does not exist yet adds a unit to the housing stock. Foreign money bidding on an existing home adds none — it just competes with a Canadian family for a fixed supply. Budget 2026 says it in its own words, in Chapter 3:

“Budget 2026 proposes to extend the ban on foreign purchases of existing residential properties for two years, to January 1, 2029, while exempting new residential properties. This would help protect housing affordability by keeping the vast majority of the market available for Canadians, while unlocking additional investment in new housing supply.”

The budget adds that the government “will propose legislation and launch a regulatory process on this change in short order.”

The construction argument is about presales. Builders finance projects on early sales; when the presale market seizes, projects stall and homes never get built. The Greater Toronto and Hamilton Area — the country’s biggest new-home market — recorded only 702 new condo sales in the second quarter of 2026 (Urbanation), with developer-held unsold inventory at a record of just over 5,000 units and no new project launches for a second straight quarter. Builders and their associations have argued for years that foreign presale buyers take early project risk and help projects reach their financing thresholds.

This is also the Australia model: Canberra restricts foreign purchases of established homes while steering foreign capital into new dwellings. Housing Minister Gregor Robertson had been pointing at that review through 2025 and 2026, and the real estate industry’s national and B.C. associations advocateded for exactly this revision. Budget 2026 adopted it.

A Two-Tier Market — What It Means in Ottawa

Put the budget beside the market Ottawa sellers are already in. In September 2026, OREB reported 1,010 sales, down 6.6 percent year over year; an average price of $685,640, down 1.0 percent; 4,813 active listings, up 7.9 percent; and 4.8 months of inventory — the highest September level in a decade. Then, on October 28, 2026, the Bank of Canada raised its policy rate 25 basis points to 2.5 percent, making carrying a listed home more expensive for buyers, not less.

Into that soft market, Budget 2026 gives new construction a double tailwind resale does not get: the proposed foreign-buyer exemption, plus HST relief worth up to $130,000 on agreements signed by March 31, 2027 — a window open to every individual buyer, with no first-time and no citizenship test. A resale seller is now competing against builder product whose HST is fully rebated inside that window and — if the exemption passes — open to a class of buyers the seller is barred from for two more years. Stated plainly: on the numbers, this budget prioritizes building new homes over protecting resale values. Whether you call that fair or not, it is the market you are selling into, and your pricing has to live in it.

The honest caution runs the other way for new homes: critics, including a Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives economist cited in Waterloo-region coverage, warn that foreign demand for new builds could push up prices on entry-level new homes — the very homes first-time buyers need. The government is accepting that trade-off in exchange for supply. And one Ottawa tempering note: this city’s foreign-buyer share was always small, so neither the loss on resale nor the gain on new construction will feel dramatic here. The local story is expectations and competition between resale and new — not a flood in either direction.

Who Says What

The industry associations. The BC Real Estate Association reported on November 6, 2026 that the Canadian Real Estate Association and BCREA “have advocated for this specific revision to the ban” — the Australia-style split between existing and new homes. CREA’s own published response to Budget 2026 is more measured: it lists the change neutrally — “Extended to January 1, 2029, while exempting new residential properties, distinguishing investment that contributes to new supply from purchases of existing homes” — and saves its criticism for the ownership gap, saying the budget’s measures “do not yet bridge the gap for middle-class Canadians.” Read both together: the industry asked for this change, and its national association is still telling Ottawa that attainable ownership is the unfinished business.

Ontario’s association. OREA has issued no statement on the Budget 2026 ban proposal itself. Its most recent sourced position is dated: on February 25, 2026, OREA joined the Housing Advancement Coalition statement urging Ottawa to “amend the prohibition on foreign home buyers, with clear and enforceable guardrails” and to expand new-home tax rebates. It also welcomed Ontario’s HST relief on new homes and recommended extending its deadline. That is the record — February 2026, not a reaction to this budget.

The regulator. RECO, Ontario’s real estate regulator, does not lobby and takes no position on this policy — that is not its role. Its relevance here is compliance: registrants must verify buyer status and must never knowingly facilitate a prohibited purchase. A non-Canadian who buys in breach of the Act commits an offence carrying a fine of up to $10,000, and a court can order the property sold. If anyone suggests a workaround to you, walk away and call me instead.

Builders. The home-building industry asked for the new-home exemption on the presale-financing argument above, and has fought the ban’s side effects on development since the first regulations in 2022. Budget 2026 gives builders most of what they asked for — the rest depends on how the coming regulations define a new residential property.

Buyers and sellers. Canadian resale buyers keep a protected market, on the government’s framing. Canadian new-home buyers get real HST relief but may meet renewed foreign competition for precon and entry-level product. Resale sellers are the clear losers of this budget — especially anyone who delayed listing in expectation of the January 2027 reopening.

Questions I Am Getting Asked

Has the foreign buyer ban actually been extended to 2029?
Not yet. Budget 2026, tabled on November 5, 2026, proposes the extension, but no bill has been introduced and no regulations have been written. Under the law as it stands today, the ban still ends on January 1, 2027. Nothing about what a foreign buyer can purchase has changed yet.

Do foreign buyers still pay the 25 percent Ontario tax?
Yes. Ontario’s Non-Resident Speculation Tax is separate provincial law: 25 percent across Ontario since October 25, 2022, with no expiry date, charged on top of land transfer tax. The federal ban decides whether a non-Canadian can buy; the NRST decides what they pay when they can. A federal exemption for new homes does not remove the provincial tax.

When can a foreign buyer purchase a new home in Ottawa?
Only once the Budget 2026 proposal becomes law and the regulations define what counts as a new residential property — there is no definition, no start date and no presale rule yet. And when it does open, the 25 percent NRST applies in full — no rebate touches it. The 13 percent HST depends on timing: Ontario rebates it entirely on qualifying purchases signed by March 31, 2027.

Does a foreign buyer get the HST back on a new home?
Inside Ontario’s window, yes — all of it. For agreements signed between April 1, 2026 and March 31, 2027, Ontario rebates the full 13 percent HST on a new home, up to $130,000, when the home will be a primary residence or a long-term rental. The window has no first-time-buyer test and no citizenship or permanent-residence test, and on most builder sales the rebate is credited against the price at closing. Miss the window or the conditions and the HST is payable in full. The one tax no rebate ever reduces is the 25 percent NRST.

I am a permanent resident of Canada. Does the ban or the tax apply to me?
No. Both the federal ban and Ontario’s NRST key on citizenship and permanent-resident status, not on where you were born or where you lived before. Canadian permanent residents were never banned and do not pay the NRST.

No — the January 2027 reopening you may have been waiting for is exactly what Budget 2026 proposes to cancel. What is the March 31, 2027 deadline for new-home buyers?
Ontario’s temporary window rebates the entire 13 percent HST on a new home — up to $130,000 — for agreements signed by March 31, 2027, provided the home will be a primary residence or a long-term rental. The window is open to every individual buyer: no first-time test, no citizenship test. After that date the full HST is payable again, aside from the smaller first-time-buyer and long-standing rebates. If you are buying new construction, that signing date is worth more than any headline in this story. Check your eligibility before you sign, not after.
