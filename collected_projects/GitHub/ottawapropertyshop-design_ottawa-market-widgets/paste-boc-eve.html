The Bank of Canada Decides Wednesday: Should Ottawa Buyers Buy Before or After the Rate Decision?

By Tomasz R. Witek | Real estate salesperson with eXp Realty, Brokerage

Ottawa mortgage rates are the question every buyer is asking me this week, and it is easy to see why: the Bank of Canada announces its next rate decision tomorrow morning, Wednesday, October 28, and nobody wants to sign a purchase the day before their payment could have been smaller — or watch the home they wanted sell to someone else while they waited for news that changed nothing. If that sounds familiar, you are asking the same question my clients are asking, and the honest answer is the one I gave in my earlier guide, Ottawa Real Estate 2026: Should You Buy a Home Now or Wait? — the answer isn’t the same for everyone.

Here is the part that matters: tomorrow’s announcement matters less than what it does to YOUR rate, YOUR rate hold, and YOUR payment. This post walks through what the Bank is actually deciding, why fixed and variable Ottawa mortgage rates do not move together, the rate-hold move that makes “before or after” the wrong question, and what a quarter of a percentage point is really worth on a typical Ottawa payment — in dollars, not headlines.

1. What the Bank of Canada Is Actually Deciding on Wednesday

The Bank of Canada sets one number: its policy interest rate — the overnight rate that anchors lending across the country. As of today, that rate is 2.25 per cent, where it has stood since October 2025. On Wednesday the Bank can cut it, hold it, or raise it, and this time forecasters are genuinely divided — after September inflation came in at 3.1 per cent, a number of economists expect a quarter-point rise, while others expect the Bank to hold and watch. I am not going to predict it here, because nobody outside the Bank’s Governing Council knows, and a guess dressed up as advice helps no one buy a home.

What the policy rate controls directly is the pricing of variable-rate mortgages and home equity lines of credit, because lenders set their prime rates off it. What it does not control directly is the fixed rate on the five-year mortgage most Ottawa home buyers actually take — and that distinction is the whole ballgame this week.

2. What a Cut, a Hold, or a Hike Would Do to Ottawa Mortgage Rates

If you hold or take a variable rate: your rate follows the Bank. A cut of 0.25 per cent would typically flow through to variable rates within days; a hike would raise them the same way. On an adjustable-rate variable mortgage your payment changes; on a fixed-payment variable, the payment stays and the share going to interest changes instead.

If you hold or take a fixed rate: your rate follows the bond market, not the announcement. Lenders price five-year fixed terms mainly off the five-year Government of Canada bond yield, and bond investors trade on expectations — which means an expected decision is often already priced into fixed rates BEFORE the announcement lands. That is why fixed Ottawa mortgage rates can rise in a week the Bank holds steady, and why they sometimes barely move on decision day itself. Watching only the headline rate while ignoring the bond market is how buyers get surprised.

If the Bank holds: variable rates do not move, fixed rates keep following the bond market, and the buyers who waited for “the news” discover there was no news in their payment at all.

3. The Rate Hold: How Buyers Lock In Now and Still Float Down

Here is the move that turns “before or after Wednesday?” into the wrong question. When you get pre-approved, most lenders will hold a rate for you — typically 90 to 120 days, depending on the lender. That hold is a ceiling, not a floor: if rates rise while you shop, your held rate protects you; if rates fall before you close, most lenders will let you float down to the lower rate. Terms vary by lender, so confirm the exact hold period and float-down policy in writing — this is standard homework, and it is the same money-chain territory my map of who is involved in buying a house in Ottawa lays out organization by organization (my companion guide on mortgage default insurance in Canada, publishing in the same series, covers the other charge that surprises buyers with less than 20 per cent down).

With a rate hold in place, the decision stops being a bet on Wednesday. You shop for the right home at a protected rate, and the announcement becomes background noise instead of a deadline. If you do not have a hold and you are actively shopping this week, getting one is the single most useful thing you can do before tomorrow morning — it costs nothing and it works in both directions.

4. What a Quarter Point Is Really Worth on a Typical Ottawa Payment

Feelings about rates get clearer with arithmetic. Take an illustrative $600,000 mortgage — a mortgage amount, not a house price — on a 25-year amortization, using the Canadian standard of semi-annual compounding. The monthly payment formula is payment = P × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)−300), where i is the monthly rate converted from the nominal rate. Run it three ways:

At 4.25 per cent, the payment is $3,237.96 a month. At 4.50 per cent, it is $3,320.84 a month. At 4.75 per cent, it is $3,404.73 a month. So a quarter-point move — in either direction — is about $83 a month on this mortgage, roughly $995 over a year. Real money, worth having, and worth planning around. It is not, on its own, a reason to buy the wrong house or to lose the right one.

Two honest caveats. First, these are illustrative rates chosen to show the size of a quarter point — they are not a quote, and your lender’s actual rate, your balance and your amortization will change the number. Second, the payment is only half the story: the same quarter point also changes how much mortgage you qualify for under the stress test, which is why buyers whose budget only works IF a cut comes are in a fragile spot — section by section, that fragility is what the verdict below is really about.

5. Will Prices Move the Week After a Decision?

House prices do not reprice overnight because of one announcement. Sellers do not wake up Thursday and rewrite their asking prices, and Ottawa houses for sale do not get cheaper or dearer by Friday. What can change after a decision is competition: a cut that brings sidelined buyers back can mean more offers on the same homes within a few weeks, while a hike can thin the field. Ottawa entered the fall with buyers holding real choice — the Ottawa Real Estate Board reported about 4.8 months of inventory for September 2026 — so this week, conditions and negotiation room matter more than a one-day rate headline. The home is the asset; the rate is a term of the loan. Buyers who keep that order straight tend to do well in either rate direction.

6. What Sellers Are Likely to Do Next

Look at it from the other side of the table, because your offer lands on a person, not a chart. Sellers listing this fall have already priced against today’s Ottawa real estate market, not against a hoped-for cut. A hold on Wednesday changes nothing in their plans. A cut may make some sellers firmer on price, expecting more buyers; a hike may make well-priced homes a little more negotiable as some buyers pause. Either way, the sellers who move are the ones whose lives are moving — a job, a growing family, a downsize — and that has been true in every rate environment I have worked in since 2005.

7. Why Your Street Matters More Than the Announcement

The Bank of Canada sets one rate for the whole country. It does not know your street. A quarter point is worth about $83 a month on a $600,000 mortgage everywhere in Canada — but the difference between the right home and the wrong one in Greely, Manotick, Riverside South, Barrhaven, Findlay Creek or Kanata is worth far more, and it lasts far longer than any single rate decision. Ottawa home buyers who anchor on the home, the location and a payment that is comfortable at TODAY’s rate — with a rate hold protecting the file — are the ones who look back on weeks like this one without regret, whatever Wednesday brings.

So, Buy Before or After Wednesday?

Read the two lists honestly and you already know your answer. If the payment only works with a cut that may never come, waiting is not fear — it is arithmetic. If your hold is in place and the right home is in front of you, Wednesday is not a reason to lose it. And if you are somewhere in between, get the rate hold first and let the announcement do whatever it is going to do.
