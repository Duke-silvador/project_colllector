Greely at a Glance

Why do buyers choose a village 30 minutes south of downtown over a closer suburb? The answer is the lot. Greely offers what the suburban districts cannot: half-acre to two-acre homesites inside the City of Ottawa, most of them wrapped around lakes, trails and mature trees, at prices that sit below comparable Manotick estate product while offering far larger lots than Riverside South or Findlay Creek at a similar spend. Village services cover the weekly list — groceries, pharmacy, medical, schools, a community centre and library — without suburban density. Downtown is roughly a 30-minute drive, the airport 15 to 20 minutes, and Manotick’s larger retail is one concession west. It is, in the phrase the village has earned, Ottawa’s estate-lot village.

A Short History — Farm Village to Estate Village

Greely’s roots are Osgoode Township roots: the township’s first settler families arrived in 1827, and the village grew up as a farm-service centre on the Bank Street corridor — the old Ottawa–Prescott road, today Ottawa Road 31. The name is younger than the settlement: the post office was renamed Greely in 1886, most likely for General Adolphus Washington Greely, the American Arctic explorer whose expeditions of 1881–84 were then famous — the Geographical Names Board’s own file records the reasoning. The welcome signs carry the village’s stagecoach emblem and an “est. 1834” date, a nod to the corridor era when Bank Street carried the south’s farm trade toward Bytown.

The Welcome to Greely sign — the stagecoach emblem marks the Bank Street corridor the village grew up on.

For a century Greely stayed what it was: a quiet farm village of a few crossroads streets. The transformation began in 1990, when a local developer, Dan Anderson, founded Sunset Lakes Developments in the village with an idea borrowed from waterfront communities in Florida, Arizona and New Zealand — that a cottage lifestyle could be built 30 minutes from Parliament Hill, on big lots around spring-fed lakes, without a metre of municipal water or sewer pipe. The first residents of Sunset Lakes moved in in 1993; by the end of the decade that first community was sold out, and the formula — estate lots, lakes doing the stormwater work, owners associations, design review on every home — repeated community after community. Greely became part of the City of Ottawa in the 2001 amalgamation with its village character intact, and it is still growing on the same idea: one local developer built seven lake communities in sequence and is building an eighth.

Neighbourhoods and Subdivisions

Modern Greely is a set of distinct communities, most of them built by one hand. Sunset Lakes Developments (founded 1990, headquartered on Old Prescott Road) built its villages-within-the-village on a repeatable formula: half-acre to one-acre lots (some communities to two acres) on private wells and septic, wrapped around spring-fed lakes that double as the stormwater-management ponds; a residents’ owners association in each community; deed restrictions and design review on every home; and shared amenities — pools, courts, trails, beaches, club buildings. The original Sunset Lakes deed restrictions even ban chemical fertilizers and pesticides to protect the lake water. The sequence ran Sunset Lakes, Woodstream, South Village, Greely Orchard Estates, Water’s Edge, Cedar Lakes and Lakewood Trails, with Forest Lake Estates as the eighth. Greely’s other great community story — the golf-course estates — belongs to a different family entirely, and both stories are told below.

The Anderson Story — How Sunset Lakes Developments Built Modern Greely

Sunset Lakes Developments is headquartered where it builds: 1705 Old Prescott Road, in the village itself. Dan Anderson founded the company in 1990 and remains its president and public face. The founding idea, in the company’s own words, was to bring the essence of world-class waterfront living to the Village of Greely — inspired by coastal communities in Florida, Arizona and New Zealand, and proof that you can enjoy the perks of a cottage lifestyle without the long commute. Greely had no municipal water or sewer then, and has none now, so the concept was engineered around private services from day one.

What Anderson repeated, community after community, was a formula nobody else in Ottawa was running at this scale: estate lots of half an acre to an acre — some communities to two acres — on private wells and septic, wrapped around spring-fed lakes that do double duty as the stormwater-management ponds. Around each lake came the rest of the package: a residents’ owners association, deed restrictions and design review on every home, and shared amenities — pools, tennis and pickleball courts, trails, beaches, club buildings. The environmental rulebook was part of the design, not an afterthought: the original Sunset Lakes deed restrictions even ban chemical fertilizers and pesticides to protect the lake water.

The proof of concept was Sunset Lakes itself, on the village’s west side: first residents in 1993, sold out by the end of the decade. Then the sequence this guide’s subdivisions section follows — Woodstream in the north end; South Village around its own lake at the south end; Greely Orchard Estates, the two-acre variation, whose last lots the developer was still marketing in 2014; Water’s Edge, with its sandy, limestone-framed shoreline; Cedar Lakes on Stagecoach Road, registered as Plans 4M-1479 and 4M-1555; and in 2017 the executive flagship, Lakewood Trails. The eighth community, Forest Lake Estates, is marketing Phase 1 now, with a new twist — a registered conservation easement and a 20-percent tree-canopy rule on every lot. Seven communities completed and an eighth selling, every one of them inside the same village boundary, and not one of them on a municipal water or sewer line.

The residential run was paired with a commercial one: the Greely Village Centre at Bank Street and Parkway Road — the grocery plaza and the professional buildings around it — which the company calls the heartbeat of the area. And Anderson has become the village’s loudest voice for more of what he builds. He has argued Greely’s case at City Hall for over a decade, and his position is on the record: the homebuilding industry in Greely, he told a City committee, is “in dire need of supply” — at the time, he counted only three lots on the market in a village of roughly 10,000 people, the largest of Ottawa’s rural villages. The next Cedar Lakes phase and the Emerald Estates lands beside the golf course are the supply he is still working to bring through the approvals process.

The Patterson Story — Creating Emerald Links

If Sunset Lakes is one man’s formula repeated, Emerald Links is one family’s land turned into a life’s work. Gibson “Gib” Patterson was born on March 5, 1938, at Billings Bridge — in the same room where his father had been born in 1900. The Pattersons had come to Canada from Ireland in 1884, and his grandparents bought garden land on Hunt Club Road in 1890; land was the family trade from the start. Gib took his Bachelor of Science in Agriculture at McGill’s Macdonald College in 1960, worked as an assistant agricultural representative in Hastings County, and in 1962 married Elsie Martin; together they bought a 100-acre farm near Metcalfe. From 1963 he taught at Osgoode Township High School for nine years — the same school Greely’s teenagers bus to today — while farming hundreds of acres of soybeans, wheat and hay and quietly accumulating real estate.

Golf came next, and it came from the farm country he knew. In 1971 Gib opened Airport Golfland on Hunt Club Road — a driving range, mini golf and a dairy bar under the slogan “Go Green, Go Golfing” — and followed it in 1978 with Innes Road Golfland in Orléans, which still operates today. By the family’s own account, real estate and development were always the interest behind the ranges; what the ranges built was the business that made a full golf course possible.

Emerald Links was built on family land in Greely, and it was built by family hands: in the mid-1980s, Gib’s son Kevin Patterson built the roads, cut the trees, dug the ponds and carved the land that would become the course and the subdivision around it. The design came from Canadian golf-course architect Graham Cooke. When Emerald Links Golf & Country Club opened in July 1990, it opened as one integrated development — an 18-hole course with the first Emerald Links Estates lots around it, custom homes on one- to two-acre lots fronting the fairways. The estates grew in step with the course, in three registered phases (Plans 4M-655, 4M-1267 and 4M-1660), and when the south nine opened on July 1, 2003 — taking the course to the 27 holes it has today — it was released together with more one-acre building lots. Sixteen of the 27 holes have water in play, and the East–West combination plays par 71 at 6,346 yards.

The family never sold. Emerald Links is still Patterson-owned and operated, run with Cloverdale Links in Winchester and Anderson Links in Carlsbad Springs as the ACE Links Group — 72 holes across the three courses — with the second generation in the business: Martin Patterson as general manager at Emerald Links, and Kevin’s K.G. Patterson Construction building out the family’s courses alongside Cooke. The honours followed the work: the Order of Ottawa, the Queen’s Golden Jubilee Medal, and McGill’s MacDonald Distinguished Alumni Award in 2022. One naming note, because Greely’s two development stories are easy to tangle: Anderson Links is named for the Anderson farm it sits on — “Black Jim” Anderson’s farm, which came to Gib through his sister Norma’s marriage into that family. Those Andersons are Gib’s in-laws; they are no relation to Dan Anderson of Sunset Lakes Developments, whose communities fill the rest of this guide.

Wells, Septic and Servicing — the Honest Section

Every Greely purchase starts with one question: where do the water and the wastewater come from? There is no municipal water or sewer system in the village — not in the subdivisions, not even in the commercial core. Most homes run on a private well and a private septic system, and that is normal here: the homework is the well records, the septic history and, on a resale, inspections budgeted before you firm up.

The exception is the communal pocket. Shadow Ridge and Lakeland Meadows are served by City of Ottawa communal drinking-water and wastewater systems — the City’s February 2026 development-charge by-law names the two communities together. The Shadow Ridge system has been operating at or near its rated limits, and 79 of its Phase-2 lots were held under a servicing reserve. The unlock is now approved: Council has adopted a water plant upgrade framework under which an interim “bridge” upgrade is delivered by the developer at 100 percent developer cost, so the held lots can proceed. City reporting has also documented a rising nitrate trend in the Shadow Ridge source wells — treated water remaining within the provincial standard, with the City monitoring and studying a deeper aquifer — which is exactly the kind of file a buyer in that pocket should ask to read. No competing Greely page carries this story; it is the single most useful servicing fact in the village, and it is why Shadow Ridge communal well searches land on this guide.

Elsewhere, the rules of the road are the rural ones: a well is a quantity-and-quality question (flow rate, potability, treatment equipment), a septic system is an age-and-capacity question, and a building lot is a feasibility question — what can be drilled, dug and approved on that specific land. On Greely’s estate lots those answers are usually good; they are still answers you get in writing, before closing, not after.

Getting Around

Greely is a car village, and this guide will not pretend otherwise. Bank Street (Ottawa Road 31) is the spine — north through Findlay Creek and Riverside South to downtown in roughly 30 minutes; Mitch Owens Road runs west to Manotick and Barrhaven; the airport is 15 to 20 minutes. For transit, the honest sentence is a short one: there is no commuter bus. OC Transpo’s only service is Shopper Route 304, running Thursdays only, one trip each way, between the Osgoode–Metcalfe–Greely villages and Billings Bridge — a weekly shopper run, not a commute. School busing, through the Ottawa Student Transportation Authority, is the exception that matters to families. Price the drive into the decision, and Greely works; expect transit, and it does not.

Greely vs Metcalfe — the Twin Villages

No dedicated comparison page ranks for Greely vs Metcalfe today, which is strange — it is the first question half of south-Ottawa buyers ask. The two villages are twins of the old Township of Osgoode: they share Osgoode Township High School, the Metcalfe & District Hockey Association, the fall-fair culture (the Metcalfe Fair has run since 1856) and the Bank Street corridor, and their residents split their weeks across both villages and Manotick without thinking about it.

The differences are character and stock. Greely is the larger village — community estimates put it near 10,000 people (it has no census population centre of its own; Metcalfe’s counted 1,811 in 2021) — and the faster-growing one, built out in estate-lot subdivisions, lake communities and a golf-course community, with the grocery plaza on its main street. Its prices sit above Metcalfe’s, driven by lot size and custom builds. Metcalfe is the smaller, steadier village: the arena, the high school and the fairgrounds, a tighter heritage core, and the more accessible end of the twin-village market. Buyers who want land, lakes and new custom construction start in Greely; buyers who want the fair-town core and a lower entry price start in Metcalfe — and most end up touring both, which is why each village carries its own guide in the set below.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Greely part of Ottawa, or its own town?

Greely is a village inside the City of Ottawa — it became part of the city with the 2001 amalgamation and sits in Ward 20, Osgoode. It keeps its own village identity, community association and village boundary, but there is no separate Greely municipality. One practical consequence: most of the village is not on municipal water or sewers, so “inside Ottawa” does not mean “city-serviced” — see the servicing section of this guide.

Are Greely homes on municipal water and sewers?

Almost none are. With no municipal water or sewer system in the village, most Greely homes run on a private well and septic system. The exception is the communal servicing in the Shadow Ridge and Lakeland Meadows area, where a City of Ottawa communal system provides drinking water and wastewater service. Council has approved a developer-funded upgrade of the Shadow Ridge water plant, which has been operating at or near its rated limits — the upgrade is the unlock for that community’s held Phase-2 lots.

How big are the lots in Greely?

Lot size is the Greely signature. The estate subdivisions are built on half-acre to two-acre lots — Forest Lake Estates and Country Hill Estates at the generous end, the lake communities mostly at half an acre to an acre. The older village core is tighter, with lots around 100 by 150 feet on the original farm-village streets. Greely’s one different housing form is the Parkbridge land-lease cluster (Albion Woods and Albion Sun Vista), where residents own a manufactured home and lease the lot.

Is there a bus or commuter transit in Greely?

There is no commuter route, and buyers should plan around that honestly. The only OC Transpo service is Shopper Route 304, which runs on Thursdays only, one trip each way, linking Greely with Metcalfe, Osgoode and Billings Bridge. School busing is separate and runs through the Ottawa Student Transportation Authority for eligible students. In practice, Greely is a driving village — check the current Route 304 schedule on octranspo.com.

Which schools serve Greely?

Public-board families have Castor Valley Elementary (JK to Grade 8, the designated Early French Immersion school for the Greely and Metcalfe area) and Greely Elementary (JK to Grade 6) in the village, then Osgoode Township High School in Metcalfe for Grades 9 to 12. Catholic families have St. Mary School (JK to Grade 6, on Bank Street at the village’s northern edge), then St. Mark Catholic High School in Manotick. French-language schools sit outside the village. Catchments are set by your exact address and program — confirm any property in the board’s school locator before you buy.

How far is Greely from downtown Ottawa and the airport?

About 30 minutes to downtown in free-flowing traffic, up Bank Street (Ottawa Road 31) through Findlay Creek and Riverside South, or west on Mitch Owens Road toward Manotick and Barrhaven. Ottawa International Airport is roughly 15 to 20 minutes away. These are road-time estimates — test your own commute at your own hour before you buy.

What is being built in Greely now — and what is only proposed?

Being built and sold: Forest Lake Estates, Sunset Lakes Developments’ eighth community, is marketing Phase 1 lots through the developer, with custom builds by its partner builders; Country Hill Estates is releasing two-acre lots in its newest phase; and Quinn Farm lots continue to release. Only proposed: the next phase of Cedar Lakes — 71 lots on the Stagecoach Road lands south of the built phases — is in the City approvals process and is not selling, and the Emerald Estates name in the planning record is a studies-stage project beside the golf course, not a neighbourhood. This guide keeps the two lists separate on purpose.

Greely or Metcalfe — what is the real difference?

The twin villages of old Osgoode Township share a high school, a hockey association and a fair culture, but they sell different things. Greely is the bigger, faster-growing village: estate-lot subdivisions, lake communities, a golf-course community and the grocery plaza. Metcalfe is the smaller, steadier village: the arena, the high school and the fairgrounds, a tighter village core and generally more accessible prices. The full comparison is in this guide’s Greely vs Metcalfe section — and each village has its own guide in the set below.
