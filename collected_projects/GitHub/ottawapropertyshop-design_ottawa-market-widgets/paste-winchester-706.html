Living in Winchester — the Village Core

Daily life in Winchester runs on two streets. Main Street is the brick commercial core the village grew around the railway and the cheese trade — storefronts, restaurants and services in 19th-century brick, with Sweet Corner Park on the site of Aaron Sweet’s old store. One of those storefronts carries a story: William Bow’s 1888 store at St. Lawrence and Caleb Streets, built the year the village incorporated, is a restaurant today. St. Lawrence Street is the service spine: the township office at 636, the shared public building at 547 that holds the library branch, the fire station, the OPP detachment and a medical clinic, and the pharmacy-anchored medical building at 545 — directly across from the hospital.

Around that core, the residential streets are quiet and genuinely walkable: older homes on the streets near Main, postwar bungalows toward the hospital, newer bungalow subdivisions on the village’s north side. It is the rare small village where the errands of a normal week — school, library, doctor, pharmacy, arena — mostly happen inside a ten-minute walk of each other. What the village does not have is equally honest: no big-box retail, no high school, no transit. Those are drives — and the Getting to Ottawa section below prices them in kilometres, not adjectives.

A Short History — Bates’ Corners to the Township’s Capital

The village begins in 1835, when Benjamin Bates built a log cabin in uncut forest on the Nation River system and the crossroads took his name: Bates’ Corners. Squared timber was the first economy, floated out through the Ottawa and St. Lawrence Rivers to Quebec; M. F. Beach’s sawmill, built in 1856, made the timber trade the area’s first industry. In February 1855 a post office opened in William Bow’s general store at Main and Ottawa Streets — and Bow stayed on as postmaster for 57 years, until 1912, the longest-serving postmaster in Dundas County history.

The decisive year was 1884, when the railway arrived. For the first time the district’s farm produce could reach real markets, and the village took off: Winchester incorporated in 1888 (the same year the West Winchester Directory became the Winchester Press), and the Winchester Cheese Board was founded in 1894 to market the dairy output the railway now carried. The Press served the village for 132 years — surviving a 1921 fire that destroyed its Main Street office, chronicling everything, and printing its last edition on January 1, 2020. In August 1934, in the depths of the Depression, the village threw itself an Old Home Week and former residents came back from across Canada and the United States.

On January 1, 1998, the old Winchester and Mountain townships and the villages of Winchester and Chesterville amalgamated into the Township of North Dundas — and Winchester, the larger village, became its administrative centre, with the township office on St. Lawrence Street. The village gave up its separate council and kept everything that mattered: the hospital, the plant, the main street and the name over the door.

The Ault Story — How Winchester Became Dairy Country

Stand on Main Street and look south: a brick chimney lettered A-U-L-T stands over the rooftops. That chimney is the village’s whole economic history in one word. In 1891, John (Jack) Wesley Ault began producing cheese in the hamlet of Cass Bridge, a few minutes from the village. In 1932 he moved the operation into Winchester — and the plant grew into Ontario’s largest milk processing plant. Winchester was billed, without much argument, the Cheese Capital of Canada.

After Jack Ault’s death the company passed to Montreal’s Ogilvie Flour Mills; his grandson Sam Ault took the reins in 1964, and when John Labatt Limited bought the business in 1968, Sam built it into Canada’s largest dairy company — by 1973 turning out 12 million pounds of cheese, 15 million pounds of skim milk and 10 million pounds of butter a year, and by the mid-1980s holding roughly half of Ontario’s dairy market across 18 plants and some 2,690 employees. Labatt sold Ault to Parmalat in 1997; Lactalis took its controlling share in 2011. Today the same Winchester site runs as a Lactalis Canada plant, employing about 300 people and producing Black Diamond cheese — a national-scale food plant at the end of a village main street, still hiring, still anchoring.

Sam Ault’s other legacy is civic: his obituary credits him with helping bring Winchester its high school, its park, its curling club and its hockey arena — the arena still carries his name. And the village still throws the party. Dairyfest was founded in 1988 by a committee of local merchants, Joel Steele among them, to celebrate the village’s centennial year; it runs every August on volunteer power and marked its 35th edition in 2023. The Winchester Press called the village the Dairy Capital of Ontario. Both titles fit: the cheese built this place, and this place has never pretended otherwise. In September 2021 the village unveiled a mural honouring the Ault family — the chimney, it turns out, was only the first monument.

The Hospital — Winchester District Memorial Hospital

Most villages Winchester’s size are lucky to keep a clinic. Winchester has a hospital — and not by accident. In 1944, Dr. J. J. McKendry wrote to the Winchester Press arguing the district needed one. The community built it: the cornerstone was laid on October 4, 1948, and Winchester District Memorial Hospital officially opened on December 8, 1948, before a crowd of more than 3,000 people — a two-storey, 32-bed hospital for a village a fraction of that size. Its first patient was a 10-year-old boy in for minor surgery; its first baby arrived on Christmas Eve of that year.

The “Memorial” in the name is literal. At the opening, a scroll bearing the names of the district’s Second World War dead was erected in the hospital — the building itself is a war memorial, recorded in the National Inventory of Canadian Military Memorials, and in 2009 the Winchester Cenotaph was relocated to the hospital’s entrance. Care and remembrance share an address here, which says a great deal about the community that built both.

The hospital grew with the district — a south wing in 1960 took it from 35 to 89 beds, and it reached roughly 120 beds by the late 1970s. Then the community did it again: the Renewing the Vision campaign raised $15.1 million locally, and a brand-new WDMH opened on March 27, 2009, described as the most technologically advanced facility in rural Ontario, with CT scanning and digital mammography. A Community Care Building joined the campus in 2013 and doubled in 2016. Today the hospital offers:

  • 24/7 emergency department — minutes from any door in the village.

  • Inpatient medical and surgical care, day surgery and outpatient clinics.

  • Maternity and newborn care — in 2012, WDMH’s maternity program was the number one recommended program in Ontario.

  • Regional partnerships in cancer services and chronic kidney disease (dialysis), plus laboratory, pharmacy and diagnostic imaging.

  • A Centre of Excellence for Rural Health and Education — WDMH is a rural teaching hospital, training clinicians in the community it serves.

Beside the campus, at 533 Clarence Street, stands Dundas Manor — the only long-term care home in Dundas County, governed jointly with the hospital. A brand-new Dundas Manor is now being built: more than double the current home’s size, with room for 30 more residents organized into four “Homesteads,” backed by a community campaign that raised $15.1 million of its $18 million goal. For buyers, all of this changes the rural equation: retirees can age in the village they love, health-care workers can live where they work, and young families get an emergency department and a maternity ward without the city. It is the single biggest reason Winchester punches above its weight — and the first thing I point to when Ottawa buyers ask why this village, of all the villages, holds its value.

Farming Around Winchester

Winchester sits in flat, fertile St. Lawrence lowland, crossed by the South Nation River and East Castor River watersheds — land the county’s own history calls ideal dairy farming land. That is not scenery; it is the economy the village was built to serve. The Cheese Board of 1894, the Ault plant of 1932 and Dairyfest today are one story told three ways, and Winchester and Chesterville together were once promoted as the Dairy Product Centre of Canada. The township around the village is still a working mix of dairy and livestock farms and corn-and-soy cash crop land — which means a Winchester home comes with something no suburb can sell you: a landscape that is somebody’s livelihood, tended fields in every direction, and a main street whose biggest customer has always been the farm.

Homes & Neighbourhoods

The heritage core is the village the railway and the cheese trade built: brick and frame houses from the 1880s through the 1910s on the older streets, the limestone United Church of 1883, the Old Town Hall of 1904. These are character homes in the true sense — deep lots, mature trees, and a century of additions and adaptations to read carefully before you buy. The postwar streets around Louise Street and the hospital carry the village’s second layer: modest bungalows and two-storeys that have housed hospital staff, plant workers and teachers for generations — the backbone of the village’s resale market.

Winchester Township — the Ring Around the Village

The village is the hub, but it is not the whole market. District 707 — the former Winchester Township — wraps the village on every side, out to Marionville Road in the north, the Finch boundary in the east, the low ground along County Roads 5 and 31 in the south, and the County Road 13 line in the west. It is one of the oldest surveyed townships in Dundas County, and it still reads the way it was drawn: concession roads on a grid, hamlets where the crossroads earned them, and farmland in between that has been worked for six or seven generations.

The hamlets carry the township’s addresses. Morewood, in the northeast, is the township’s second village in everything but incorporation — its own main street, its own newer streets, and an easy run down County Road 13 or 43 to Winchester or Chesterville. Ormond, in the northwest along Ormond Road, is the quiet crossroads version: country lots and farmhouses within sight of the Marionville Road line. Winchester Springs, south of the village on the County Road 38 corridor, grew around its springs and its church and remains a loose, rural knot of homes. Nation Valley, in the southwest, takes its name from the South Nation River that winds past it. And Cass Bridge, on the river southwest of the village, is where this whole story started — Jack Ault’s first cheese factory stood there in 1891, before the plant, the chimney and the Cheese Capital title moved into Winchester.

The farm country is the township’s volume story. Between the hamlets, the concessions carry the working landscape the village was built to serve: dairy and livestock farms, corn-and-soy cash crop land, and the farmhouses, hobby farms and acreage homes that come with them. Township properties trade in a different grammar than village homes — lot frontage and acreage instead of lot size, barns and drivesheds as real value, well and septic as the default servicing, and land that can be a garden, a pasture or a business depending on who buys it. Every year the township’s sales include genuine working farms alongside the country homes, and that mix is exactly why this hub reports the village and the township together: a Winchester buyer is almost always shopping both, whether they planned to or not.

What the township asks of a buyer is respect for the homework. A country property is a small system — well, septic, heating, drainage, road frontage, and neighbours whose livelihood runs on the land beside you. Bought with open eyes, it is the best space-per-dollar equation in the counties south of Ottawa. The Servicing section below is written for exactly this purchase.

Servicing — the Winchester Homework

Winchester is a serviced village core wrapped in a rural township, and the line between the two is a lot line, not a guess. Before any purchase here:

  • Confirm the services for the exact lot with the Township of North Dundas — whether the property connects to municipal water and sewer or runs on a private system, and what connection charges or capacity fees apply. Never assume from the street name.

  • On a well and septic property — the norm on the township fringe — budget for a potability and bacteria water test, a flow check, and a septic inspection with the tank pumped and the bed assessed. A replacement bed is one of the largest cheques a rural buyer can inherit.

  • Heating history matters — oil, propane and electric heat are all common out here, and each carries a very different monthly reality through a Dundas County winter.

  • For heritage-age homes, look hard at foundation, wiring and insulation before conditions come off. Charm and knob-and-tube are frequent roommates in 1890s stock.

None of this is a reason to avoid Winchester — half the Ottawa valley lives happily on some version of it. It is a reason to buy with your eyes open, and it is standard practice in every offer I write out here.

Schools — Elementary in the Village, High School in Chesterville

The split is simple to remember: Winchester keeps its children for elementary school, and sends them ten minutes east for high school.

  • Winchester Public School (Upper Canada District School Board, Junior Kindergarten to Grade 6), 547 Louise Street South — founded in 1890. The original school burned in 1927 and was rebuilt on the same lot that same year; the school has anchored village family life ever since.

  • North Dundas District High School (UCDSB, Grades 7 to 12), on County Road 43 in Chesterville — opened in 1963 after fire destroyed Winchester High School the year before; the North Dundas Intermediate School (Grades 7 and 8) sits on its second floor. One bus ride, one school, for every Winchester teenager.

  • St. Mary’s Catholic School in Chesterville (Catholic District School Board of Eastern Ontario) is the Catholic elementary for the township.

  • French-language boards: assignments for Winchester addresses are address-specific and were not published as a general rule for this area — run your exact address through the French boards’ locators rather than relying on any summary, including this one.

For most Winchester families the school run is the public school around the corner and then a short bus ride down County Road 43 — but the pickup time is part of the house. Ask about it before you buy.

Churches & Community Life

Winchester United Church, 519 St. Lawrence Street — “The Old Limestone Lady of St. Lawrence Street,” built in the early 1880s (dated 1883) from local quarry stone. Photo: Cantons-de-l’Est, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

The village’s churches cover most of the map of Canadian faith: Winchester United (Methodist ministers were preaching in local homes here in the 1840s; its Sunday School Hall was begun in 1881, before the stone church rose beside it), St. Paul’s Presbyterian at 517 Main Street, Winchester Baptist at 486 Main Street East, the Southgate Church Winchester Campus at 539 Main Street, Harmony Community Church at 475 Centre Street, and Bethany Chapel at 557 Main Street. St. Clare’s Anglican parish serves the village, and Catholic families worship at St. Mary of the Presentation in Chesterville, shared with St. Daniel the Martyr in South Mountain — there is no Catholic church building inside the village itself.

The Old Town Hall, at 478 Main Street East, is the village’s other great room: built in 1904 for $5,000 and designated under the Ontario Heritage Act, it spent 55 years as the village library (1906 to 1961), 65 years holding the village jail — the cell bars survive — and a decade as OPP headquarters, before becoming home in 1996 to the Dundas County Players, whose 97-seat theatre still fills it. Add the Joel Steele Community Centre and Sam Ault Arena at 577 Main Street West, with the Winchester Centennial Pool on site and the Winchester Hawks on the ice, the library branch on St. Lawrence Street, 100 Club Park, Dairyfest in August and the North Dundas Parade of Lights every December, and this village of 2,400 keeps a fuller calendar than suburbs ten times its size. It also exports talent: Hockey Hall of Famer Larry Robinson was born in Winchester, as were NHL defenceman Slater Koekkoek and gospel singer George Beverly Shea.

Getting to Ottawa — and Reading the Map

Winchester exists because two roads cross. Highway 31 — Bank Street, continued south — runs north–south: Ottawa one way, Morrisburg and Highway 401 the other. County Road 43 runs east–west between Kemptville and Chesterville. County Road 3 (Ottawa Street) angles out of the village to the northwest. The working distances: downtown Ottawa about 50 kilometres, roughly 45 minutes; Ottawa’s southern suburbs about 30 minutes; Chesterville about 10 kilometres east; Kemptville about 25 kilometres west; Morrisburg and the 401 about 25 kilometres south; Russell and Embrun about half an hour north.

Winchester vs the Ottawa Run — and Its Village Neighbours

Buyers find Winchester from two directions. From Ottawa, the math is the draw: the price of a city townhouse buys a detached village home here, with a hospital, an elementary school and a main street in the bargain — the cost is a 45-minute highway commute and a high-school bus ride for the kids. From the other villages, the comparison is character: Russell is the polished township village closer to the city; Embrun is the bigger, francophone service hub; Osgoode is Ottawa’s own river village inside the city limits. Winchester is the working one — the hospital town, the dairy town, the county town — with the deepest history of the set and, on most days, the most house for the money.

Questions Buyers Ask About Winchester

Is Winchester, Ontario a good place to live?
For the right buyer, it is one of the best-value full-service villages south of Ottawa. You get a real main street, an in-village elementary school, an arena and pool, and a hospital with a 24/7 emergency department and a maternity program that was rated the most recommended in Ontario — at village prices well below the city. The honest trade-offs: high school is a bus ride to Chesterville, big shopping runs are a drive to Ottawa or Kemptville, there is no public transit, and daily life runs on a car. If that trade sounds fair, Winchester rewards it — the township grew steadily through the last census, and people who move here tend to stay. And if the village itself feels small, the township ring around it — Morewood, Ormond, Winchester Springs and the concession roads — offers the acreage version of the same trade.

What is the difference between buying in the village and in Winchester Township?
Same township, same taxes-and-services conversation, two different products. A village home (district 706) sits on a village lot inside the serviced core — municipal water and sewer, sidewalks, the school and the hospital a walk away. A township home (district 707) sits on a country lot or a farm parcel along the concession roads — Morewood, Ormond, Winchester Springs, Nation Valley, Cass Bridge country — usually on a private well and septic, with room for a shop, a barn or a second building, and a longer driveway in every sense. This guide and its live search cover both, because buyers cross-shop them constantly: the village sells convenience, the township sells space, and the homework section below applies twice as hard on the concession roads.

How far is Winchester from Ottawa?
About 50 kilometres — roughly 45 minutes by car to downtown in normal conditions, straight up Highway 31 (Bank Street). Ottawa’s southern suburbs are about half an hour. It is a single, straight highway run with no route-finding: Bank Street south out of the city becomes Highway 31 and arrives in the middle of the village. Winter adds its opinion, as it does everywhere in the valley.

What does having Winchester District Memorial Hospital in the village mean day to day?
It means the rural-health question most village buyers worry about is already answered. WDMH runs a 24/7 emergency department, inpatient medical and surgical care, day surgery, a maternity and newborn program, diagnostic imaging including CT, and partnerships in cancer care and dialysis — minutes from any front door in the village instead of a highway drive away. It is also one of the township’s anchor employers and a teaching hospital, and the new Dundas Manor long-term care home rising beside the campus makes Winchester a genuine health-care hub for this whole corner of the counties.

Why is there a chimney that says AULT over Main Street?
Because Winchester was dairy country’s capital. Jack Ault started making cheese at Cass Bridge in 1891 and moved the operation into Winchester in 1932; the plant became Ontario’s largest milk processor and the village was billed the Cheese Capital of Canada. The company grew into Canada’s largest dairy business, passed through Parmalat, and today the same site runs as a Lactalis Canada plant making Black Diamond cheese with about 300 employees. The lettered chimney from the old plant still stands over Main Street — the village’s landmark and its whole economic history in one word.

Which schools serve Winchester?
Winchester Public School (Upper Canada District School Board, Junior Kindergarten to Grade 6) is right in the village, at 547 Louise Street South — it has stood on its lot since 1890. Older students go to North Dundas District High School (Grades 7 to 12) in Chesterville, about ten minutes east, where the intermediate grades sit on the school’s second floor. Catholic elementary is St. Mary’s Catholic School in Chesterville (CDSBEO). French-language school assignment is address-specific, so confirm it with the French boards directly. And as always: confirm the schools assigned to an exact address with the board before you buy.

Is Winchester on municipal water and sewer?
Ask the question lot by lot — that is the honest answer, and it is one of the first checks on any purchase here. Winchester is a serviced village core wrapped in a rural township, so a village street and a concession road can be serviced very differently. Before you buy, confirm with the Township of North Dundas whether the exact lot connects to municipal water and sewer or runs on a private well and septic system, and budget the inspections accordingly — a water test and a septic inspection are standard practice on rural-fringe offers.

What is Dairyfest?
Winchester’s signature weekend, held every August. Local merchants founded it in 1988 to celebrate the village’s centennial — Dairyfest founder Joel Steele’s name is now on the community centre — and it has run on volunteer power ever since, marking its 35th edition in 2023. It is exactly what it sounds like: a working dairy town celebrating the industry that built it, in the middle of Main Street country.

Is Winchester growing?
Steadily. The Township of North Dundas grew about 4.5 percent between the 2016 and 2021 censuses, Winchester Meadows brought a 55-plus bungalow community to Ottawa Street, a second subdivision is in the draft-plan stage in the district, the medical-commercial building across from the hospital keeps drawing investment, and a brand-new long-term care home is being built beside the hospital campus. This is not a village being left behind — it is a village being chosen, at a pace it can absorb.
