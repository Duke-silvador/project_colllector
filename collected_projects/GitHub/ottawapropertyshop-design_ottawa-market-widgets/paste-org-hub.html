Who is involved in buying a house in Ottawa? Most people name two: their REALTOR® and their bank. By the time you hold your keys, more than a dozen organizations will have touched your purchase — and three different bodies stand behind your agent alone. Almost none of them will ever introduce themselves.

This guide follows one fictional Barrhaven resale — an illustrative example, not a real client — from the first showing to registration, and introduces every organization at the moment it enters the deal: who it is, what it actually does, and the one thing buyers most often get wrong about it. If you are weighing Ottawa houses for sale in Barrhaven or anywhere else in the capital, this is the map of everyone working on your purchase behind the scenes.

Part 1 — The People You Meet First, And The Three Bodies Behind Your Agent

Your agent is one person, but three separate organizations stand behind the business card — and confusing them is the most common misunderstanding in Ontario real estate.

RECO — the Real Estate Council of Ontario (reco.on.ca) is the regulator. It administers the Trust in Real Estate Services Act (TRESA), licenses — properly, registers — every salesperson, broker and brokerage in Ontario, runs the public registrant search, and enforces the rules, including how your deposit must be held. If an agent’s conduct is ever in question, RECO is the body with the power to act.

OREA — the Ontario Real Estate Association (orea.com) is the industry association. It writes the standard forms your offer is printed on, trains, and advocates for the profession. It regulates nobody and cannot discipline your agent — that is RECO’s job alone.

OREB — the Ottawa Real Estate Board (oreb.ca) is Ottawa’s local board: it runs the co-operative listing system Ottawa agents work in and publishes the city’s housing statistics. Membership is how an Ottawa agent gets full MLS® access and the local data behind pricing advice.

CREA — the Canadian Real Estate Association (crea.ca) owns the REALTOR® trademark and runs REALTOR.ca, the national showcase where your listing — or your future home — appears. Only members of the board system may call themselves a REALTOR®.

Three misconceptions, cleared up: Licensed and REALTOR® are not the same thing — registration is RECO’s; the REALTOR® mark is CREA’s, earned through board membership. OREA cannot discipline an agent, however official its forms look. And the board is not the regulator — OREB is a membership organization, not a government watchdog.

Part 2 — Where Ottawa Listings Actually Live Now: PropTx

When your agent searches for homes, books showings or pulls comparable sales, the screen in front of them is no longer an Ottawa-built system. Since November 25, 2024, OREB members have worked in PropTx (proptx.ca) — the platform owned by TRREB, the Toronto board, through its wholly owned subsidiary — using the Matrix and REALM™ tools on Ottawa data, feeding listings out to REALTOR.ca and the brokerage sites.

What the switch means for you is practical, not technical: Ottawa listings now sit inside the same system the much larger Toronto market uses, so a Barrhaven home is visible to a far wider pool of agents and buyers — while pricing, board statistics and local rules remain Ottawa’s own. What it does not do is change who regulates your agent (still RECO) or who publishes Ottawa’s numbers (still OREB).

Part 3 — The Money Chain: From The Bank Of Canada To Your Deposit

The Bank of Canada (bankofcanada.ca) sets the weather every mortgage lives in — its policy rate steers the variable rates and bond yields behind fixed ones. From there, your mortgage comes through one of two doors. A mortgage broker is licensed by FSRA, Ontario’s Financial Services Regulatory Authority (fsrao.ca), and shops many lenders for you. A bank or credit union lends its own money under federal supervision by OSFI, with the FCAC watching consumer fairness. Either way, federal rules apply the mortgage stress test — you must qualify at a rate above the one you will actually pay, a cushion built in on purpose.

Before anyone lends, the lender reads your file at a credit bureau — Equifax or TransUnion. And if your down payment is under 20%, a second organization joins quietly: a mortgage default insurer — CMHC, Sagen or Canada Guaranty. The lender chooses the insurer, you pay the premium, and the protection belongs to the lender, not to you. Insured mortgages require a purchase price under $1.5 million, and since December 15, 2024, first-time buyers and buyers of new construction can take a 30-year insured amortization.

The three insurances — the box that ends the confusion: Mortgage default insurance protects the lender if you default. Title insurance protects against covered title problems (Part 5). Home insurance protects the property itself — and your lender will require a policy before funding. Three different products, three different jobs. FSRA regulates the insurers licensed in Ontario.

Your deposit — the money you hand over with the offer — does not sit in your agent’s account or the seller’s hands. Under TRESA and RECO’s rules it is held in the listing brokerage’s trust account until closing (a full guide to deposits follows in this series). Federal programs meet you in the same budget: the Home Buyers’ Plan lets first-time buyers withdraw up to $60,000 from an RRSP, and the First Home Savings Account (FHSA) lets you save for the down payment itself. One corner of the money chain most buyers never see: syndicated mortgages and similar investment products sit under the Ontario Securities Commission — a different regulator for a different kind of risk.

Part 4 — The House Gets Checked, Valued And Proven

Photo: FEMA / Amanda Bicknell — Public Domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

A home inspector gives you a professional opinion on a home’s visible condition — structure, roof, heating, plumbing, moisture. Here is the fact that surprises every buyer: home inspectors are not licensed in Ontario. An Ontario licensing act was passed in 2017 but has never been brought into force, so the profession is self-governed through associations such as OAHI and OntarioACHI. Choose on qualifications, scope, insurance and a sample report — never on the word “licensed.”

An appraiser answers a different question: not the home’s condition, but its value, for the lender. Appraisers hold designations from the Appraisal Institute of Canada, and the lender — not you — orders and relies on the appraisal. A third valuation runs silently underneath: MPAC (mpac.ca) assesses every property in Ontario for municipal taxes, and its current assessments still rest on a January 1, 2016 valuation date — one reason your tax bill rarely matches today’s market.

Rural Ottawa branch — wells and septic: buy in Greely, Manotick, Osgoode or another rural community and two more desks join the deal. The Ottawa Septic System Office, run with the conservation authorities (rvca.ca), holds septic records going back to 1978 — a file search belongs in every rural purchase. The City of Ottawa tests well water, and the conservation authorities map floodplains and regulated land. A dedicated rural well-and-septic guide follows in this series.

The new-build fork: buy new construction and the cast changes. The HCRA (hcraontario.ca) licenses the builder; Tarion (tarion.com) backs the new-home warranty — two organizations since 2021, where one used to do both jobs. New homes also carry HST where resale homes do not — 13% in Ontario, a 5% federal part and an 8% provincial part.

The HST Rebates On A New Home

A stack of rebates now offsets that tax, each run by a different level of government:

  • First-time buyers get the deepest help. The federal first-time home buyers’ GST/HST rebate (Bill C-4, law since March 2026) returns 100% of the federal 5% on a new home up to $1 million — up to $50,000 back — phasing out by $1.5 million, for agreements signed on or after March 20, 2025. Ontario matches it on the provincial 8%, up to $80,000 more. Together, a first-time buyer can recover the entire 13% — up to $130,000.
  • Every new-home buyer, first-time or not, can claim the long-standing New Housing Rebates on a primary residence: federally, 36% of the GST paid, up to $6,300 (full on homes up to $350,000, gone by $450,000); from Ontario, 75% of the provincial part, up to $24,000, at any price.
  • And for one year — agreements signed April 1, 2026 to March 31, 2027 — Ontario’s enhanced rebate and its affordability payment can lift any eligible buyer’s relief to the full 13%, up to $130,000, first-time buyer or not.

When The Home Has Tenants

One cast member appears only when the home is rented — and it rewrites the deal. A sale never ends a tenancy: the tenant goes with the property, and only the Landlord and Tenant Board (tribunalsontario.ca/ltb) can order an eviction — enforced, if it comes to that, only by the sheriff. Rent law runs in two tiers: a unit first occupied on or before November 15, 2018 is capped by the provincial guideline (2.1% for 2026); a unit first occupied after that date has no cap. Either way, increases need 90 days’ notice and can happen only once every 12 months — and which tier a home sits in is a pricing fact every buyer of a tenanted home should know before offering.

If a purchaser genuinely intends to move in, the route is an N12 notice: 60 days to the end of a rental period, with one month’s rent compensation in the tenant’s hands before the termination date — or, under Ontario’s Bill 60 (in force September 21, 2026), 120 or more days’ notice with that compensation waived. A tenant who wants to stay can wait for a Board hearing on the landlord’s L2 application, which takes months. Showings follow strict rules — 24 hours’ written notice, between 8 a.m. and 8 p.m. — and the common alternative, “cash for keys,” is simply a voluntary deal, signed on a Form N11. Promising vacant possession in a contract does not override any of this, which is why experienced agents protect buyers with time, clauses and holdbacks. Part 5 — Closing Day, Where Everything Converges

On closing day, one professional stands at the centre of the web: your real estate lawyer, a member of the Law Society of Ontario (lso.ca). The lawyer searches title, arranges title insurance, registers the transfer, moves the money and hands over the keys — independently, for you, not for the seller or the lender.

Expect one moment that puzzles every buyer: the lawyer, like your agent, will ask for your ID and where your money came from. That is FINTRAC (fintrac-canafe.canada.ca), Canada’s anti-money-laundering regime — real estate professionals carry legal duties to identify clients and keep records. It is not suspicion; it is the system working as designed.

Two moments are worth separating, because buyers merge them: your purchase agreement binds you and the seller the moment it is signed and accepted — that is the contract. Registration at the Ontario land registry (OnLand / Teraview) is the later act that completes the transfer of legal title into your name. Your lawyer handles both, in that order.

Title Insurance — Its Own Word, Because It Earns It

Title insurance protects against covered problems with the property’s title and certain off-title risks — old liens, fraud, encroachments, errors in the public record — for a one-time premium arranged by your lawyer through providers such as First Canadian Title or Stewart Title. It comes in two policies: a lender policy, which your mortgage lender requires and which protects the lender, and an owner policy, which protects you for as long as you own the home. Ask for both, and know which one you are paying for. (A licensed surveyor’s plan answers the boundary questions title insurance only insures over — the two are complements, not substitutes.)

And the tax bill at the door: Ontario charges land transfer tax on the purchase, collected at registration. Eligible first-time buyers get a refund of up to $4,000. Unlike Toronto, Ottawa has no municipal land transfer tax — one tax, not two. The CRA (canada.ca) closes the loop: you report the sale of a principal residence when you sell, HST applies to new homes rather than resale, and the new-housing rebates in Part 4 run through its forms.

Part 6 — After The Sale, Your Home Becomes The Data

The day after closing, your purchase joins the statistics. OREB publishes Ottawa’s monthly numbers, CREA rolls them into the national picture, and Statistics Canada tracks the wider market. Read those headlines carefully: an “average price” moves with which homes happened to sell that month, not just what homes are worth — benchmark thinking beats headline thinking, and it is why local, street-level advice still outperforms a national average.

Step back and the pattern is the point. The complexity is the protection. Every organization in this guide exists because some part of a home purchase is too important to leave to trust alone — your licence, your deposit, your title, your money, your home.

Who protects what? RECO → your agent’s registration and conduct · Mortgage default insurance → the lender · Title insurance → covered title risks · A home inspection → an opinion on visible condition — not a warranty · The land registry → the official record of who owns what.

Questions Buyers Ask About Who Is Involved In Buying A House In Ottawa

Does OREA regulate my agent? No. OREA is the industry association behind the standard forms. Your agent is regulated and registered by RECO, and RECO’s public search confirms any agent’s status.

Does CMHC insurance protect me if I can’t pay? No — mortgage default insurance protects the lender. You pay the premium; the lender holds the protection. Your own safety nets are the stress test, your budget, and home insurance.

Are home inspectors licensed in Ontario? No. Ontario’s licensing act passed in 2017 but was never brought into force. Inspectors are self-governed through associations like OAHI and OntarioACHI — choose on qualifications, scope and insurance.

Who holds my deposit? The listing brokerage, in its trust account, under TRESA and RECO’s rules, until closing — never the seller personally and never your agent.

What is PropTx, and why did Ottawa switch? PropTx is the listing platform OREB members have used since November 25, 2024, owned by the Toronto board’s subsidiary. Ottawa’s data and statistics stayed Ottawa’s; the software — and the exposure — got bigger.

Is there a municipal land transfer tax in Ottawa? No. Ottawa buyers pay the Ontario land transfer tax only — Toronto is the Ontario city with a second, municipal tax.
