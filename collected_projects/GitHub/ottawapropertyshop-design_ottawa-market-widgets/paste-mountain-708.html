Living in Mountain Township — Villages and Concessions

Daily life in the township runs out of South Mountain. The village holds the machinery of a normal week: Nationview Public School on County Road 1, St. Daniel the Martyr Catholic Church on Maple Street, the fairgrounds and the Agricultural Hall on Lough Road, and the village store run. Up County Road 1, the hamlet of Mountain is the smaller, quieter sibling — and the orchard’s home. Hallville to the west and Inkerman to the east, toward the South Nation River, are crossroads communities with their own churches and halls, and Harmony, just outside South Mountain, is where the township’s newest homes are rising.

Between the villages, the concessions are the township: straight gravel and paved farm roads, century farmhouses behind maples, newer country homes on severed lots, working farms and bush lots in between. What the township does not have is equally honest: the hospital and the township office are in Winchester, the bigger service town is Kemptville, and there is no public transit — this is car country by design. Those are drives, and the Getting to Ottawa section below prices them in kilometres, not adjectives.

A Short History — the Bishop’s Township

The name arrived before the settlers did. In 1798, John Graves Simcoe, the first Lieutenant Governor of Upper Canada, organized and named the township for Jacob Mountain, the first Anglican Bishop of Quebec — which is why a township of flat, fertile farmland carries a mountain’s name to this day. The township he named was a surveyor’s grid of 57,778 acres on the upper reaches of the South Nation country, waiting for the farm families who would spend the next century turning it into some of the most productive agricultural land in Dundas County.

The building began in earnest in 1868 to 1872, when John Smirle and James Hyndman raised the first sawmills. The post offices tell the settlement story in their own order: South Mountain in 1851, Hallville in 1873 and Mountain in 1888. The railway arrived in September 1887, and the Mountain station became a shipping point for the township’s produce — cheese, livestock, milk and eggs went out by rail, and the villages grew around the stations and the mills. A century later, on January 1, 1998, the old Mountain and Winchester townships and the villages of Winchester and Chesterville amalgamated into the Township of North Dundas. Mountain Township gave up its separate council and kept everything that mattered: the fair, the orchard, the school and the name.

Mountain Orchards — the Township’s Signature Stop

If the township has a front porch, it is an orchard. Mountain Orchards, at 10175 Clark Road in the hamlet of Mountain, grows on land that has been in apples for nearly a century: John Allen planted the first trees in 1932, grafted from the original McIntosh trees at Dundela, some 30 kilometres away — the same township-county soil that produced the most famous apple in Canada. The Gibson family expanded the orchard to 50 acres, built the first cold storage, and in 1970 started the first pick-your-own apple operation in Eastern Ontario. The Lyall and Hobson families bought the orchard in the spring of 1973, knowing almost nothing about growing apples, and have run it for five decades since.

Today the orchard carries more than 10,000 trees — McIntosh, Lobo, Cortland, Silken, Honeycrisp, Spartan, Ambrosia, Golden Delicious and Eden among them — with pick-your-own from late summer into fall, wagon rides, two corn mazes, forest trails, a straw jump, fresh cider and a bakery. It is the rare farm that is also a regional institution: in apple season, half of Ottawa seems to find its way down Clark Road. Homes & Neighbourhoods

South Mountain is the township’s village product: older brick and frame homes on the village streets around the school and the churches, postwar family homes, and the fairgrounds at the edge of the village on Lough Road — the walkable core of a rural township, at price points Ottawa buyers cross the city’s edge to find. Mountain, up County Road 1, is the smaller hamlet version: a handful of streets, the orchard at its door, and country lots beyond. Harmony, just outside South Mountain, is the township’s growth edge — a run of new detached homes on generous severed lots, the subdivision product translated into country grammar: bigger lots, no sidewalks, and a well and septic under every lawn.

Servicing — the Mountain Township Homework

Mountain Township is rural construction all the way down, and the homework is the price of the space. Before any purchase here:

  • Confirm the services for the exact lot with the Township of North Dundas — whether the property connects to any municipal service or runs fully private. Never assume from the village name or the street.

  • On a well and septic property — the norm across the township — budget for a potability and bacteria water test, a flow check, and a septic inspection with the tank pumped and the bed assessed. A replacement bed is one of the largest cheques a rural buyer can inherit.

  • Heating history matters — oil, propane, wood and electric heat are all common out here, and each carries a very different monthly reality through a Dundas County winter.

  • If you are building, price the full rural checklist before the offer: severance consent where a lot is being created, zoning (much of the township is agricultural), minimum-distance separation from livestock barns, a perc test and septic design, hydro connection, and the driveway entrance permit. On a new build, add the builder’s record and warranty coverage to the same list.

None of this is a reason to avoid Mountain Township — half the Ottawa valley lives happily on some version of it. It is a reason to buy with your eyes open, and it is standard practice in every offer I write out here.

Schools — Nationview to North Dundas District High School

This is the fact that organizes family life in the township, and the reason this ring of guides is built the way it is: if you buy a home here, your younger child starts at Nationview Public School in South Mountain, and your older one finishes at North Dundas District High School in Chesterville — one path, two villages, and the same high school the whole North Dundas ring attends.

  • Nationview Public School (Upper Canada District School Board), 3045 County Road 1, South Mountain — Kindergarten to Grade 6, English and French Immersion, home of the Knights. The school opened in 1971 and is the township’s own elementary school, in the village, for every township child.

  • North Dundas District High School (UCDSB), 12835 County Road 43, Chesterville — Grades 7 to 12, home of the Panthers, about 500 students. The school opened in 1963 after fire destroyed Winchester High School the year before, and the North Dundas Intermediate School (Grades 7 and 8) sits on its second floor. Its Studio 138 STEAM space — film, photography, design and a makerspace — gives a village high school a program list most city schools would envy. From South Mountain it is one village east; the bus run is part of the township’s daily rhythm.

  • Catholic schools: Catholic families in the township worship at St. Daniel the Martyr, right in South Mountain. Catholic elementary schooling runs through the Catholic District School Board of Eastern Ontario, and the assignment for a township address is address-specific — run your exact address through the board’s school locator rather than relying on any summary, including this one.

  • French-language boards: assignments for Mountain Township addresses are likewise address-specific — confirm them with the French boards directly.

Read the path the way a family lives it: kindergarten starts in your own village, at a school of a few hundred children where the staff know every family, and high school is a short bus ride to the school the whole ring shares — the same building whose story anchors the Chesterville guide. Where your little one goes to primary school and where their older sister goes to secondary is the question every buying family actually asks, and in Mountain Township the answer is Nationview, then North Dundas.

Churches & Community Life

The township’s churches carry its history in stone and brick. St. Daniel the Martyr Catholic Church, at 10606 Maple Street in South Mountain, was established in 1849 — and when fire destroyed the church on September 1, 2011, the parish rebuilt it, rising from the ashes to reopen in 2013; it is served today as a mission of St. Mary of the Presentation in Chesterville. A block away, South Mountain United Church stands on Main Street, part of the Hallville–South Mountain pastoral charge that links the township’s United congregations. Around them, the community calendar is the real institution: the South Mountain Fair every August — the Mountain Township Agricultural Society, founded in 1892, draws more than 10,000 guests to the fairgrounds on Lough Road, where the Agricultural Hall hosts the township’s gatherings year-round — the community halls in Mountain, Hallville and Inkerman, and the orchard’s own season of wagon rides and corn mazes each fall.

Getting to Ottawa — and Reading the Map

Mountain Township sits between two corridors: County Road 43 runs along its northern edge — Winchester about ten minutes northeast, then Highway 31 (Bank Street) straight north into Ottawa — and Kemptville lies about 15 minutes west, with Highway 416 beyond it. South, the concession roads run down to Morrisburg and Highway 401 in about 25 minutes. The working distances are: downtown Ottawa about an hour by car; Kemptville a quarter hour; Chesterville about twenty minutes east.

Questions Buyers Ask About Mountain Township

Is Mountain Township, Ontario a good place to live?
For the buyer who wants real country with a working safety net, it is one of the strongest townships south of Ottawa. You get space — farmhouses, acreage, new homes on big lots — plus the things rural buyers worry about losing: an elementary school in South Mountain, the district high school one village east, an orchard that is a destination in itself, a fair that has run since 1892, and Kemptville and Winchester inside a quarter hour. The honest trade-offs: daily life runs on a car, most homes run on a well and septic, bigger shopping is a drive, and there is no public transit. If that trade sounds fair, Mountain Township rewards it — families here put down roots measured in generations.

Which schools serve Mountain Township?
One clear public path. Nationview Public School (Upper Canada District School Board, 3045 County Road 1 in South Mountain) takes children from Kindergarten to Grade 6, in English and French Immersion. From there, students go to North Dundas District High School (UCDSB, 12835 County Road 43 in Chesterville) for Grades 7 to 12 — the same high school the whole North Dundas ring attends. Catholic elementary schooling runs through the Catholic District School Board of Eastern Ontario, and French-language school assignment for township addresses is address-specific, so confirm both with the boards directly. And as always: confirm the schools assigned to an exact address with the board before you buy.

How far is Mountain Township from Ottawa?
Closer than it feels. South Mountain, the township’s main village, is 52 kilometres straight-line from downtown Ottawa — roughly an hour by car in normal conditions, either west through Winchester and straight up Highway 31 (Bank Street), or north through Kemptville onto Highway 416. Kemptville is about 15 minutes west, Winchester about ten minutes northeast, and Morrisburg and Highway 401 about 25 minutes south. Winter adds its opinion, as it does everywhere in the valley.

What is Mountain Orchards?
The township’s signature stop, at 10175 Clark Road in the hamlet of Mountain. The first apple trees were planted in 1932, grafted from the original McIntosh trees at Dundela; the Gibson family expanded the orchard and started what became the first pick-your-own operation in Eastern Ontario in 1970; and the Lyall and Hobson families bought it in 1973 and run it still. Today there are more than 10,000 trees — McIntosh, Lobo, Cortland, Silken, Honeycrisp and more — plus a corn maze, wagon rides, forest trails, a bakery and fresh cider. I filmed a family visit there; the video is in this guide. In apple season, half of Ottawa seems to find its way down Clark Road.

What is the South Mountain Fair?
The township’s biggest weekend, run by the Mountain Township Agricultural Society — founded in 1892 — at the fairgrounds on Lough Road in South Mountain, every August. Midway, rodeo, agricultural exhibits, entertainment and camping, drawing more than 10,000 guests across the weekend. In farm country the fair is not nostalgia; it is the industry’s annual report, delivered as a party, and South Mountain’s has been delivered for well over a century.

Are homes in Mountain Township on wells and septic systems?
Mostly, yes — that is the normal construction on the concession roads, and even in the villages the answer is lot by lot. Before you buy, confirm with the Township of North Dundas whether the exact property connects to any municipal service or runs fully private, and on a well and septic property budget for a potability and bacteria water test, a flow check, and a septic inspection with the tank pumped and the bed assessed. None of this is unusual out here — it is simply the homework, and it is standard practice on every rural offer I write.

What is the difference between Mountain Township, Mountain and South Mountain?
Three names, three different things. Mountain Township is the whole former township — 234 square kilometres of concession roads in North Dundas, district 708, and the subject of this guide. South Mountain is its main village: the school, the fairgrounds, the churches and the store. Mountain is a smaller hamlet up County Road 1, home to Mountain Orchards. Add the crossroads communities of Hallville and Inkerman and the newer streets at Harmony, and you have the township’s settlement map.

Why is it called Mountain Township if there are no mountains?
There are none — the name is a person, not a landform. The township was organized and named in 1798 by John Graves Simcoe, the first Lieutenant Governor of Upper Canada, for Jacob Mountain, the first Anglican Bishop of Quebec. The township kept the bishop’s name through two centuries of farming, and kept it again in 1998 when it amalgamated into the Township of North Dundas. Buyers expecting a ski hill should adjust their expectations to excellent apple country instead.
