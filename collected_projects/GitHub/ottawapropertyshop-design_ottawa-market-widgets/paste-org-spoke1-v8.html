Who regulates real estate agents in Ontario? Ask a room of home buyers and most will name the wrong organization — because three bodies with nearly identical names stand behind every agent in this province, and each one does a completely different job. One registers and disciplines your agent. One writes the forms your offer is printed on. One runs Ottawa’s board and publishes this city’s housing numbers. Mixing them up is more than a trivia error: when something matters — a complaint, a deposit, a question about a contract — each problem has exactly one right door to knock on.

This guide sorts the three out, plus the fourth name that rides along: CREA. It expands the first part of my map of who is involved in buying a house in Ottawa — the full cast of organizations behind one home purchase.

The Short Answer

RECO

The regulator. Registers every salesperson, broker and brokerage in Ontario, enforces the law, and is the only body that can discipline your agent.

OREA

The provincial association. Writes the standard forms, trains, and advocates for the profession. Regulates nobody.

OREB

Ottawa’s local board. A membership organization: MLS® access, local rules for members, and the city’s official housing statistics.

CREA

The national association. Owns the REALTOR® trademark and runs REALTOR.ca.

RECO — The Regulator With The Power To Act

RECO — the Real Estate Council of Ontario (reco.on.ca) is a delegated administrative authority, accountable to the Ontario government. It administers the Trust in Real Estate Services Act (TRESA) — the law, substantially updated on December 1, 2023, that governs how real estate services are delivered in this province — and its Code of Ethics.

What RECO actually does, in the order a buyer meets it:

  • It registers everyone. Every salesperson, broker and brokerage trading in real estate in Ontario must be registered with RECO. The everyday word is “licensed,” but the legal term is registered — and registration is RECO’s alone. Nobody is registered by OREA, by a board, or by a brokerage.

  • It lets you check before you commit. RECO’s free public registrant search confirms whether the agent in front of you is registered, with which brokerage, and whether their registration carries conditions or a disciplinary history. Two minutes there is the cheapest due diligence in the whole transaction.

  • It sets the conduct rules. TRESA and the Code of Ethics govern representation, disclosure, advertising, and how client money is handled — including the rule that your deposit is held in a brokerage trust account, never in an agent’s own hands.

  • It runs the mandatory insurance program every registrant pays into, which includes consumer deposit protection, errors-and-omissions coverage and commission protection — a safety net standing behind the deposit you hand over with an offer.

  • It enforces. RECO investigates complaints, and it can act: conditions, suspensions and revocations of registration, and discipline for breaches of the Code of Ethics. If an agent’s conduct is ever in question, this is the only body on this page with the power to do something about it.

One boundary worth knowing: RECO is funded by registrant fees, not by consumers and not by board dues — and it regulates registrants, not the associations below. Membership in OREA or OREB is not registration, and registration is not membership. An agent holds both, from different bodies, for different reasons.

OREA — The Association Behind The Forms

OREA — the Ontario Real Estate Association (orea.com), founded in 1922, is the provincial industry association. If RECO is the referee, OREA is the league office — except that a league office, too, cannot hand out penalties to players.

OREA’s real footprint in your transaction is paper. It develops and maintains the standard forms used in most Ontario deals — the Agreement of Purchase and Sale your offer is printed on, listing agreements, buyer representation agreements. That standardization is a genuine consumer protection of a quieter kind: a buyer in Ottawa and a seller in Kingston sign substantially the same documents, with the same clauses, schedules and protections, instead of whatever contract an individual brokerage might invent. OREA also delivers professional development and advocates for the profession at Queen’s Park — on housing supply, on TRESA reform, on the issues its members vote to pursue.

What OREA cannot do follows from what it is: it regulates nobody and cannot discipline your agent, however official its forms look. A complaint about an agent’s conduct sent to OREA is a letter sent to the wrong building — it belongs at RECO. Membership dues, for the record, flow to OREA through the local boards; you will never be billed by it as a consumer.

OREB — Ottawa’s Board, Ottawa’s Numbers

OREB — the Ottawa Real Estate Board (oreb.ca) is this city’s local board: a not-for-profit association of more than 4,000 Ottawa-area REALTOR® members. Membership is how an Ottawa agent gets full MLS® access — the co-operative listing system every local search, showing and comparable sale runs through, operated since November 25, 2024 on the PropTx platform.

For buyers and sellers, OREB’s most visible product is the truth about this market: it publishes Ottawa’s official monthly housing statistics — sales counts, benchmark and average prices, inventory — the local numbers that pricing decisions should actually rest on. When a national headline announces what “Ottawa prices” did last month, check it against OREB’s own release; the national figure is often a Toronto-weighted average wearing an Ottawa label.

OREB sets and enforces board and MLS® rules for its members — listing practices, data accuracy, cooperation between brokerages. That is a real rulebook, but it is a membership rulebook, not the law: the law is TRESA, and its referee is RECO. Ottawa’s board landscape is itself in transition — in 2025 OREB members voted in favour of amalgamation discussions with neighbouring Eastern Ontario boards — but the division of labour on this page does not change with the letterhead.

CREA — The Name On The Sign

CREA — the Canadian Real Estate Association (crea.ca) is the national association, and it owns the two marks consumers see most: REALTOR® and MLS®. It also runs REALTOR.ca, the national showcase where most Ottawa buyers actually browse homes, fed by the board systems.

The distinction that ties this whole guide together: registered and REALTOR® are two different statuses from two different bodies. Registration is law — RECO’s. REALTOR® is a trademarked membership, held through the three-way structure of local board, provincial association and CREA, and it binds the member to the REALTOR® Code on top of the law. Most Ottawa salespeople hold both — but only one of the two is what makes it legal for them to trade, and only one of them can take that right away.

Questions Buyers Ask About Who Regulates Real Estate Agents In Ontario

Is my agent licensed by OREA or the Ottawa board? Neither. Your agent is registered by RECO — that is the legal permission to trade in real estate in Ontario. OREA and OREB are associations the agent belongs to; membership is not the licence.

Who do I complain to about an agent? RECO. It investigates complaints about registrants and can impose conditions, suspensions, revocations and discipline. OREA and OREB can enforce their own membership and MLS® rules, but neither can touch an agent’s registration.

Is every registered agent a REALTOR®? No. REALTOR® is CREA’s trademark, earned through board membership. Registration (RECO) is the legal requirement; the REALTOR® mark is a membership on top of it.

Who sets the commission I pay? None of these organizations. Commissions are negotiable between you and your brokerage — no body on this page fixes rates.

Who protects my deposit? The system RECO enforces: your deposit is held in the listing brokerage’s trust account under TRESA, and RECO’s mandatory insurance program includes consumer deposit protection.
