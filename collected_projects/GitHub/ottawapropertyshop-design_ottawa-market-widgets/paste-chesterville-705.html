Living in Chesterville — the Village Core

Daily life in Chesterville runs on the cross the village was built around: Main Street and Queen Street through the core, King Street carrying some of its finest heritage homes, and County Road 43 passing along the southern edge. The machinery of a normal week is all inside the boundary — the two elementary schools and the high school, the Chesterville and District Arena at 153 Queen Street, the public pool at 1 William Street, the Nelson LaPrade Centre at 9 William Street for community events, the Community Hall at 1 Mill Street, and the fairgrounds for the biggest weekend of the year. The Chesterville Record, publishing since 1894, still lands in mailboxes every week — a village that reads its own news in print is a village that knows itself.

The streets themselves are quiet and genuinely walkable: heritage brick and frame homes near the core, postwar family streets around the schools, newer homes at the edges where the village is still growing. What the village does not have is equally honest: the hospital and the township office are in Winchester, bigger shopping runs are drives to Winchester, Kemptville or Ottawa, and there is no public transit. A Short History — Armstrong’s Mills to Chesterville

The village begins on the river. In the early 1800s Thomas Armstrong built saw and grist mills on the South Nation River where Chesterville now stands, and the settlement took his name: Armstrong’s Mills. Mills meant a millpond, a dam, a road to the river — and a village. When a post office opened in 1845, it was given the name Winchester. That choice caused thirty years of quiet chaos, because another Winchester — today’s hub village, ten minutes west — carried the same name in the same county, and mail, freight and travellers sorted themselves out as best they could.

The fix came with the telegraph. In 1875 the settlement was renamed Chesterville, for Chester T. Casselman, its first telegraph operator — a working man’s name for a working village. The village incorporated in 1890, and in 1894 The Chesterville Record began publishing; it has not stopped since. Through it all the village kept its shape: a mill and market village for the farm country around it, on the road between Winchester and Finch, with the river doing the heavy work at the bottom of the street.

The Chesterville and District Heritage Centre, 14 Victoria Street — built in 1867 as the village town hall, and since a fire hall, court house, jail, church and movie theatre. Photo: Cantons-de-l’Est, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

One building tells the whole story: the former town hall at 14 Victoria Street, built in 1867 — before Confederation’s ink was dry in this part of the province — served the village in turn as town hall, fire hall, court house, jail, church and movie theatre, and stands today as the Chesterville and District Heritage Centre. On January 1, 1998, the old Winchester and Mountain townships and the villages of Winchester and Chesterville amalgamated into the Township of North Dundas. Chesterville gave up its separate council and kept everything that mattered: the high school, the fair, the river and the name.

The South Nation River — the Village’s Second Main Street

The South Nation River at Chesterville — the river the mills were built on, still running past the village. Photo: P199, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Chesterville exists because of this river. The South Nation rises far to the south and winds through the village on its way to the Ottawa River, and everything about the settlement’s first century ran on it: Armstrong’s mills, the millpond, the farms laid out along its banks. Today the river is the village’s green edge — the waterfront terrace hosts Art on the Waterfront each June, an outdoor art show that has run since 2011, and the banks give the village a second, quieter main street of grass and water. River villages age differently than road villages: the water keeps them honest, and keeps them beautiful.

Servicing — the Chesterville Homework

Chesterville is a compact serviced village wrapped in a rural township, and the line between the two is a lot line, not a guess. Before any purchase here:

  • Confirm the services for the exact lot with the Township of North Dundas — whether the property connects to municipal water and sewer or runs on a private system, and what connection charges or capacity fees apply. Never assume from the street name.

  • On a well and septic property — the norm just outside the village edge — budget for a potability and bacteria water test, a flow check, and a septic inspection with the tank pumped and the bed assessed. A replacement bed is one of the largest cheques a rural buyer can inherit.

  • Heating history matters — oil, propane and electric heat are all common out here, and each carries a very different monthly reality through a Dundas County winter.

  • For heritage-age homes, look hard at foundation, wiring and insulation before conditions come off. Charm and knob-and-tube are frequent roommates in 1890s stock.

None of this is a reason to avoid Chesterville — half the Ottawa valley lives happily on some version of it. It is a reason to buy with your eyes open, and it is standard practice in every offer I write out here.

Schools — the Whole Path Inside One Village

This is the fact that organizes everything else about Chesterville, and the reason this ring of guides is built the way it is: if you buy a home here, your younger child starts at Chesterville Public School and your older one finishes at North Dundas District High School — and both schools are inside the same village. In most of the counties, the high school is a bus ride to another town. Here, the district’s high school is Chesterville’s own.

  • Chesterville Public School (Upper Canada District School Board), 38 College Street — Kindergarten to Grade 6, English and French Immersion both offered, home of the Coyotes. One school, both official-language tracks, for every village child.

  • North Dundas District High School (UCDSB), 12835 County Road 43 — Grades 7 to 12, home of the Panthers, about 500 students. The school opened in 1963 after fire destroyed Winchester High School the year before, and the North Dundas Intermediate School (Grades 7 and 8) sits on its second floor. Its Studio 138 STEAM space — film, photography, design and a makerspace — gives a village high school a program list most city schools would envy.

  • St. Mary Catholic School (Catholic District School Board of Eastern Ontario), 37 Main Street — Junior Kindergarten to Grade 6, with French Immersion and Core French streams.

  • French-language boards: assignments for Chesterville addresses are address-specific — run your exact address through the French boards’ locators rather than relying on any summary, including this one.

Read the path the way a family lives it: the same high school serves the whole ring — Winchester’s children bus here from the hub village, Nationview’s come from Mountain Township — while Chesterville’s own students walk. That is why the guides in this ring are organized by school catchments first: where your little one goes to primary school and where their older sister goes to secondary is the question every buying family actually asks, and in Chesterville the answer is one village, start to finish.

Churches & Community Life

St. Mary of the Presentation Catholic Church, 10 Church Street — the village’s stone church. Photo: Cantons-de-l’Est, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

The village’s churches anchor its corners the way they have for a century and a half: St. Mary of the Presentation Catholic Church at 10 Church Street, its stone tower over the core; St. Andrew’s Presbyterian Church at 34 Mill Street; and Trinity United Church — the village’s church map is small, old and still working. Around them, the community calendar is the real institution: the Chesterville Fair each summer, Art on the Waterfront in June, the township-wide Meet Me on Main Street in July, hockey and community sport at the arena on Queen Street, the summer pool on William Street, and the Nelson LaPrade Centre hosting the weddings, meetings and gatherings in between. A village of 1,500 that sustains a fair since 1932, a newspaper since 1894 and a high school of 500 is not coasting on its history — it is still making it.

Getting to Ottawa — and Reading the Map

Chesterville sits on County Road 43, the east–west spine of North Dundas: Winchester and Highway 31 about 10 kilometres west, Finch about 13 kilometres east. North–south, the village’s own roads run out to the concessions — and the working distances are: downtown Ottawa about 55 kilometres, roughly an hour; Winchester about ten minutes; Morrisburg and Highway 401 about 25 minutes south; Russell and Embrun about half an hour north.

Questions Buyers Ask About Chesterville

Is Chesterville, Ontario a good place to live?
For the right buyer, it is one of the most complete small villages in the counties. You get the whole school path inside one village — elementary and high school both — a fair that has run since 1932, an arena, a pool, a river at the end of the street and a weekly newspaper older than most Canadian towns, at village prices well below Ottawa. The honest trade-offs: the hospital and the township office are ten minutes west in Winchester, bigger shopping is a drive, there is no public transit, and daily life runs on a car. If that trade sounds fair, Chesterville rewards it — families who move here tend to stay, and the village keeps adding streets at its edges without losing the core that makes it itself.

Which schools serve Chesterville?
The whole public path sits inside the village. Chesterville Public School (Upper Canada District School Board, 38 College Street) takes children from Kindergarten to Grade 6, in English and French Immersion. North Dundas District High School (UCDSB, 12835 County Road 43) takes Grades 7 to 12 — the same high school Winchester’s teenagers bus to, except Chesterville’s students are already here. Catholic elementary is St. Mary Catholic School (CDSBEO, 37 Main Street, Junior Kindergarten to Grade 6). French-language school assignment is address-specific, so confirm it with the French boards directly. And as always: confirm the schools assigned to an exact address with the board before you buy.

How far is Chesterville from Ottawa?
About 55 kilometres — roughly an hour by car to downtown in normal conditions. The run is simple: County Road 43 west to Winchester, then Highway 31 (Bank Street) straight north into the city. Ottawa’s southern suburbs are about 40 minutes. Winter adds its opinion, as it does everywhere in the valley, but there is no route-finding in this commute — it is two roads, both of them straight.

What is ten minutes west in Winchester?
The rest of the township’s machinery. Winchester is the hub of this ring: Winchester District Memorial Hospital with its 24/7 emergency department, the Township of North Dundas office, the township’s main commercial street — and Chesterville’s sister village, with its own guide on this site. The two villages work as a pair: Chesterville holds the high school and the fair, Winchester holds the hospital and the township office, and County Road 43 joins them in about ten minutes. Most families here use both, every week, without thinking about it.

What is the Chesterville Fair?
The village’s signature weekend, run by the Chesterville and District Agricultural Society since 1932 — and a fair or market day in some form has been held here since 1862. It lands in the middle of summer at the fairgrounds: a tractor pull, a demolition derby, horse and cattle shows, a midway and grandstands full of neighbours. In dairy and farm country like North Dundas, the fair is not nostalgia — it is the industry’s annual report, delivered as a party. The village also hosts Art on the Waterfront each June and joins the township-wide Meet Me on Main Street in July.

Is Chesterville on municipal water and sewer?
Ask the question lot by lot — that is the honest answer, and it is one of the first checks on any purchase here. Chesterville is a compact serviced village, but the village edge and the concession roads just outside it can be serviced very differently. Before you buy, confirm with the Township of North Dundas whether the exact lot connects to municipal water and sewer or runs on a private well and septic system, and budget the inspections accordingly — a water test and a septic inspection are standard practice on rural-fringe offers.

What is the difference between buying in the village and in the township around it?
Chesterville the village (district 705) is an enclave: its own streets, its own lots, the schools and the fairgrounds inside the boundary. Around it on every side sits the Winchester Township ring (district 707) — concession roads, farmhouses, acreage and hamlets like Morewood and Nation Valley — which this site’s Winchester hub guide covers, with its own live search. Buyers cross-shop the two constantly: the village sells the walkable core and the schools at the door, the township sells space. The homework section below applies either way, and twice as hard on the concession roads.

Why is it called Chesterville?
The village began as Armstrong’s Mills, named for Thomas Armstrong, who built saw and grist mills on the South Nation River where the village now stands. When the post office opened in 1845 it took the name Winchester — which collided with the other Winchester down the road, and the confusion lasted thirty years. In 1875 the settlement was renamed Chesterville for Chester T. Casselman, its first telegraph operator, and the name stuck. The village incorporated in 1890 and joined the Township of North Dundas on January 1, 1998.
