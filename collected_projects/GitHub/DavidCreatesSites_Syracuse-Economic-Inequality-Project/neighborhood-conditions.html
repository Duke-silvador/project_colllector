01 / EVIDENCE & ANALYSIS

Poverty rate and child poverty

The paper reports 37,289 residents below poverty out of 129,544 whose poverty status was determined, or 28.8%. Among children under 18, the corresponding rate is 44.0%. [2]

Child poverty warrants attention because children depend on household resources for everyday needs. These measures identify the scale of hardship; they do not establish how a particular policy, school, or employer caused it. The draft uses several inconsistent national rates, so a national comparison is withheld until the same measure and period are aligned.

02 / EVIDENCE & ANALYSIS

Neighborhood poverty

The citywide average can conceal differences between small areas. The source’s interactive map lets readers inspect census tracts and change the demographic group. CNYVitals identifies tract 42 as having the largest count of residents below poverty in its 2024 display, at 2,042. [14]

A tract with more people below poverty does not necessarily have a higher poverty rate: larger tracts can have larger counts. Use the map’s label and legend carefully. Census tracts are statistical areas, and their boundaries do not always match familiar neighborhood names.

03 / EVIDENCE & ANALYSIS

Housing cost burden

The draft reports that 46.0% of renter households with a computed rent-to-income ratio spent at least 35% of income on gross rent. Adding the 30%–34.9% group brings the share spending at least 30% to 55.3%. [4]

A large housing bill can leave less room for food, transportation, and other expenses. This statistic measures a share of income, so it is different from the dollar amount of rent. Households without a computable ratio are not included in its denominator.

04 / EVIDENCE & ANALYSIS

Homeownership and occupancy

The occupied housing stock includes 24,889 owner-occupied and 34,927 renter-occupied units. Renters account for approximately 58.4% of occupied units. The paper’s separate 34,001 figure describes renter units paying cash rent. [4] [6]

Homeownership can create an opportunity to build equity, but a home’s value is not the same as an owner’s net wealth. Mortgage debt, maintenance costs, and changes in value matter. Renting also includes a wide range of household circumstances.

05 / EVIDENCE & ANALYSIS

Median house value and owner costs

The median value of owner-occupied housing is $138,400. The value distribution adds detail about the housing stock around that midpoint. [1] [4]

The paper also records monthly owner costs with and without a mortgage. Those costs should be considered separately from gross rent because the populations and expense definitions differ. The grouped chart preserves the original counts while showing how costs differ by mortgage status.

06 / EVIDENCE & ANALYSIS

Opportunity Atlas: household income at age 35

The paper records broad income ranges while exploring the Opportunity Atlas, including $26,000–$44,000 for parts of the Westside and $35,000–$57,000 around Syracuse University. These are exploratory notes, not verified averages for official neighborhood boundaries. [7]

The Atlas follows people according to where they grew up, even if they moved away. Because the paper does not record the parent-income filter, cohort, race, gender, dollar year, and exact tracts, the ranges are shown with a clear limitation instead of being assigned to a map. [20]

07 / EVIDENCE & ANALYSIS

Opportunity Atlas: individual income at age 35

Individual income and household income answer different questions. A household measure can include a spouse’s earnings, while an individual measure concerns the person’s own income. The paper records the ranges below for broad areas. [7]

A nearby university or commercial center may be relevant to a research hypothesis, but these observations cannot establish that local employers caused the adult outcomes. Reproducing the Atlas settings is necessary before making a stronger geographic comparison. [20]

08 / EVIDENCE & ANALYSIS

Opportunity Atlas: reaching the top 20%

The paper describes many areas as being around or below an 11% probability of reaching the national top fifth of household incomes. The underlying filters and tract values were not retained, so that observation is not presented as a verified map or citywide statistic. [7]

This remains a useful research question: does the probability of upward mobility differ across childhood neighborhoods under the same starting-income assumptions? The Atlas can explore that question when its filters are recorded consistently. [20]

09 / EVIDENCE & ANALYSIS

Health and lead exposure

The verified 2025 CNYVitals export reports elevated blood lead levels for 16.9% of children in tract 6, 19.7% in tract 14, and 11.2% in tract 39 under its indicator definition. The source profile reports a citywide figure of 9.05%. [15]

The source uses a threshold of 5 µg/dL or higher. These selected tracts are examples from the paper, not a full ranking. The downloaded table does not supply tested-child denominators. Geographic overlap with poverty can motivate further study but does not establish that poverty, race, or housing conditions caused a particular child’s exposure.

10 / EVIDENCE & ANALYSIS

Other health context

CNYVitals reports 5.7 age-adjusted mentally unhealthy days in the past 30 days for Onondaga County. This is a county measure, not an estimate for Syracuse city, and the draft does not record its data-collection year. [16]

The asthma section requires a correction: the source describes hospitalizations per 10,000 children ages 0–17 in 2016, while its headline uses an inconsistent percent sign. The draft’s statement that 10% of children were hospitalized is therefore not reproduced as a verified statistic. [17]

Read the evidence in context

ACS figures are estimates. The manuscript did not retain margins of error, so small differences should not be treated as statistically significant. “Source checked” means the displayed values were matched to a source during this review. “Paper transcription” identifies values retained from the manuscript that were not fully reproduced independently.
